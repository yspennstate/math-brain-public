    Let X = (X_1, ..., X_n) have INDEPENDENT coordinates taking values in arbitrary measurable spaces, and let P be the product law on the product space. For a subset A and a point x define the convex distance d_T(x, A) = sup { inf_{y in A} sum_{i=1}^n alpha_i 1{x_i != y_i} : alpha in R^n, alpha_i >= 0, |alpha|_2 <= 1 }. Then for every measurable A, E[ exp( d_T(X, A)^2 / 4 ) ] <= 1 / P(A), and consequently P(A) P( d_T(X, A) >= t ) <= exp( - t^2 / 4 ) for every t > 0. COROLLARY for convex Lipschitz functions: if f : [0,1]^n -> R is CONVEX and 1-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean norm and the coordinates of X are independent with values in [0,1], then for every t > 0, P( |f(X) - M| >= t ) <= 4 exp( - t^2 / 4 ) where M is a median of f(X). The hypotheses are exactly: independence, a product structure, and for the corollary convexity together with a bounded range. Convexity cannot be dropped - the conclusion is false for general Lipschitz functions of bounded independent variables at this strength, which is why the bounded-differences (McDiarmid) inequality with its sum-of-squared-ranges denominator is the general-purpose tool and Talagrand's is the sharp one for convex functionals. The centring is at a MEDIAN; converting to the mean costs an absolute constant. [Talagrand (1995), Publ. IHES 81; see Boucheron, Lugosi and Massart, Concentration Inequalities, Theorem 7.11 and Section 7.4] X has independent coordinates and P is a product measure for the corollary: f convex and 1-Lipschitz for the Euclidean norm, coordinates valued in [0,1] the corollary centres at a MEDIAN, not the mean

[4] Technique: Talagrand's Convex Distance Concentration Inequality  (id: claim_8bc6218023d8f024ef67f80f2057021b0e367a5c321ceeb28f5937f91f9987b7; claim; score 28.41)

    TECHNIQUE CARD (drafted from a spec by a helper model, every card read and seven corrected by the Fable agent on 2026-09-07; canonical statements only, no original claim). On a product probability space (Omega_1 x ... x Omega_n, mu), define the convex distance d_T(x,A) = sup_{alpha>=0, |alpha|=1} inf_{y in A} sum_i alpha_i 1_{x_i != y_i}, a weighted Hamming-type distance from x to a set A, maximized over convex combinations of nonnegative unit-norm weight vectors. Talagrand's inequality states integral exp(d_T(x,A)^2/4) dmu(x) <= 1/mu(A), which implies mu(A) mu({x : d_T(x,A) >= t}) <= exp(-t^2/4), a strong concentration of any set A around itself in convex distance. Applied to a function f that is certifiable, meaning any difference f(x) - f(y) can be witnessed by a bounded number of coordinates with bounded weight, this yields subgaussian concentration of f around its median without requiring f to be linear or even close to it, making the method far more flexible than Azuma-Hoeffding for combinatorial optimization functionals such as the longest increasing subsequence, bin-packing cost, or chromatic number. It does not directly give concentration around the mean, only the median, so a separate median-to-mean gap argument is needed, and it requires exhibiting an explicit certificate structure for f, which can be technically demanding to construct. Typical failure: Applying Talagrand concentration by treating f as ordinary Hamming-Lipschitz (uniform weight 1 per coordinate change) when the natural certificate for f actually requires nonuniform or superlinear weighting, since changing one coordinate can move f by an amount depending on many other coordinates; using the wrong or absent certificate yields either the wrong concentration scale or, more commonly, no valid proof at all, since the convex-distance formulation is easy to state and easy to misapply. Canonical reference: Talagrand, 1995, Publications Mathematiques de l'IHES; Steele, Probability Theory and Combinatorial Optimization, 1997, SIAM. a product probability measure on independent, not necessarily identically distributed coordinates the function or event of interest admits a certifiable Lipschitz-type structure with bounded, possibly nonuniform, weights measurability of the relevant sets typically only median concentration is obtained directly; mean concentration needs a separate argument
