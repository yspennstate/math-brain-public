    Let (Z_n) be a Galton-Watson branching process with Z_0 = 1 and offspring distribution (p_k)_{k >= 0} having generating function f(s) = sum_k p_k s^k and mean m = sum_k k p_k, assumed finite. Let q = P( Z_n = 0 for some n ) be the extinction probability. Then q is the SMALLEST root in [0,1] of the equation f(s) = s, and: if m < 1 (subcritical) then q = 1; if m = 1 (critical) then q = 1 UNLESS p_1 = 1, in which case the process is deterministic and never dies; if m > 1 (supercritical) then q < 1. The critical case is the one that gets misquoted: a critical branching process dies out almost surely even though E[Z_n] = 1 for every n, so the population is not uniformly integrable and the mean survives while the process does not. Excluding the degenerate p_1 = 1 is necessary. A second standard trap: q < 1 does not mean the process survives with probability 1 - it survives with probability exactly 1 - q, and conditioned on survival Z_n grows geometrically at rate m. [standard; e.g. Athreya and Ney, Branching Processes, Chapter I, or Durrett, Probability: Theory and Examples, 5th ed., Section 4.3.3] offspring distribution with finite mean m Z_0 = 1 (otherwise q is raised to the power of the initial population) the critical conclusion excludes the degenerate case p_1 = 1

Given a Markov chain with kernel P and a nonnegative P-harmonic function h, the Doob h-transform defines a new Markov chain with kernel P^h(x,y) = P(x,y) h(y)/h(x), representing the law of the original chain conditioned, in an appropriate limiting sense such as conditioning to survive forever or on an asymptotic event, to respect the weighting given by h. Applied to a critical or subcritical Galton-Watson branching process conditioned to survive forever, or, for critical trees, conditioned to have total size n with n -> infinity, the Kesten tree construction realizes the limiting law as an explicit infinite random tree: a single infinite spine, with each spine vertex additionally carrying an independent number of extra children distributed according to the size-biased-minus-one offspring law, each extra child then supporting an ordinary, unconditioned Galton-Watson subtree. This gives the exact local weak limit of large conditioned critical or subcritical branching trees and underlies scaling-limit results, such as convergence to the continuum random tree, that first pass through this explicit infinite-tree description. It does not directly give scaling limits of the whole tree's metric structure, which requires an additional contour or height-function argument, and the exact form of the spine's offspring law is specific to the conditioning event used, since conditioning on survival forever and conditioning on total size n give related but generally distinct limiting infinite trees. Typical failure: Confusing the different conditioning regimes for the conditioned Galton-Watson tree, such as conditioning on survival to infinity versus conditioning on total progeny exactly n as n -> infinity versus conditioning on height n, as if they all give the identical Kesten tree limit; they give related but generally distinct limiting infinite trees, differing in the spine's offspring bias and finer distributional details, and using one variant's known properties for a computation set up under a different conditioning is a frequent, easy-to-miss error.
