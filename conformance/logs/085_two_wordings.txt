[6] Pitfall: The Hopf Lemma Needs an Interior Sphere Condition  (id: claim_f3f681977eb9ae940809b4e58a616eac3c6360d8024519e4cff1f440e2681399; claim; score 18.68)

    Load-bearing hypothesis: an interior ball touching the boundary at the point x0 in question (and, for the strong maximum principle, the fact that such a ball can always be produced inside the open set where the solution is strictly below its maximum). Without it the conclusion 'the inward derivative at a boundary minimum is strictly positive' is false. Counterexample at a convex corner: in the plane let Omega be the sector {0 < theta < beta, 0 < r < 1} with 0 < beta < pi and u(r, theta) = r^{pi/beta} sin(pi theta/beta). Then u is harmonic in Omega, continuous on the closure, u > 0 inside and u = 0 on both straight edges, so 0 is a strict boundary minimum of u; but pi/beta > 1, so for every direction e pointing into Omega the difference quotient (u(te) - u(0))/t = O(t^{pi/beta - 1}) tends to 0: there is no Hopf gain at the vertex. No interior ball touches the vertex, exactly as the hypothesis requires. How far the hypothesis can be relaxed: the interior sphere condition may be replaced by an interior C^{1,Dini} (or interior paraboloid/cone-with-Dini) condition, but not by mere C^1 regularity of the boundary; there are C^1 domains where a positive harmonic function vanishing on the boundary has vanishing gradient at a boundary point - for instance the image of the upper half-plane near 0 under the conformal map z -> z log(1/z), whose boundary is C^1 but not Dini smooth at 0, and for which the inverse map has derivative tending to 0. Practical rule for an auditor: a proof that invokes Hopf's lemma (to get strict inequality, strict monotonicity, a sign of the normal derivative, simplicity of the first eigenvalue, or the strong maximum principle at the boundary) must say why the domain has the interior ball property at the relevant point; convex corners and cusps of the domain are where this silently breaks.
