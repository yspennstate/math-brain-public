For f: {-1,1}^n -> R with Fourier-Walsh expansion f = sum_S hat{f}(S) chi_S and the noise operator T_rho, which independently resamples each coordinate to a fresh uniform sign with probability 1-rho (equivalently, flips each coordinate with probability (1-rho)/2, so that each coordinate keeps correlation rho with the input), the Bonami-Beckner inequality states ||T_rho f||_q <= ||f||_p whenever 1 <= p <= q and 0 <= rho <= sqrt((p-1)/(q-1)). Equivalently, T_rho f = sum_S rho^{|S|} hat{f}(S) chi_S, and the inequality forces the Fourier weight of f on high-degree levels to decay geometrically once rho<1, giving sum_S rho^{2|S|} hat{f}(S)^2 <= (E[|f|^p])^{2/p} for suitable p. This is the central tool converting L^2 Fourier information into higher-moment (typically L^4) control, and it underlies the Kahn-Kalai-Linial theorem on the existence of an influential coordinate, the Benjamini-Kalai-Schramm noise-sensitivity theorems, and Friedgut's junta theorem for low-influence functions. It does not directly give influence bounds without further combination with such machinery, and the sharp constant and range of rho given here hold for the uniform measure on {-1,1}^n; biased-p product measures or non-product (dependent) measures require different, generally weaker, p-biased or dependency-adjusted hypercontractive inequalities. Typical failure: Applying the sharp uniform-measure {-1,1}^n hypercontractive constant to a biased-p product measure or to a dependent measure such as the random-cluster or Ising Gibbs measure without switching to the correct p-biased or dependency-adjusted hypercontractivity inequality, whose constants generally degrade as p approaches 0 or 1; this silently invalidates quantitative noise-sensitivity conclusions claimed for critical percolation at p != 1/2 or for genuinely non-product models.
