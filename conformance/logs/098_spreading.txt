    Let mu_sc be the semicircle law with density (1/(2 pi)) sqrt(4 - x^2) on [-2, 2]. Its Stieltjes transform m(z) = integral (x - z)^{-1} mu_sc(dx) satisfies the self-consistent equation m(z)^2 + z m(z) + 1 = 0 for every z in C^+, equivalently m(z) = -1/( z + m(z) ), and is the UNIQUE solution of that equation with Im m(z) > 0 for z in C^+; explicitly m(z) = ( -z + sqrt(z^2 - 4) )/2 with the branch of the square root chosen so that m(z) is asymptotic to -1/z as |z| tends to infinity. This equation is the fixed point that resolvent proofs of Wigner's theorem converge to: the Schur complement identity applied to the resolvent of a Wigner matrix gives (1/n) tr (H - z)^{-1} approximately equal to -1/(z + (1/n) tr (H - z)^{-1}) up to errors that vanish as n grows, and stability of the fixed point then forces the empirical Stieltjes transform to converge to m. For the Marchenko-Pastur law the analogous equation carries the aspect ratio and reads m(z) = 1/( 1 - y - y z m(z) - z ), a different fixed point that must not be substituted for this one.     For the semicircle density (1/(2 pi)) sqrt(4 - x^2) on [-2, 2] (mean 0, variance 1), all odd moments vanish and the 2k-th moment equals the Catalan number C_k = (1/(k+1)) binom(2k, k): 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, ... . A semicircle of radius R has 2k-th moment C_k (R/2)^{2k}. In the moment method for a Wigner matrix W = X / sqrt n, where X is real symmetric with independent entries above the diagonal, mean 0 and variance 1, the normalized traces satisfy E tr(W^{2k}) / n -> C_k: expanding the trace as a sum over closed walks of length 2k on the index set, only walks whose edges are each traversed exactly twice and whose underlying graph is a tree survive as n grows, and such walks are counted by C_k; the Catalan numbers determine the semicircle law uniquely because it has compact support. Wigner's theorem then follows from convergence of moments plus a variance bound (or a concentration bound on the trace) giving weak convergence of the empirical spectral distribution in probability. entries independent up to symmetry with mean 0 and variance 1 before scaling by 1/sqrt n moments bounded uniformly in n (finite fourth moment suffices for the semicircle limit of the empirical spectral distribution; the diagonal entries may have a different variance) the moment method identifies the limit because the semicircle law is determined by its moments (compact support)

    The GAUSSIAN ORTHOGONAL ENSEMBLE GOE_n is the law of a real symmetric n by n matrix H whose entries are independent up to symmetry with H_{ii} distributed as N(0, 2/n) and H_{ij} distributed as N(0, 1/n) for i < j. Its density with respect to Lebesgue measure on the space of real symmetric matrices is proportional to exp( - (n/4) trace(H^2) ), and the law is invariant under H -> O H O^T for every orthogonal O. The GAUSSIAN UNITARY ENSEMBLE GUE_n is the law of a Hermitian H with H_{ii} distributed as N(0, 1/n) real and, for i < j, H_{ij} = x + i y with x and y independent N(0, 1/(2n)), so that E|H_{ij}|^2 = 1/n; its density is proportional to exp( - (n/2) trace(H^2) ) and it is invariant under H -> U H U^* for every unitary U. With THESE normalisations the empirical spectral distribution of either ensemble converges to the semicircle law on [-2, 2], the largest eigenvalue converges to 2, and the edge fluctuations are of order n^{-2/3} with Tracy-Widom limits (beta = 1 for GOE, beta = 2 for GUE). The DIAGONAL VARIANCE DIFFERS BETWEEN THE TWO ENSEMBLES - 2/n for GOE, 1/n for GUE - and that is what makes both densities come out as the invariant exponential of the trace; getting it wrong changes the density but not the limiting spectral law. Normalisation conventions vary widely across the literature: some authors use unnormalised entries of variance 1 and divide by sqrt(n) afterwards, some put the semicircle on [-1,1] or [-2 sqrt(2), 2 sqrt(2)], and some use exp(-trace(H^2)/2) with n absorbed elsewhere. Any imported constant must be checked against the convention it was derived under. [standard; e.g. Anderson, Guionnet and Zeitouni, An Introduction to Random Matrices, Sections 2.5 and 2.6]
