\begin{abstract}
Among symmetric subsets of $\R^n$ of prescribed Gaussian measure, which one maximizes the probability that two
$\rho$-correlated standard Gaussian vectors both lie in the set? A centred ball or its complement was suggested, and
Heilman showed that this fails in the plane to second order at $\rho=0$. We show that it fails for every
$\rho\in(0,1)$ and every $n\ge2$: at measure close to $1$ the slab $\{|x_1|\le t\}$ is strictly more noise stable than
every cylinder $B^k\times\R^{n-k}$ with $k\ge2$ and every cylinder complement, and at measure close to $0$ the
complement of the slab wins. Computations in dimensions one, two and six are consistent with cylinders and their
complements being the optimal sets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $X$ and $Y$ be standard Gaussian vectors in $\R^n$ such that $(X,Y)$ is a centred Gaussian vector in $\R^{2n}$
with $\E[X_iY_j]=\rho\,\delta_{ij}$, and for a measurable set
$A\subseteq\R^n$ let
\[
\Stab(A)=\Prob(X\in A,\ Y\in A)
\]
be its noise stability. Borell's theorem~\cite{Borell} says that among all sets of fixed Gaussian measure a half
space has the largest noise stability when $0<\rho<1$. Its proofs by heat flow and by stochastic calculus all use the
fact that translates of half spaces are again half spaces, and this is lost when one asks the same question for
symmetric sets, $A=-A$. The symmetric version arose in the lower bound of Chakrabarti and Regev~\cite{CR} for the
Gap-Hamming-Distance problem, and O'Donnell listed it among the open problems in the analysis of Boolean
functions~\cite{OD}: for $0<a,b<1$, minimize $\Prob(X\in A,\ Y\in B)$ over symmetric $A,B$ with $\gamma_n(A)=a$ and
$\gamma_n(B)=b$, where $\gamma_n$ is the standard Gaussian measure. O'Donnell first imposes symmetry on $A$ only and
remarks that requiring it of both sets gives an equivalent problem. The suggested answer, also implicit in
Barthe's work on unconditional sets~\cite{Barthe}, is that $A$ is a centred ball and $B$ the complement of a centred
ball, or the other way round.

Heilman~\cite{H1} gave the first results. In dimension one he proved that the centred interval or its complement is
optimal when $|\rho|$ is below an explicit, very small bound~\cite[Theorem~1.5]{H1}. In dimension two he showed that
the conjecture is false: differentiating twice in $\rho$ at $\rho=0$ reduces the question to a functional of the
second-degree Hermite coefficients, and for the measure $0.943865$ a slab and an ellipse do better than the ball and
its complement. He noted that a slab doing best there agrees with the S-inequality of Latała and
Oleszkiewicz~\cite{LO}, by which among symmetric convex subsets of the plane with the measure of a slab, the slab has
the smallest measure after any dilation by a factor at least $1$. A second-variation computation~\cite[Theorem~1.7]{H1} shows that a ball or a ball complement of large
radius is not even a local maximizer of that functional. He suggested that the optimal sets might be solid cylinders or their
complements, whose boundaries are dilations of $S^k\times\R^{n-k-1}$. The limit $\rho\to1$ concerns Gaussian surface
area. Heilman~\cite[Theorem~1.11]{H2} showed that a set minimizing Gaussian surface area among symmetric sets of its
measure is a cylinder $B^k\times\R^{n-k}$ or the complement of one if the set or its complement is convex and a
curvature-integral inequality holds, and~\cite[Theorem~2.4]{H3} gives the same conclusion under the assumption that
$\Omega\times\R$ is minimizing, with one exceptional case of signs. Barchiesi and Julin~\cite{BJ} proved that the
slab is the symmetric minimizer of Gaussian perimeter when the measure is close to $1$, and Milman~\cite{Milman}
classified the planar symmetric minimizers as discs, strips and their complements. Barthe~\cite{Barthe} had shown
that among unconditional sets slabs minimize a modified Gaussian perimeter. Fusco and La Manna~\cite{FLM} studied the
Gaussian integral of the mean curvature under a Gaussian volume constraint, and showed that in the plane, among convex
sets satisfying regularity and containment assumptions, the centred disc maximizes it for certain values of the
measure.

Heilman's counterexamples concern the second derivative at $\rho=0$, so they show that the conjecture fails for all
sufficiently small $\rho>0$ at those measures. Here we show that it fails at every correlation.

For $1\le k\le n$ and $s>0$ write
\[
C_k(s)=\{x\in\R^n:\ x_1^2+\dots+x_k^2\le s^2\},
\]
so that $C_1(s)$ is the slab $\{|x_1|\le s\}$ and $C_n(s)$ is the centred ball of radius $s$. All of these sets and
their complements are symmetric.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $n\ge2$ and $0<\rho<1$. There is $a_0=a_0(n,\rho)<1$ such that for every $a\in(a_0,1)$ the slab of Gaussian
measure $a$ has strictly larger noise stability than every $C_k(s)$ with $2\le k\le n$ of measure $a$ and than every
complement $C_k(s)^c$ with $1\le k\le n$ of measure $a$. More precisely, let $\varepsilon=1-a$, let $t$ and $r$ be
defined by $\gamma_n(C_1(t))=\gamma_n(C_k(r))=a$, and write $J(A)=\Prob(X\notin A,\ Y\notin A)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rates}
\frac{J(C_1(t))}{J(C_k(r))}\ \ge\ c\,t^{(k-1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\quad(2\le k\le n),\qquad
J(C_k(s)^c)\le(1-\rho^2)^{-k/2}\varepsilon^2,
\end{equation}
for $\varepsilon$ small, with $c=c(k,\rho)>0$, while $J(C_1(t))\ge c'\,t^{-2\rho/(1+\rho)}\varepsilon^{2/(1+\rho)}$.
\end{theorem}

Since $\Stab(A^c)=1-2\gamma_n(A)+\Stab(A)$, a set of measure $a$ is optimal exactly when its complement is optimal
among sets of measure $1-a$. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} therefore also says that the complement of the slab beats every
other cylinder and cylinder complement when the measure is close to $0$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:false}
For every $n\ge2$ and every $\rho\in(0,1)$ there are $0<a_1<a_0<1$, depending on $n$ and $\rho$, such that for
$a\in(0,a_1)\cup(a_0,1)$ neither the centred ball of measure $a$ nor the complement of a centred ball of measure $a$
maximizes $\Stab$ among symmetric sets of measure $a$. In O'Donnell's formulation with $b=1-a$, the slab of measure $a$
paired with its complement when $a>a_0$, and the complement of a slab of measure $a$ paired with the slab when
$a<a_1$, give a smaller value of $\Prob(X\in A,\ Y\in B)$ than the pairs formed by a ball and a ball complement.
\end{corollary}

The second statement follows from the first because, for $B=A^c$, $\Prob(X\in A,\ Y\in B)=a-\Stab(A)$, and the
conjectured pairs with $b=1-a$ are exactly $(A,A^c)$ with $A$ a centred ball or the complement of one.

The mechanism is simple. For a set of measure close to $1$, the noise stability is $1-2\varepsilon+J(A)$, so only the
probability $J(A)$ that both points leave $A$ matters. Write $S=X+Y$ and $D=X-Y$, which are independent. Both points
leave the ball of radius $r$ in $\R^k$ mostly when $|S|$ is slightly larger than $2r$ and the component of $D$ along $S$
is of order $1/r$; both points leave the slab of half-width $t$ when $S_1>2t+|D_1|$, and mostly when $|D_1|$ is of order
$1/t$. The two probabilities decay like $e^{-r^2/(1+\rho)}$ and $e^{-t^2/(1+\rho)}$ up to powers of $r$ and $t$, and
matching the measures gives $r^2=t^2+(2k-2)\log t+O(1)$, with $O(1)$ bounded as $t\to\infty$. The ball pays more in the
factor $e^{-(r^2-t^2)/(1+\rho)}$ than it gains from its larger radius.

In Section~\ref{sec:numerics} we report computations beyond the theorem. Baker~\cite[Section~3.4]{Baker} compared the
four planar cylinders (ball, slab and their complements) at $\rho=0.9$ and found the slab best near measure $1$ and its
complement best near measure $0$. We extend that comparison to $13$ correlations. In the plane the best of the four
cylinders is never beaten by the sets returned by a search over symmetric sets; in dimension one the returned sets are
the interval or its complement for all the correlations tested; and in $\R^n$ every $C_k$ or its complement is the
best of the cylinders compared for some measure. This is consistent with Heilman's suggestion that the optimal
symmetric sets are cylinders.

\section{Gaussian tails and the sum and difference}\label{sec:lemmas}

Write $\varphi$ and $\bar\Phi$ for the standard normal density and upper tail. Throughout, $c$ and $C$ denote positive
constants that depend only on the dimension and on $\rho$; their values may change from line to line. We use Gordon's inequalities~\cite{Gordon}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mills}
\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,\varphi(x)\le\bar\Phi(x)\le\frac{\varphi(x)}{x}\qquad(x>0).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:chi}
Let $Q_m$ have the $\chi^2$ distribution with $m\ge1$ degrees of freedom, and let $\Gamma$ be Euler's gamma function.
Then for $q>0$
\[
\Prob(Q_m>q)\le C_m(1+q)^{(m/2-1)_+}e^{-q/2},
\]
and if $m\ge2$,
\[
\Prob(Q_m>q)\ge\frac{(q/2)^{m/2-1}e^{-q/2}}{\Gamma(m/2)}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\Prob(Q_m>q)=\Gamma(m/2,q/2)/\Gamma(m/2)$, where the upper incomplete gamma function is $\Gamma(\sigma,x)=e^{-x}\int_0^\infty(x+y)^{\sigma-1}e^{-y}\,dy$. If
$\sigma\ge1$, then $x^{\sigma-1}\le(x+y)^{\sigma-1}\le2^{\sigma-1}(x^{\sigma-1}+y^{\sigma-1})$, which gives both bounds.
If $\sigma=1/2$, then $(x+y)^{-1/2}\le y^{-1/2}$ and $\Gamma(1/2,x)\le e^{-x}\Gamma(1/2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:decomp}
Let $S=X+Y$ and $D=X-Y$. Then $S$ and $D$ are independent, $S\sim N(0,2(1+\rho)I_n)$ and $D\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_n)$ with
$\sigma^2=2(1-\rho)$. Let $u=S/|S|$, $w=\langle D,u\rangle$ and $v=D-wu$. Then $|S|$, $w$ and $|v|$ are independent,
$|S|^2/(2(1+\rho))$ is $\chi^2_n$, $w\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $|v|^2/\sigma^2$ is $\chi^2_{n-1}$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:min}
4\min(|X|,|Y|)^2=(|S|-|w|)^2+|v|^2.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\E[SD^{\mathsf T}]=\E[XX^{\mathsf T}]-\E[YY^{\mathsf T}]=0$, so the jointly Gaussian vectors $S$ and $D$ are
independent, with the stated covariances. Since $D$ is isotropic and independent of $S$, conditionally on $S$ the
coordinates of $D$ in an orthonormal basis whose first vector is $u$ are independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$; this gives the
laws and the independence. Finally $2X=S+D$ and $2Y=S-D$, so $4|X|^2$ and $4|Y|^2$ equal $|S|^2\pm2|S|w+w^2+|v|^2$,
whose minimum is $(|S|-|w|)^2+|v|^2$.
\end{proof}

\section{The slab and the cylinders}\label{sec:proof}

The noise stability of $C_k(s)$ in $\R^n$ is that of the ball of radius $s$ in $\R^k$, since the remaining coordinates
play no role; the same holds for complements. So in the next two lemmas we work in $\R^k$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:slab}
For $k=1$ and $t\ge1$,
\[
J(C_1(t))=\Prob(|X_1|>t,\ |Y_1|>t)\ge c_1\,t^{-2}e^{-t^2/(1+\rho)},\qquad
c_1=\frac{e^{-1/(4(1-\rho))-5/(4(1+\rho))}}{3\pi\sqrt{1-\rho}}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
With $S_1=X_1+Y_1$ and $D_1=X_1-Y_1$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:decomp}, $X_1>t$ and $Y_1>t$ if and only if
$S_1>2t+|D_1|$. Hence
\[
\Prob(X_1>t,\ Y_1>t)\ge\Prob(|D_1|\le1/t)\,\Prob(S_1>2t+1/t).
\]
The density of $D_1$ is at least $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-1/(2\sigma^2t^2)}$ on $[-1/t,1/t]$, so the first factor is at
least $2t^{-1}(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-1/(4(1-\rho))}$. For the second, put $x=(2t+1/t)/\sqrt{2(1+\rho)}$. Then
$1\le x\le3t/\sqrt2$ and $x^2/2=(4t^2+4+t^{-2})/(4(1+\rho))\le t^2/(1+\rho)+5/(4(1+\rho))$, so by~\eqref{eq:mills}
$\bar\Phi(x)\ge\varphi(x)/(2x)\ge\frac{\sqrt2}{6t\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-5/(4(1+\rho))}e^{-t^2/(1+\rho)}$. The events
$\{X_1>t,Y_1>t\}$ and $\{X_1<-t,Y_1<-t\}$ are disjoint and have the same probability, which gives the factor $2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ball}
For $k\ge2$ there is $c_2=c_2(k,\rho)$ such that for $r\ge1$
\[
J(C_k(r))=\Prob(|X|>r,\ |Y|>r)\le c_2\,r^{k-3}e^{-r^2/(1+\rho)},
\]
where $X,Y$ are $\rho$-correlated standard Gaussian vectors in $\R^k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:min} the event is $E=\{(|S|-|w|)^2+|v|^2>4r^2\}$. Put $g=4/(1+\rho)$, so that $0<4-g=4\rho/(1+\rho)$.
We split $E$ into three parts.

(i) $|S|\le|w|$. Then $(|w|-|S|)^2\le w^2$, so $E$ forces $|D|^2=w^2+|v|^2>4r^2$. Since $|D|^2/\sigma^2$ is
$\chi^2_k$, Lemma~\ref{lem:chi} bounds this probability by $C(1+r^2)^{k/2-1}e^{-r^2/(1-\rho)}$.

(ii) $|v|^2>gr^2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:chi} for $\chi^2_{k-1}$ this has probability at most
$C(1+r^2)^{k}e^{-gr^2/(2\sigma^2)}=C(1+r^2)^ke^{-r^2/(1-\rho^2)}$.

(iii) $|S|>|w|$ and $|v|^2\le gr^2$. Then $E$ forces $|S|>|w|+\sqrt{4r^2-|v|^2}$, hence
\[
|S|^2>s^2:=4r^2-|v|^2+2\sqrt{4-g}\,r|w|,
\]
because $4r^2-|v|^2\ge(4-g)r^2$. Conditionally on $(w,v)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:chi} gives
$\Prob(|S|^2>s^2)\le C(1+s^2)^{k/2-1}e^{-s^2/(4(1+\rho))}$. On this part $s^2\le4r^2(1+|w|)$ for $r\ge1$, so
$(1+s^2)^{k/2-1}\le(8r^2)^{k/2-1}(1+|w|)^{k/2-1}$, and therefore the probability of part (iii) is at most
\[
C\,r^{k-2}e^{-r^2/(1+\rho)}\ \E\bigl[e^{|v|^2/(4(1+\rho))}\bigr]\ \E\bigl[(1+|w|)^{k/2-1}e^{-\kappa r|w|}\bigr],
\qquad\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{4-g}}{2(1+\rho)}=\frac{\sqrt\rho}{(1+\rho)^{3/2}},
\]
using the independence of $|v|$ and $w$. The first expectation equals $(1-(1-\rho)/(1+\rho))^{-(k-1)/2}=
((1+\rho)/(2\rho))^{(k-1)/2}$, which is finite because $\rho>0$. The second is at most
$2(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}\int_0^\infty(1+y)^{k/2-1}e^{-\kappa ry}\,dy\le C/r$ for $r\ge1$. So part (iii) is at most
$C\,r^{k-3}e^{-r^2/(1+\rho)}$.

Parts (i) and (ii) have exponential rates $1/(1-\rho)$ and $1/(1-\rho^2)$, both larger than $1/(1+\rho)$, so for
$r\ge1$ they are bounded by a constant times $r^{k-3}e^{-r^2/(1+\rho)}$ as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:small}
For $k\ge1$ and $s>0$, $\Stab(B^k(s))\le(1-\rho^2)^{-k/2}\gamma_k(B^k(s))^2$, where $B^k(s)$ is the centred ball of
radius $s$ in $\R^k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $Y=\rho X+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z$ with $Z$ standard Gaussian and independent of $X$. Conditionally on $X=x$, $Y$ is a
centred Gaussian vector shifted by $\rho x$, and by Anderson's theorem~\cite{Anderson} the probability that it lies in
the symmetric convex set $B^k(s)$ is at most $\Prob(\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,|Z|\le s)=\gamma_k(B^k(\lambda s))$ with
$\lambda=(1-\rho^2)^{-1/2}\ge1$. Since $\varphi_k(\lambda y)\le\varphi_k(y)$,
$\gamma_k(B^k(\lambda s))=\lambda^k\int_{B^k(s)}\varphi_k(\lambda y)\,dy\le\lambda^k\gamma_k(B^k(s))$. Integrating over
$x\in B^k(s)$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Let $\varepsilon=1-a$ be small, so that $2\bar\Phi(t)=\varepsilon$ and $\Prob(Q_k>r^2)=\varepsilon$. Since
$\Prob(Q_k>r^2)\ge\Prob(X_1^2>r^2)=2\bar\Phi(r)$, we have $r\ge t$. By~\eqref{eq:mills},
$\varepsilon\le\sqrt{2/\pi}\,e^{-t^2/2}/t$, that is $e^{-t^2/2}\ge\sqrt{\pi/2}\,t\varepsilon$, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:chi}, $e^{-r^2/2}\le\Gamma(k/2)(r^2/2)^{1-k/2}\varepsilon$. We take $\varepsilon$ so small that $t\ge1$,
and then $r\ge1$ as well.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:slab},
\[
J(C_1(t))\ge c_1t^{-2}\bigl(e^{-t^2/2}\bigr)^{2/(1+\rho)}\ge c\,t^{-2}(t\varepsilon)^{2/(1+\rho)}
=c\,t^{-2\rho/(1+\rho)}\varepsilon^{2/(1+\rho)},
\]
which is the last claim of the theorem, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:ball},
\[
J(C_k(r))\le c_2r^{k-3}\bigl(e^{-r^2/2}\bigr)^{2/(1+\rho)}\le C\,r^{k-3-2(k-2)/(1+\rho)}\varepsilon^{2/(1+\rho)}.
\]
The exponent of $r$ is
$k-3-2(k-2)/(1+\rho)=-2\rho/(1+\rho)-(k-1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)<0$, so $r\ge t$ gives $r^{k-3-2(k-2)/(1+\rho)}\le
t^{k-3-2(k-2)/(1+\rho)}$, and the ratio of the two bounds is at least $c\,t^{\beta}$ with
\[
\beta=-\frac{2\rho}{1+\rho}-(k-3)+\frac{2(k-2)}{1+\rho}=\frac{(k-1)(1-\rho)}{1+\rho}>0.
\]
This is the first bound in~\eqref{eq:rates}. The complement $C_k(s)^c$ of measure $a$ has $J(C_k(s)^c)=\Stab(C_k(s))$ with
$\gamma_n(C_k(s))=\varepsilon$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:small} gives the second bound. Since $\varepsilon\le e^{-t^2/2}$ for
$t\ge1$,
\[
\frac{\varepsilon^2}{J(C_1(t))}\le C\,t^{2\rho/(1+\rho)}\varepsilon^{2\rho/(1+\rho)}\le C\,t^{2\rho/(1+\rho)}
e^{-\rho t^2/(1+\rho)}\to0 .
\]
Finally, for sets of measure $a$ we have $\Stab(A)=1-2\varepsilon+J(A)$, so differences of noise stability are
differences of $J$. Taking $a_0$ larger than the thresholds for the finitely many cylinders and complements proves the
theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:sharp}
Laplace's method suggests that the bound $t^{(k-1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}$ gives the true order of $J(C_1(t))/J(C_k(r))$. In
the plane it predicts
\[
\frac{J(C_1(t))}{J(C_2(r))}\sim\frac{2r^2}{t^2}\,\frac{e^{(r^2-t^2)/(1+\rho)}}{r\sqrt{2\pi/\rho}}
\]
where $\sim$ means that the ratio of the two sides tends to $1$ (the factor $r\sqrt{2\pi/\rho}$ comes from the circle on which the ball's integrand is largest), and the quadrature of
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} agrees with it to $12$, $3$ and $1$ per cent at $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$, $10^{-16}$ and
$10^{-64}$ for $\rho=0.1$, with the same convergence at $\rho=0.5$ and $0.9$. The intermediate bounds in the
proofs of Lemmas~\ref{lem:slab} and~\ref{lem:ball}, evaluated by quadrature rather than through their constants, are
not far from the truth: for $n=2,3$, $\rho\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}$ and $10^{-32}\le\varepsilon\le10^{-2}$, the product
bound $2\Prob(|D_1|\le1/t)\Prob(S_1>2t+1/t)$ is between $0.33$ and $0.55$ times $J(C_1(t))$, and the sum of the three
parts of the ball bound is between $1.16$ and $3.2$ times $J(C_n(r))$. The constant of Lemma~\ref{lem:slab} is cruder:
on the same range $c_1t^{-2}e^{-t^2/(1+\rho)}$ is between $0.006$ and $0.08$ times $J(C_1(t))$. Finally $J(C_1(t))/J(C_n(r))$ divided by $t^{(n-1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}$ changes by
at most $6$ per cent between $\varepsilon=10^{-16}$ and $10^{-32}$. The exponent tends to $0$ as $\rho\to1$,
in line with the limit of surface area: at equal large measure the Gaussian perimeters of the slab and of the ball
in $\R^2$ are $2\varphi(t)$, which is asymptotic to $t\varepsilon$, and $re^{-r^2/2}=r\varepsilon$, so their ratio tends to $1$.
\end{remark}

\section{Numerical evidence}\label{sec:numerics}

a one-dimensional integral: conditionally on $|X|=R$, the variable $|Y|^2/(1-\rho^2)$ has the noncentral $\chi^2_k$
law with noncentrality $\rho^2R^2/(1-\rho^2)$.

\subsection*{The four cylinders in the plane}

\subsection*{Search over symmetric sets in the plane}
We cut $[-6.5,6.5]^2$ into a $256\times256$ grid and relax the problem: a symmetric set becomes a symmetric function
$f$ on the cells with values in $[0,1]$, and we maximize $\sum f\,(KfK)$, where $K$ is the matrix of the
one-dimensional transition kernel between cells. The Frank--Wolfe step takes the cells with the largest values of
$(KfK)/w$, with $w$ the cell mass, up to total mass $a$, the last cell fractionally, and then symmetrizes. For an
exactly positive semidefinite kernel the quadratic form is convex, and a full step from a feasible symmetric iterate
never decreases it. For $\rho\in\{0.1,0.3,
0.5,0.7,0.9\}$ and eleven measures between $0.05$ and $0.95$ we ran $128$ random symmetric starts each ($7{,}040$ starts
in all, from random Fourier fields, angular fields, unions of ellipses and smoothed noise) and polished the best returned
sets in double precision; in $28$ of the $55$ cases the best run stopped at the cap of $300$ iterations, so these are
the best sets returned, not certified local maxima. In none of the $55$ cases does a returned set exceed the exact
value of the best of the four cylinders: the difference lies between $-1.6\times10^{-4}$ and $-8.9\times10^{-8}$. Frank--Wolfe started from a
ball complement often moves to a slab complement, in agreement with Heilman's second-variation result.

At the measure where slab and ball tie (for $\rho=0.5$ this is $a=0.798026$, where both have noise stability
$0.6632936549$), we compared shapes between the two on a $400\times400$ grid with a smoothed boundary, so that every
shape has measure exactly $a$: ellipses $x_1^2/\sigma^2+\sigma^2x_2^2\le c^2$ with $0.2\le\sigma\le0.95$, slabs cut by a
disc, and stadiums (points within distance $t$ of a segment). All fall below the better cylinder computed on the same
grid, by $3\times10^{-6}$ to $8.6\times10^{-4}$ relative, and approach the slab as the shapes approach it, though not
monotonically: at $\rho=0.5$ the stadium values first decrease and then rise, and the ellipses dip as well. The same holds at
$\rho=0.2$ and $\rho=0.8$, except for the thinnest ellipse at $\rho=0.8$, which exceeds the gridded slab by
$1.3\times10^{-7}$, two hundred times less than the grid error of the slab itself.

\subsection*{Dimension one}
Heilman's theorem~\cite[Theorem~1.5]{H1} covers $|\rho|$ below an explicit bound that is at most $e^{-40}/1000$. We
cut $[0,7]$ into $1{,}400$ cells and ran the same Frank--Wolfe iteration from $300$ random symmetric starts with $2$
to $40$ blocks, for $11$ values of $\rho$ from $0.05$ to $0.95$ and $13$ measures from $0.02$ to $0.98$. In all $143$
cases the best returned set is the interval $[-t,t]$ or its complement, whichever has the larger noise stability, to
ten digits, and the discretized values agree with the exact ones to $3.7\times10^{-6}$.

\subsection*{Cylinders in higher dimension}
In $\R^n$ we compared all $C_k$ and $C_k^c$, $1\le k\le n$, for $n=2,3,4,6$, eight values of $\rho$ between $0.05$ and
$0.95$ and $13$ measures. Among these cylinders, for $a>1/2$ the winning $k$ decreases from $n$ (the ball, near $a=1/2$) to $1$ (the slab) as
$a\to1$, and for $a<1/2$ the picture is reflected, with complements, as the identity $\Stab(A^c)=1-2a+\Stab(A)$
requires. In $\R^6$ a cylinder with $1<k<6$ beats both the ball and the slab in $26$ of the $104$ cases, by up to
$3.8\times10^{-3}$ relative, the largest at $\rho=0.7$, $a=0.3$, where the complement of $C_3$ wins. Larger $\rho$
moves the winner towards smaller $k$. A Monte Carlo run with $4\times10^7$ pairs at $\rho=0.7$, $a=0.7$ gives
$\Stab(C_3)-\max(\Stab(C_1),\Stab(C_6))=4.8\times10^{-4}$, against $6.1\times10^{-4}$ by quadrature. The binomial
scale $\sqrt{2\cdot0.25/N}$, equal to $1.1\times10^{-4}$ to two figures at this $N$, ignores the positive correlation between the paired estimates and
so overstates the standard error of the difference.

These computations suggest the following refinement of the conjecture, which is Heilman's suggestion in the form of
cylinders.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:cyl}
For $0<\rho<1$ and $0<a<1$, the noise stability among symmetric subsets of $\R^n$ of Gaussian measure $a$ is maximized
by a cylinder $C_k(s)$ or the complement of one, for some $1\le k\le n$. In dimension one the maximizer is the centred
interval or its complement for every $\rho\in(0,1)$.
\end{conjecture}
