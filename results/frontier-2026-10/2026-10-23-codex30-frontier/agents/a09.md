## Model and the exact batch kernel

Let \(\sigma_a(z)=z\) for \(z\geq0\), \(\sigma_a(z)=az\) for \(z<0\), with \(0<a<1\), and use the half-squared loss \(\ell(w;X,Y)=\tfrac12(\sigma_a(w^\top X)-Y)^2\). At each iteration take **fresh, mutually independent** pairs \((X_i,Y_i)_{i=1}^b\), each with the central symmetry \((X,Y)\stackrel d=(-X,-Y)\), and update by the *average* gradient:

\[
 W^+=w-\frac{\eta}{b}\sum_{i=1}^b
 \sigma_a'(w^\top X_i)\bigl(\sigma_a(w^\top X_i)-Y_i\bigr)X_i.
\]

Assume \(\Pr(w^\top X=0)=0\) for every nonzero \(w\). For any fixed nonzero \(w\), set \(u=w^\top x\) and \(g_c(w;x,y)=c(cu-y)x\), \(c\in\{1,a\}\). The two gradients from the centrally symmetric orbit \((x,y),(-x,-y)\) are exactly \(g_1(w;x,y)\) and \(g_a(w;x,y)\), in some order; also \(g_c(w;-x,-y)=g_c(w;x,y)\). Thus for every bounded measurable test function \(f\),

\[
 \mathbb E f(g(w;X,Y))
 =\tfrac12\mathbb E f(g_1(w;X,Y))
  +\tfrac12\mathbb E f(g_a(w;X,Y)).
\]

Applying this identity **separately to each independent sample**, the batch transition kernel is exactly that of

\[
 W^+=A_b w+B_b,\qquad
 A_b=I-\frac{\eta}{b}\sum_{i=1}^b C_i^2 X_iX_i^\top,\qquad
 B_b=\frac{\eta}{b}\sum_{i=1}^b C_iY_iX_i,                 \tag{1}
\]

where \(C_i\) are *independent fair* \(\{1,a\}\) variables, independent of the iid original pairs. The same \(C_i\) occurs in both terms for sample \(i\), so \(A_b\) and \(B_b\) are generally dependent. This is equality **in conditional law for fixed \(w\)**, not a pathwise assertion that the actual sign gates are independent of \(X_i\). Nor is there one common gate for a whole batch. Fresh independent \((A_b,B_b)\) can be used at every step to reproduce the Markov law as long as \(W\ne0\).

For the Gaussian specialization below, with \(X\sim N(0,I_d)\) and independent symmetric \(Y\) of positive variance, starting at deterministic \(W_0\ne0\) avoids the kink for every finite time almost surely: conditional on fixed \(C_i,Y_i,w\ne0\), the equation \(A_bw+B_b=0\) is the zero set of a nonidentically-zero polynomial in the continuously distributed \(X_i\) (at all \(X_i=0\) it equals \(w\)). Hence a chosen convention for \(\sigma_a'(0)\) does not affect these results. If the unhalved squared loss is used, substitute \(2\eta\) for every \(\eta\) here.

## Exact Gaussian second-moment phase boundary

Now suppose \(X_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim N(0,I_d)\), \(Y_i\) are independent of all \(X_j\), symmetric with \(\mathbb EY_i^2=\tau^2>0\), and iid across \(i\). Write

\[
 c_2=\mathbb EC^2=\frac{1+a^2}{2},\qquad
 c_4=\mathbb EC^4=\frac{1+a^4}{2}.
\]

The mean obeys \(\mathbb E[W^+\mid w]=(1-\eta c_2)w\). To calculate the complete raw second moment \(M_n=\mathbb E[W_nW_n^\top]\), use the Gaussian fourth-moment identity
\(\mathbb E[XX^\top ww^\top XX^\top]=2ww^\top+\|w\|^2 I\). Terms from **different** examples in \(A_bww^\top A_b\) have expectation \(c_2^2ww^\top\), not zero. Terms \(B_iB_j^\top\) for \(i\ne j\) vanish because \(\mathbb EY_i=0\). Though \(A_b,B_b\) share \(C_i,X_i\), their mixed moment vanishes after conditioning on all \(C_i,X_i\), again because each \(Y_i\) is centered. Consequently,

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 M_{n+1}&=\alpha_bM_n+\beta_b\operatorname{tr}(M_n)I_d
           +\frac{\eta^2c_2\tau^2}{b}I_d,\\
 \alpha_b&=1-2\eta c_2
  +\frac{\eta^2}{b}\bigl((b-1)c_2^2+2c_4\bigr),\\
 \beta_b&=\frac{\eta^2c_4}{b}.                         \tag{2}
 \end{aligned}
\]

Taking the trace, \(m_n=\mathbb E\|W_n\|^2\) obeys the **exact** scalar recurrence

\[
 m_{n+1}=\rho_b m_n+q_b,\quad
 \rho_b=1-2\eta c_2+\frac{\eta^2}{b}
       \bigl((d+2)c_4+(b-1)c_2^2\bigr),\quad
 q_b=\frac{\eta^2d c_2\tau^2}{b}.                       \tag{3}
\]

The \((b-1)c_2^2\) term is the indispensable cross-example contribution. The individual fourth-moment term is \((d+2)c_4\). With \(\eta>0\), the exact mean-square phase boundary is

\[
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \eta_{2}(b,d,a)=
 \frac{2bc_2}{(d+2)c_4+(b-1)c_2^2}
 =\frac{2c_2}{c_2^2+[(d+2)c_4-c_2^2]/b}.}             \tag{4}
\]

For \(0<\eta<\eta_2\), \(\rho_b<1\), and the affine chain has a unique finite-second-moment stationary law (an \(L^2\) synchronous coupling contracts by factor \(\rho_b\)); its stationary covariance is isotropic:

\[
 \mathbb E\|W_\infty\|^2
 =\frac{\eta\,d c_2\tau^2}
 {2bc_2-\eta[(d+2)c_4+(b-1)c_2^2]},\qquad
 \mathbb E[W_\infty W_\infty^\top]
 =\frac{\mathbb E\|W_\infty\|^2}{d}I_d.               \tag{5}
\]

At \(\eta=\eta_2\), \(m_n=m_0+nq_b\); above it, \(m_n=\rho_b^nm_0+q_b(\rho_b^n-1)/(\rho_b-1)\) grows exponentially. A stationary law for the affine kernel may **still exist with infinite variance** above (4), when the Lyapunov exponent below is negative. If such a stationary law had finite second moment, (3) would force \(m=\rho_bm+q_b\), an impossibility. These stationary-law statements refer to the affine kernel and, for literal SGD, stationary laws with no atom at the kink; a specially chosen zero derivative at \(w=0\) can make an artificial absorbing \(\delta_0\). The threshold is strictly increasing in \(b\), because \((d+2)c_4-c_2^2>0\), and tends to \(2/c_2\). At \(a=1\), the useful limiting linear-neuron check is \(\eta_2=2b/(b+d+1)\).

## Exact homogeneous \(p\)-growth, Lyapunov exponent, and tail caveats

The law of \(A_b\) is invariant under orthogonal conjugation. For any deterministic unit \(e\), define
\(\lambda_b(p)=\mathbb E\|A_be\|^p\) for \(p>0\). If \(t_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim N(0,1)\), \(D_i=C_i^2\), \(Q\sim\chi^2_{d-1}\) independently (\(Q=0\) for \(d=1\)), and \(S_1=\sum_iD_it_i^2\), \(S_2=\sum_iD_i^2t_i^2\), then Gaussian orthogonal decomposition gives the exact low-dimensional integral

\[
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \lambda_b(p)=\mathbb E\left[
 \left\{\left(1-\frac{\eta S_1}{b}\right)^2
       +\frac{\eta^2S_2}{b^2}Q\right\}^{p/2}\right].}   \tag{6}
\]

In particular \(\lambda_b(2)=\rho_b\). Along the product \(A_n\cdots A_1v\), conditional on any past direction, the next radial factor has the same law \(\|A_be\|\), independent of the past. Therefore the successive radial factors are iid, and, for every \(v\ne0\),

\[
 \mathbb E\|A_n\cdots A_1v\|^p=\lambda_b(p)^n\|v\|^p,
 \qquad
 \gamma_b=\mathbb E\log\|A_be\|
 =\left.\frac{d}{dp}\log\lambda_b(p)\right|_{p=0}. \tag{7}
\]

The strong law and a finite-basis comparison show that \(\gamma_b\) is also the almost-sure top operator-norm Lyapunov exponent. Gaussian inputs make these logarithms integrable; Jensen additionally gives \(\gamma_b\leq\tfrac12\log\rho_b\), so (4)'s finite-variance region is stationary. If \(\gamma_b<0\), the backward affine perpetuity converges and gives a stationary law: the pair \((A_b,B_b)\) may be dependent *within a time step*, but time steps are iid. If \(\gamma_b<0\), strict convexity of \(\log\lambda_b(p)\) and unbounded Gaussian multipliers give one positive root \(\kappa_b\) of \(\lambda_b(\kappa_b)=1\). For Gaussian (or sufficiently light-tailed) \(Y\) and the additional nondegeneracy/nonarithmetic hypotheses of scalar implicit-renewal theory, this is the actual scalar stationary power-tail exponent. In multiple dimensions, identifying \(\kappa_b\) with an actual regularly varying stationary tail calls for a separate multivariate renewal-theorem verification; (6) by itself proves product growth, **not** the full tail asymptotic. With only \(\mathbb EY^2<\infty\), \(Y\) may itself have a heavy tail with exponent just above 2, so the actual stationary tail for \(\kappa_b>2\) is not determined by \(\lambda_b\) alone.

Relevant primary theory: C. M. Goldie, *Implicit Renewal Theory and Tails of Solutions of Random Equations*, Annals of Applied Probability 1 (1991), 126–166, [journal page](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-applied-probability/volume-1/issue-1/Implicit-Renewal-Theory-and-Tails-of-Solutions-of-Random-Equations/10.1214/aoap/1177005985.full); for general matrix recursions, Y. Guivarc'h, *Heavy tail properties of stationary solutions of multidimensional stochastic recursions*, [original preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0608239), and G. Alsmeyer & S. Mentemeier, *Tail behavior of stationary solutions of random difference equations: the case of regular matrices*, [original preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.1728). These sources motivate the theorem qualifications; equations (1)–(7) are derived here directly.

## What batching does and does not order

Let \(Z_i=(I-\eta D_iX_iX_i^\top)e\). Then \(A_be=b^{-1}\sum_iZ_i\). The mean of \(b+1\) iid \(Z_i\) is the average of its \(b+1\) leave-one-out means of size \(b\). Jensen's inequality for the convex map \(z\mapsto\|z\|^p\) proves, without any Gaussian assumption,

\[
 \lambda_{b+1}(p)\leq\lambda_b(p)\qquad\text{for every }p\geq1. \tag{8}
\]

It follows that, whenever a stationary multiplicative index \(\kappa_b\geq1\) is defined, larger batches have \(\kappa_{b'}\geq\kappa_b\) (when comparing defined roots): evaluate (8) at \(p=\kappa_b\), then use the unique positive crossing of \(\lambda_{b'}(p)=1\). In the finite-variance phase, \(\lambda_b(2)<1\), so \(\kappa_b>2\) and this monotonic conclusion is available, subject to the separate additive-noise caveat for *actual* tails.

For \(0<p<1\), \(z\mapsto\|z\|^p\) is not convex; (8) does **not** follow, nor does global tail-index monotonicity. There is even a Gaussian-design counterexample while both batches are stationary. Take \(d=1\), \(a=1\) initially (the linear-neuron limit), \(\eta=11/5\), and Gaussian \(Y\). Then \(A_b=1-(\eta/b)T_b\), \(T_b\sim\chi_b^2\), and

\[
 \lambda_b(p)=\frac{1}{2^{b/2}\Gamma(b/2)}
   \int_0^\infty\left|1-\frac{\eta t}{b}\right|^p
   t^{b/2-1}e^{-t/2}\,dt.                         \tag{9}
\]

One-dimensional quadrature of this explicit integral gives

| Batch | \(\gamma_b\) | \(\lambda_b(1/3)\) | Positive root \(\kappa_b\) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 8 | \(-0.233468\) | \(0.987419\) | \(\approx0.41123\) |
| 16 | \(-0.095891\) | \(1.008526\) | \(\approx0.25393\) |

The numerical comparisons in this table are evaluations of (9), not a claim of computer-verified interval bounds. Their margins show the mechanism: both logarithmic exponents are negative, but \(\lambda_8(1/3)<1<\lambda_{16}(1/3)\), so \(\kappa_8>1/3>\kappa_{16}\). Because these are strict inequalities and the integrals and logarithmic integrals are continuous in the slope near \(a=1\), the same failure persists for genuine \(0<a<1\) sufficiently close to 1. With Gaussian \(Y\), scalar renewal-theorem conditions (continuous, nonarithmetic coefficient; nondegenerate Gaussian additive innovation conditional on the design; all needed moments) make these roots actual stationary tail indices. Thus the index can decrease with batch even though the exact mean-square stability threshold always improves.

An analytic warning independent of numerical root evaluation: for \(d=1\), \(a=1\), \(b=2\), \(T_2/2\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)\), so
\(\gamma_2=-e^{-1/\eta}\operatorname{Ei}(1/\eta)<0\) at \(\eta=2\), hence also for \(\eta>2\) sufficiently close by continuity, whereas \(\gamma_b\to\log(\eta-1)>0\) as \(b\to\infty\). Thus outside a controlled moment regime, batching can ultimately remove stationary contraction rather than improve it indefinitely. This likewise persists for leaky slopes sufficiently near 1.
