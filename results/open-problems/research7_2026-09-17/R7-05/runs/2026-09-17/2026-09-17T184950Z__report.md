## 4. B27 — swap the quantifiers: one quadratic norm for **every** switching signal

The previous formulation asked, for each rate `r`, whether the stochastic exponent lies below the endpoint threshold. B27 swaps those quantifiers: seek one positive quadratic form which decreases for **all switching signals at once**.

### Theorem 4.1 — exact common-symmetrizer classification in the hyperbolic-average chart

For the pair `B_+(q),B_-(q)` above, there exists a symmetric positive-definite matrix `M` such that

\[
MB_s(q)=B_s(q)^T M\qquad(s=\pm1)
\]

if and only if

\[
q>0.
\]

When `q>0`, one may take explicitly

\[
\boxed{M(q)=\begin{pmatrix}q/b^2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
\]

#### Proof

Assume first `q>0`. Direct multiplication gives

\[
M\bar B=\bar B^TM
\]

and

\[
MD(q)=
\begin{pmatrix}
(q/b^2)a&q/b\\
q/b&-a
\end{pmatrix},
\]

which is symmetric. Hence `MB_s=B_s^TM` for both signs.

Conversely, suppose a positive-definite symmetric matrix

\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}m_{11}&m_{12}\\m_{12}&m_{22}\end{pmatrix}
\]

symmetrizes both `B_+` and `B_-`. Adding the two self-adjointness identities shows that `M\bar B=\bar B^TM`. Since the two eigenvalues of `\bar B` are the distinct numbers `\beta` and `-\beta`, the off-diagonal equation is

\[
-\beta m_{12}=\beta m_{12},
\]

hence `m_{12}=0`. Thus `M=diag(m_1,m_2)` with `m_1,m_2>0`.

Subtracting the two self-adjointness identities gives `MD=D^TM`. Its off-diagonal equation is

\[
m_1 b=m_2\frac q b.
\]

Therefore

\[
q=\frac{m_1}{m_2}b^2>0.
\]

This proves necessity and sufficiency. `QED`.

### Corollary 4.2 — simultaneous symmetric normal form

For `q>0`, let

\[
T=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt q/|b|&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=T^TT.
\]

Then

\[
C_s:=TB_sT^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\beta+sa&s\,\operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt q\\
s\,\operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt q&-\beta-sa
\end{pmatrix}
\]

is real symmetric for both `s=+1,-1`, with eigenvalues `+beta_s(q)` and `-beta_s(q)`.

This is stronger than a rate-by-rate statement: the same similarity works for both environments and for every rate.

### Corollary 4.3 — arbitrary-switching stability after Hurwitz scalar shifts

Write the original matrices in this chart as

\[
A_s=B_s-c_sI.
\]

If both `A_s` are Hurwitz and `q>0`, then

\[
c_s>\beta_s(q)\qquad(s=\pm1).
\]

Set

\[
\eta:=\min_{s=\pm1}\big(c_s-\beta_s(q)\big)>0.
\]

For **every measurable switching signal** `s(t) in {+1,-1}`, not merely for Markov switching, put `y=Tx`. Then

\[
\frac12\frac d{dt}\|y(t)\|^2
=y(t)^T(C_{s(t)}-c_{s(t)}I)y(t)
\le -\eta\|y(t)\|^2.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{\|Tx(t)\|\le e^{-\eta t}\|Tx(0)\|.}
\]

Thus **no stochastic switching-rate instability is possible anywhere on the `q>0` side of this hyperbolic-average chart once both endpoint matrices are Hurwitz**.

For the traceless pair itself the same calculation yields, for common-rate symmetric Markov switching whose occupation fractions are `1/2`,

\[
\boxed{F(q,r)\le S(q)\quad\text{for every }q>0,\ r>0.}
\]

The old positive-`q`, `r->0` boundary layer is therefore impossible for a much stronger reason than the desired asymptotic expansion.

### Boundary `q=0`

At `q=0` the pair is simultaneously upper triangular:

\[
B_s(0)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\beta+sa&sb\\
0&-\beta-sa
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

If `A_s=B_s(0)-c_sI` are both Hurwitz, all four diagonal rates are at most `-eta<0` for some `eta`. For any switching signal,

\[
|x_2(t)|\le e^{-\eta t}|x_2(0)|,
\]

and variation of constants gives

\[
|x_1(t)|\le e^{-\eta t}\big(|x_1(0)|+|b|t|x_2(0)|\big).
\]

For any `0<eta'<eta`, the right side is at most `C e^{-eta't}\|x(0)\|`. Thus the common-line boundary is also uniformly stable under arbitrary switching whenever both endpoint matrices are Hurwitz.

### Scope of the result

This is an exact theorem for the **hyperbolic-average canonical chart** used by the preceding R7-05 analysis. It is not a theorem that all planar all-Hurwitz pairs can be put in this chart: an elliptic traceless average is a genuinely different regime. Indeed the classical Lawley–Mattingly–Reed planar example has an elliptic/rotational traceless average. The present theorem therefore localizes the remaining contextual problem rather than falsely claiming a universal planar impossibility theorem.

## 5. B18 — say the normalized stress test as a one-dimensional PDMP and solve it exactly

The stuck diagnosis singled out

\[
B_s(q)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&s\\
sq&-1
\end{pmatrix},\qquad s\in\{\pm1\},\quad q>0,
\]

as the simplest unresolved stress test. Here

\[
\lambda:=\sqrt{1+q},\qquad \delta:=\lambda-1,
\]

so the endpoint top eigenvalue is `lambda` for both environments.

Let `z=y/x` be the projective coordinate. Between switches,

\[
\dot z=sq-2z-sz^2.
\]

Define the environment-corrected coordinate

\[
w:=sz.
\]

Then every deterministic leg has the **same** scalar drift

\[
\dot w=g(w):=q-2w-w^2=(\delta-w)(\delta+w+2),
\]

while each environment switch sends

\[
w\mapsto-w.
\]

On the natural attracting interval `[-delta,delta]` the generator is therefore

\[
\boxed{Lf(w)=g(w)f'(w)+r\big(f(-w)-f(w)\big).}
\]

This is the one-dimensional PDMP promised by the stuck diagnosis.

### 5.1 Exact stationary density

Let `p(w)` be a stationary density on `(-delta,delta)` and define the probability flux

\[
J(w):=g(w)p(w).
\]

The stationary forward equation is

\[
J'(w)=r\big(p(-w)-p(w)\big).
\]

Evaluating at `-w` shows `J'(-w)=-J'(w)`. Hence `d[J(w)-J(-w)]/dw=0`, and the difference vanishes at `w=0`. Therefore

\[
J(w)=J(-w).
\]

Substituting this evenness into the forward equation gives

\[
\frac{J'(w)}{J(w)}
=r\left(\frac1{g(-w)}-\frac1{g(w)}\right)
=
-\frac{4rw}{(\delta^2-w^2)((\delta+2)^2-w^2)}.
\]

Put

\[
\alpha:=\frac r{2\lambda}.
\]

Direct integration yields

\[
\boxed{
J(w)=C
\left[
\frac{\delta^2-w^2}{(\delta+2)^2-w^2}
\right]^{\alpha}
}
\]

and hence

\[
\boxed{
p(w)=\frac{C}{g(w)}
\left[
\frac{\delta^2-w^2}{(\delta+2)^2-w^2}
\right]^{\alpha}.
}
\]

The endpoint singularity at `w=delta` is of order `(delta-w)^{alpha-1}` and is integrable for every `r>0`; at `w=-delta` the density vanishes like `(w+delta)^alpha`.

Now change variables

\[
y:=\frac{\delta-w}{2\delta}\in[0,1].
\]

After cancelling constants, the invariant density becomes

\[
\boxed{
\pi_{\delta,r}(dy)\propto
 y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^\alpha
 (1+\delta y)^{-\alpha}
 (1+\delta(1-y))^{-\alpha-1}\,dy.
}
\]

This representation separates a Beta law from a bounded deformation and makes the singular two-parameter corner explicit.

### 5.2 Exact sign of the Lyapunov deficit

Along a deterministic leg,

\[
\frac d{dt}\log|x(t)|=1+sz=1+w.
\]

On the attracting projective class, `z` is bounded, so replacing `log|x|` by any fixed Euclidean norm changes the long-time average by a bounded coboundary. Thus the top Lyapunov exponent is

\[
F(q,r)=1+\mathbb E[w]
=\lambda-2\delta\,\mathbb E[y].
\]

Because the stationary density is positive in the interior, `E[y]>0`, and therefore

\[
\boxed{F(q,r)<\lambda=S(q)\qquad(q>0,r>0).}
\]

This reproduces, in the normalized model, the stronger common-symmetrizer conclusion by a completely different representation.

There is also a useful orientation check. Since `J` is even and `g(w)<g(-w)` for `w>0`, one has `p(w)>p(-w)` for `w>0`, hence `E[w]>0` and `F(q,r)>1`. So the formula is consistent with the top, rather than bottom, projective exponent.

### 5.3 Uniform two-parameter corner estimate

Write the invariant density as a Beta density times

\[
h(y):=(1+\delta y)^{-\alpha}(1+\delta(1-y))^{-\alpha-1}.
\]

A direct derivative gives

\[
\frac d{dy}\log h(y)=
\frac{\delta\,[1-\alpha\delta+\delta(2\alpha+1)y]}
{(1+\delta y)(1+\delta(1-y))}.
\]

Therefore, whenever `alpha*delta<1`, which holds throughout a full neighborhood of `(q,r)=(0,0)`, the function `h` is increasing. Moreover

\[
\frac{h(1)}{h(0)}=1+\delta=\lambda.
\]

The undeformed Beta law is `Beta(alpha,alpha+1)` and has mean

\[
m_0=\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+1}=\frac r{2(\lambda+r)}.
\]

Since `h_min <= h <= h_max` with `h_max/h_min=lambda`, elementary comparison of weighted expectations gives

\[
\frac{m_0}{\lambda}\le \mathbb E[y]\le \lambda m_0.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\delta r}{\lambda(\lambda+r)}
\le \lambda-F(q,r)
\le
\frac{\delta\lambda r}{\lambda+r}.
}
\]

Using

\[
q=\lambda^2-1=\delta(\lambda+1),
\]

we obtain

\[
\frac1{\lambda(\lambda+r)(\lambda+1)}
\le
\frac{\lambda-F(q,r)}{qr}
\le
\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+r)(\lambda+1)}.
\]

Both bounds converge to `1/2` as `(q,r)->(0,0)` through positive values. Hence

\[
\boxed{
F(q,r)-\lambda=-\frac12qr+o(qr)
}
\]

**uniformly in the joint corner**.

For this normalized family the overlap factor from the preceding diagnosis is

\[
K(q)=\frac1{1+q}=\lambda^{-2}.
\]

Thus

\[
\frac r2\log K(q)=-r\log\lambda=-\frac12qr+o(qr),
\]

and therefore the exact missing statement from the stuck report follows:

\[
\boxed{
F(q,r)-\lambda
=\frac r2\log K(q)+o(qr)
}
\]

uniformly as `(q,r)->(0,0)` in the positive quadrant.

So the **specific recovery action selected by the stuck diagnosis is complete**: the normalized positive-`q` one-dimensional PDMP admits an exact stationary density and its supposedly dangerous joint boundary layer has the unfavorable sign with a uniform quantitative margin.

## 6. B30 — localize the parameter space and identify the actual gluing failure

The canonical hyperbolic-average chart now splits sharply by the sign of `q`.

The pair is simultaneously self-adjoint in one positive-definite inner product. If the shifted endpoint matrices are Hurwitz, the system is uniformly exponentially stable under **arbitrary** switching. No stochastic instability band can occur at any rate.
