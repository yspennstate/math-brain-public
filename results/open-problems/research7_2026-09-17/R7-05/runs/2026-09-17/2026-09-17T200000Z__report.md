## 4. B12 — hidden gauge conservation for a traceless planar mode

Let

\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&-a\end{pmatrix},
\qquad e_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
\qquad e_\theta^\perp=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta).
\]

Define the angular velocity and radial growth functions

\[
f_B(\theta):=\langle Be_\theta,e_\theta^\perp\rangle,
\qquad
g_B(\theta):=\langle Be_\theta,e_\theta\rangle.
\]

A direct calculation gives

\[
f_B(\theta)
=\frac{c-b}{2}+\frac{b+c}{2}\cos 2\theta-a\sin 2\theta,
\]

\[
g_B(\theta)
=a\cos2\theta+\frac{b+c}{2}\sin2\theta,
\]

and therefore the exact identity

\[
\boxed{f_B'(\theta)=-2g_B(\theta).}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Suppose now that `B` is elliptic, so `f_B` never vanishes on projective space. Along a deterministic leg driven by `B`, if `R=\|X\|` and `\theta` is projective angle, then

\[
\dot\theta=f_B(\theta),\qquad \frac d{dt}\log R=g_B(\theta).
\]

Using (4.1),

\[
\frac d{dt}\left(\log R+\frac12\log|f_B(\theta)|\right)
=g_B+\frac12\frac{f_B'}{f_B}\dot\theta
=g_B+\frac12f_B'=0.
\]

Hence each elliptic mode has an exact pathwise gauge invariant:

\[
\boxed{R\sqrt{|f_B(\theta)|}\quad\text{is constant between environment switches}.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This turns the Lyapunov exponent in the co-rotating elliptic regime into a pure jump entropy-production quantity.

## 5. Exact scalar reduction for two co-rotating elliptic modes

Let `B_0,B_1` be traceless elliptic real `2 x 2` matrices whose projective angular fields have the same orientation. Reverse the common orientation if necessary, so

\[
f_0(\theta)>0,\qquad f_1(\theta)>0
\]

for every projective angle `\theta in R/(pi Z)`.

Let the environment switch `0 <-> 1` at common rate `r>0`. Write the stationary projective densities as `rho_0,rho_1`. Their forward equations are

\[
(f_0\rho_0)'=r(\rho_1-\rho_0),
\qquad
(f_1\rho_1)'=r(\rho_0-\rho_1).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Adding them shows that the stationary probability current

\[
J:=f_0\rho_0+f_1\rho_1
\tag{5.2}
\]

is constant in `\theta`.

Introduce the common-time coordinate `x` and the local mode fraction `u` by

\[
\frac{dx}{d\theta}=\frac1{f_0(\theta)}+\frac1{f_1(\theta)},
\qquad
u(\theta):=\frac{f_0(\theta)}{f_0(\theta)+f_1(\theta)}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Let its period be

\[
A:=\oint\left(\frac1{f_0}+\frac1{f_1}\right)d\theta.
\]

Finally define

\[
q(x):=\frac{f_0(\theta(x))\rho_0(\theta(x))}{J}.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Then `1-q=f_1 rho_1/J`. Dividing (5.1) by `dx/dtheta` gives the exact scalar ODE

\[
\boxed{q_x=r(u-q),\qquad q(x+A)=q(x).}
\tag{5.5}
\]

Thus `q` is nothing more than the periodic one-sided exponential smoothing of `u`:

\[
\boxed{
q_r(x)=\frac{r}{1-e^{-rA}}\int_0^A e^{-rs}u(x-s)\,ds.
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

The normalization of the stationary probability measure becomes

\[
1
=J\int_0^A\left[u+q-2uq\right]dx,
\]

or

\[
\boxed{
J(r)^{-1}=\int_0^A\left[u(x)+q_r(x)-2u(x)q_r(x)\right]dx.
}
\tag{5.7}
\]

Everything about the stationary angular law has therefore been reduced from two coupled transport equations to one scalar linear relaxation equation.

## 6. B12 main theorem — an exact Jeffreys-divergence formula for the Lyapunov exponent

Let `F(r)` denote the top Lyapunov exponent of the **traceless** switched pair `B_0,B_1`.

From the pathwise conservation law (4.2), the quantity

\[
Y_t:=\log R_t+\frac12\log f_{I_t}(\theta_t)
\]

is constant between environment jumps. At a switch `i -> 1-i`, its jump is

\[
\frac12\log\frac{f_{1-i}(\theta)}{f_i(\theta)}.
\]

Averaging jump increments in stationarity gives

\[
F(r)=\frac r2\int
(\rho_0-\rho_1)\log\frac{f_1}{f_0}\,d\theta.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Write

\[
\ell(s):=\log\frac{s}{1-s}.
\]

Since `f_1/f_0=(1-u)/u`, changing variables from `theta` to `x` and using (5.4) gives

\[
F(r)=\frac{Jr}{2}\int_0^A (u-q)\ell(u)\,dx.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The ODE (5.5) also implies

\[
\int_0^A(u-q)\ell(q)\,dx
=\frac1r\int_0^A q_x\ell(q)\,dx=0,
\tag{6.3}
\]

because `ell(q)` is the derivative of `q log q+(1-q)log(1-q)` and `q` is periodic. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
F(r)=\frac{J(r)r}{2}\int_0^A
(u-q_r)\bigl(\ell(u)-\ell(q_r)\bigr)\,dx.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

For `a,b in (0,1)`,

\[
(a-b)(\ell(a)-\ell(b))
=D(\operatorname{Ber}(a)\|\operatorname{Ber}(b))
+D(\operatorname{Ber}(b)\|\operatorname{Ber}(a)),
\]

so (6.4) is an exact integral of Bernoulli Jeffreys divergences.

### Theorem 6.1 — strict positivity in the co-rotating elliptic regime

For every finite `r>0`,

\[
\boxed{F(r)\ge0.}
\]

Moreover,

\[
F(r)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
B_1=cB_0\text{ for some }c>0.
\tag{6.5}
\]

#### Proof

The integrand in (6.4) is pointwise nonnegative because `ell` is strictly increasing. If the integral vanishes, then `u=q` almost everywhere. Equation (5.5) then gives `q_x=0`, hence `u` is constant. Constancy of

\[
u=\frac{f_0}{f_0+f_1}
\]

means `f_1=c f_0` for a positive constant `c`. Therefore the traceless matrix `B_1-cB_0` has identically zero projective vector field. A real matrix with zero projective vector field is scalar; since `B_1-cB_0` is traceless, it is zero. Hence `B_1=cB_0`. The converse is immediate. `QED`.

This is stronger than a perturbative sign calculation: switching between two nonproportional co-rotating elliptic traceless generators creates strictly positive radial growth at **every finite rate**.

For actual matrices `A_i=B_i-h_iI`, the symmetric environment spends asymptotic fraction `1/2` in each mode, so

\[
\lambda_A(r)=F(r)-\frac{h_0+h_1}{2}.
\tag{6.6}
\]

Thus the strong contextual problem in this regime is precisely a **shape** problem for the positive function `F`: can a positive horizontal level cut `F` in two separated rate bands?

## 7. B19 — quotient out similarity: the entire co-rotating elliptic problem is only two-dimensional

The reduction above is still written in terms of two arbitrary angular fields. The actual `2 x 2` matrix geometry constrains them much more strongly.

Because `B_0` is traceless elliptic, `B_0^2=-omega_0^2 I` for some `omega_0>0`. A fixed real similarity and a common positive rescaling put

\[
B_0=J_0:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Fixed similarity does not change the Lyapunov exponent, and common rescaling only rescales the rate axis. The remaining rotations commute with `J_0` and can be used to align the first harmonic of `B_1`.

Consequently every nonproportional co-rotating elliptic pair is represented by exactly two real parameters

\[
R>0,\qquad k>1,
\]

with angular speeds

\[
\boxed{f_0(\theta)=1,\qquad f_1(\theta)=R(k+\cos2\theta).}
\tag{7.2}
\]

The proportional case is the boundary `R=0` after keeping `Rk` finite.

The scalar profile in (5.3) is therefore

\[
\boxed{
u(\theta)=\frac1{1+R(k+\cos2\theta)}.}
\tag{7.3}
\]

The common-time coordinate has exact period

\[
\boxed{
A=\pi+\frac{\pi}{R\sqrt{k^2-1}}.
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

In particular the remaining co-rotating elliptic search is not a four- or six-parameter matrix search: modulo the exact invariances it is a two-parameter problem `(R,k)`, followed by the one-dimensional filter (5.5).

### A realizability first integral

Set

\[
y(x):=\ell(u(x))=\log\frac{u(x)}{1-u(x)}.
\]

From (7.3),

\[
y=-\log\bigl(R(k+\cos2\theta)\bigr),
\]

and `dtheta/dx=1-u`. Hence

\[
y_x=2Ru\sin2\theta.
\]

Eliminating `theta` by

\[
\cos2\theta=\frac{1-u}{Ru}-k
\]

gives the exact first integral

\[
\boxed{
\frac12 y_x^2
-4(1+Rk)u
+2\bigl((1+Rk)^2-R^2\bigr)u^2
=-2.
}
\tag{7.5}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
y_x^2
=4\bigl((1+Rk)^2-R^2\bigr)(u-u_-)(u_+-u),
\tag{7.6}
\]

where

\[
u_- =\frac1{1+R(k+1)},
\qquad
u_+=\frac1{1+R(k-1)}.
\]

Thus matrix-realizable profiles are a very small two-parameter energy shell inside the space of arbitrary periodic functions `u:(R/AZ)->(0,1)`. They have exactly one maximum and one minimum per projective period.

This first integral is the main reason the entropy formula by itself is not the end of the problem.

## 8. B19 obstruction test — the scalar entropy filter itself *can* have multiple humps

A tempting but false overgeneralization would be: for every periodic `u`, the function produced by (5.5)–(6.4) is unimodal in `r`. The next calculation disproves that generalized statement and therefore pinpoints exactly where matrix realizability must enter.

Take a circle of period `2pi`, fix `m in (0,1)`, and put

\[
u_\varepsilon(x)
=m+\varepsilon v(x),
\qquad
v(x)=\cos x+N^{-1/2}\cos(Nx),
\]

with `N>=16` and `|epsilon|` small enough that `0<u_epsilon<1`.

For the periodic filter

\[
q_x=r(u-q),
\]

the Fourier multiplier on frequency `omega` is `r/(r+i omega)`. Expanding (6.4) at the constant profile `u=m` gives, uniformly on compact rate intervals,

\[
F_\varepsilon(r)
=\frac{\varepsilon^2}{8m^2(1-m)^2}
\left[
\frac{r}{r^2+1}
+
\frac{Nr}{r^2+N^2}
\right]
+O(\varepsilon^3).
\tag{8.1}
\]

Indeed, at quadratic order the Jeffreys integrand is

\[
\frac{\varepsilon^2}{m(1-m)}|(I-T_r)v|^2,
\]

and the two orthogonal Fourier modes contribute independently.

Define

\[
f_N(r):=\frac{r}{r^2+1}+\frac{Nr}{r^2+N^2}.
\]

Then

\[
f_N(1)>\frac12,
\qquad
f_N(N)>\frac12,
\]

while

\[
f_N(\sqrt N)=\frac{2\sqrt N}{N+1}<\frac12
\qquad(N\ge16).
\tag{8.2}
\]

Therefore, for every fixed `N>=16` and all sufficiently small nonzero `epsilon`,

\[
F_\varepsilon(1)>F_\varepsilon(\sqrt N),
\qquad
F_\varepsilon(N)>F_\varepsilon(\sqrt N).
\]

Since the exact filtered response tends to zero at both rate endpoints, a horizontal level between the middle value and the smaller outer value cuts the generalized response in at least two separated components.

So **multi-band behavior is already present in the scalar entropy-production mechanism**. What prevents the construction above from solving the matrix problem is precise: its two-frequency profile has many extrema and violates the matrix realizability first integral (7.5). The missing theorem, if the co-rotating matrix regime is in fact unimodal, must exploit (7.5) or an equivalent projective `SL_2(R)` constraint. Positivity/entropy production alone cannot prove it.

This also explains why simply improving a generic stationary-density solver is unlikely to settle the remaining contextual problem: the decisive information is geometric, not numerical resolution.

## 9. B26 — the proportional elliptic boundary has a single-hump quadratic response

The exact equality case of Theorem 6.1 is the proportional locus

\[
B_1=cB_0,
\qquad c>0,
\]

for which `u` is constant and

\[
F(r)\equiv0.
\]

Now normalize `B_0=J_0` and perturb transversely:

\[
B_1=cJ_0+\varepsilon S,
\]

where the constant `J_0` component of the perturbation has been absorbed into `c`, so the projective angular perturbation is a single first harmonic

\[
h(\theta)=H\cos(2\theta-\psi).
\]

At `epsilon=0`,

\[
m=\frac1{1+c},
\qquad
A_0=\pi\left(1+\frac1c\right),
\qquad
x=\left(1+\frac1c\right)\theta.
\]

The first variation of the scalar profile is

\[
u(x)=m-\varepsilon\frac{H}{(1+c)^2}
\cos(\omega x-\psi)+O(\varepsilon^2),
\]

with the single frequency

\[
\boxed{\omega=\frac{2c}{1+c}.}
\tag{9.1}
\]

Applying the quadratic entropy expansion from Section 8 gives, uniformly for `r` in any fixed compact subset of `(0,infinity)`,

\[
\boxed{
F(r)
=\varepsilon^2\frac{H^2}{8c^2}
\frac{r\omega^2}{r^2+\omega^2}
+O(\varepsilon^3).
}
\tag{9.2}
\]

The leading term has exactly one critical point, a strict maximum at

\[
r=\omega.
\]

Thus a two-band rate response cannot bifurcate at quadratic order from the proportional co-rotating elliptic boundary. This is not yet a full neighborhood theorem because the `O(epsilon^3)` control above is only uniform on compact rate intervals; a tiny secondary feature escaping toward `r=0` or `r=infinity` is not excluded. The correct status is therefore a **proved infinitesimal obstruction**, not a global impossibility theorem.
