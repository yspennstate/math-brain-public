## 3. B3 — prove a stronger spectral statement: every finite-group cardinality threshold already works

The preceding run proved Conjecture 5.7 for groups of order at most three by exploiting layer transitivity. This run first removes the spectral/cardinality issue in **all finite dimensions**.

### Lemma 3.1 — geometric-mean domination for Poisson-binomial sums

Let `lambda_1,...,lambda_N in [0,1]`, let independent `xi_i~Bern(lambda_i)`, and define

\[
p=(\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)^{1/N}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^N\xi_i\succeq_{\rm st}\operatorname{Bin}(N,p).}
\tag{3.1}
\]

#### Proof
If one parameter vanishes then `p=0` and the assertion is immediate. Otherwise, for two parameters `a,b`, put `g=sqrt(ab)`. The two nontrivial upper tails satisfy

\[
\Pr(B_a+B_b\ge2)=ab=g^2,
\]

and

\[
\Pr(B_a+B_b\ge1)=a+b-ab\ge2g-g^2
\]

by AM-GM. Hence

\[
B_a+B_b\succeq_{\rm st}B_g+B_g'.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Adding independent summands preserves stochastic order. Therefore pairwise geometric averaging of any two Bernoulli parameters can only decrease the total sum in stochastic order while preserving the product of all parameters.

Write `x_i=log lambda_i`. Repeated pairwise arithmetic averaging of the `x_i` along any connected averaging schedule preserves their mean and drives their variance to zero. In the original parameters this converges to `(p,...,p)`. Tail probabilities of a Poisson-binomial law are continuous polynomials in the parameters. Passing to the limit in the monotone chain of stochastic dominations proves (3.1). `QED`

For finite `Q`, Lyons's Theorem 2.11 gives

\[
|X|\stackrel d=\sum_i\operatorname{Bern}(\lambda_i),\qquad X\sim P^Q.
\]

On a finite group of order `N`,

\[
\operatorname{FK}(Q)=(\det Q)^{1/N}=(\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)^{1/N}.
\]

Therefore, for **every finite group**, every cardinality threshold already obeys the conjectured lower bound:

\[
\boxed{
\Pr_{P^Q}(|X|\ge m)
\ge
\Pr(\operatorname{Bin}(N,\operatorname{FK}(Q))\ge m),
\quad 0\le m\le N.
}
\tag{3.3}
\]

**B3 outcome: SUCCESS.** Any finite-group counterexample must distinguish configurations inside a fixed-cardinality layer. A cardinality-only obstruction is impossible.

## 4. B14 — quotient by translation: invariant upper sets are enough

Let `mu=P^Q` and `nu=Ber(p)^{otimes Gamma}` on a finite group. Both laws are left-translation invariant. For an arbitrary increasing event `E`, define

\[
f(A)=\frac1{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}{\bf1}_E(gA).
\]

Then `f` is invariant and increasing, while invariance of the laws gives

\[
\mathbb E_\mu f=\mu(E),\qquad \mathbb E_\nu f=\nu(E).
\]

Because the state space is finite,

\[
f=\int_0^1\mathbf1_{\{f\ge t\}}\,dt,
\]

and every superlevel event is a translation-invariant upper set. Thus it is enough to verify stochastic domination on the quotient poset of translation orbits of subsets.

At order four the exact self-check finds eight invariant upper sets for `Z/4Z` and twelve for `(Z/2Z)^2`. After deleting trivial/cardinality events using (3.3), only a handful of pair-orbit events remain.

## 5. B11 — Fourier duality solves both groups of order four

Every group of order four is abelian, so an equivariant Hermitian positive contraction diagonalizes in the character basis. Write the four eigenvalues as

\[
a,b,c,d\in[0,1],\qquad p=(abcd)^{1/4}.
\]

### 5.1 Cyclic group `Z/4Z`

There are two pair orbits: adjacent and antipodal. If `D_A` and `D_O` denote the inclusion probabilities of a fixed adjacent or antipodal pair, and `T` the inclusion probability of a fixed triple, Fourier evaluation of the principal minors gives

\[
D_O=\frac{(a+c)(b+d)}4,
\tag{5.1}
\]

\[
D_A=\frac{(a+c)(b+d)+2(ac+bd)}8,
\tag{5.2}
\]

\[
T=\frac{abc+abd+acd+bcd}{4}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

The antipodal-pair event has probability

\[
2D_O-p^4\ge2p^2-p^4
\]

because `(a+c)(b+d)/4 >= sqrt(acbd)=p^2`.

For the adjacent-pair event, inclusion-exclusion gives

\[
\Pr(E_A)=4D_A-4T+p^4,
\]

so the product comparison is equivalent to

\[
D_A-T\ge p^2(1-p).
\tag{5.4}
\]

Put

\[
u=\sqrt{ac},\quad v=\sqrt{bd},\quad A=a+c,\quad B=b+d.
\]

Then `A>=2u`, `B>=2v`, `p=sqrt(uv)`, and eight times the difference in (5.4) is

\[
F(A,B,u,v)
=AB+2u^2+2v^2-2u^2B-2v^2A-8uv+8(uv)^{3/2}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

For `0<=u,v<=1`,

\[
\partial_A F=B-2v^2\ge2v(1-v)\ge0,
\quad
\partial_B F=A-2u^2\ge2u(1-u)\ge0.
\]

Thus the minimum occurs at `A=2u`, `B=2v`. If `x=sqrt(u)`, `y=sqrt(v)`, then

\[
\boxed{
F(2u,2v,u,v)
=2(x-y)^2\big((x+y)^2-2x^2y^2\big)\ge0,
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

since `(x+y)^2>=4xy>=2x^2y^2`. This proves all remaining invariant upper sets for `Z/4Z`.

### 5.2 Klein four group

For `(Z/2Z)^2`, there are three pair orbits, each a perfect matching. For a nonzero group element `g`, the four characters split into two `+1` and two `-1` values, so the corresponding pair-minor is

\[
D_g=\frac{(\lambda_{+1}+\lambda_{+2})(\lambda_{-1}+\lambda_{-2})}{4}\ge p^2.
\tag{5.7}
\]

This settles every invariant upper set generated by one matching type. For the union of two matching types, relabel so

\[
D_1=\frac{(a+c)(b+d)}4,
\qquad
D_2=\frac{(a+b)(c+d)}4.
\]

Its event probability is

\[
2(D_1+D_2)-4T+p^4,
\]

and comparison with product measure is equivalent to

\[
D_1+D_2-2T\ge2p^2(1-p).
\tag{5.8}
\]

After multiplying by four and setting

\[
A=a+d,\quad B=b+c,\quad u=\sqrt{ad},\quad v=\sqrt{bc},
\]

(5.8) reduces **exactly** to the same `F(A,B,u,v)>=0` in (5.5), so (5.6) proves it.

Hence the conjectured lower domination holds for both groups of order four. Applying the same lower theorem to `I-Q` and taking complements proves the upper domination. The full-set and empty-set events force the constants, so the bounds are sharp.

### Theorem 5.1 — exact order-four extension

For every finite group `Gamma` with `|Gamma|<=4` and every `Gamma`-equivariant positive contraction `Q`,

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Ber}(\operatorname{FK}(Q))^{\otimes\Gamma}
\preccurlyeq P^Q
\preccurlyeq
\operatorname{Ber}(1-\operatorname{FK}(I-Q))^{\otimes\Gamma},
}
\]

with both constants optimal.
