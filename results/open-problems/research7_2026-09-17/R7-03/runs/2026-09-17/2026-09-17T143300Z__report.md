## 4. B25 — count the same structure twice: missing degree one is impossible

### Proposition 4.1

In the `z=0` branch, no vertex has `a_x=1`.

### Proof

Suppose `a_x=1`. Let `m` be the unique nonneighbor of `x`.

Because `|P(x)|=2a_x-1`, we have

`P(x)={p}`

for one source `p`. Also

`T(x)=N^+(x) union {m}`

and the capacity lemma is at equality. Since `P(x)` is a singleton, equality says

`N^+(p)=T(x)\{p}`.    (4.1)

There are two cases.

#### Case A: `p=m`

Then (4.1) gives

`N^+(p)=N^+(x)=:A`,

where `|A|=8`.

Because both `x` and `p` dominate every vertex of `A`, no vertex of `A` can point to either `x` or `p`. Thus the vertices reachable from `x` in exactly two steps via `A` are exactly the same as those reachable from `p` in exactly two steps via `A`; neither `x` nor `p` occurs among those second-step targets.

Since every source has exactly three unreachable targets, there exist two vertices `r,s` such that

`U(x)={p,r,s}` and `U(p)={x,r,s}`.

In particular `x,p in P(r)`. But every positive target capacity is tight in the `z=0` branch, so `P(r)` induces a tournament. The vertices `x,p` are nonadjacent by `p=m`, contradiction.

#### Case B: `p != m`

Since `P(x) subset T(x)` and `p` is not the unique nonneighbor `m`, necessarily

`x -> p`.

Put

`S=N^+(x)\{p}`.

Then (4.1) is

`N^+(p)=S union {m}`.    (4.2)

We claim `x in U(p)`. Indeed `p not-> x`, every member of `S` is dominated by `x` and therefore cannot point to `x`, and `m` is nonadjacent to `x`. Hence no path of length at most two leads from `p` to `x`.

Write

`U(p)={x,r,s}`.

We next show `r,s in U(x)`. Take `r`. Because `r in U(p)`, neither `p` nor any member of `S union {m}=N^+(p)` points to `r`. If `x->r`, then `r in S` (it is not `p`), but (4.2) would imply `p->r`, contradiction. Hence `x not->r`. Moreover neither `p` nor any member of `S=N^+(x)\{p}` points to `r`, so `r in U(x)`. The same argument gives `s in U(x)`.

Thus for either `r` or `s` we have

`x,p in P(r)`.

At target `r`, equality in the capacity lemma gives a tournament `P(r)` and a common set

`Q_r=T(r)\P(r)`

such that for every `v in P(r)`,

`N^+(v)=Q_r union O_{P(r)}(v)`.    (4.3)

Since `Q_r subset N^+(x)` and `m notin N^+(x)`, we have `m notin Q_r`. But `m in N^+(p)` by (4.2). Equation (4.3) therefore forces

`m in P(r)`.

Now both `x,m in P(r)`, and `P(r)` is a tournament, contradicting that `m` is nonadjacent to `x`.

Both cases are impossible. QED.

### Corollary 4.2 — the missing graph is forced to be a 2-factor

Every `a_x>=2`, while

`sum_x a_x=38=19*2`.

Therefore

`a_x=2` for every vertex `x`.    (4.4)

Since every vertex has outdegree eight on eighteen possible neighbors, (4.4) also gives

`d^-(x)=18-8-2=8`.

So any zero-slack order-19 counterexample would be **8-outregular and 8-inregular**, and its graph of missing unordered pairs would be a **2-regular graph on 19 vertices**, i.e. a disjoint union of cycles.

Furthermore `q_x=2`, hence

`|P(x)|=3`, `|Q_x|=7`.

The equality structure forces every vertex of `P(x)` to have exactly one outgoing edge inside the 3-vertex tournament `P(x)`. Therefore:

> **Every `P(x)` is a directed 3-cycle.**

If `P(x)=(p_1,p_2,p_3)` is oriented `p_1->p_2->p_3->p_1`, then

`N^+(p_1)=Q_x union {p_2}`,

`N^+(p_2)=Q_x union {p_3}`,

`N^+(p_3)=Q_x union {p_1}`.

This is a much smaller structural class than the arbitrary 19-vertex MILP attempted in the first run.

## 5. B11 — dualize by reversing every arc

The preceding reduction is self-dual in a strong sense.

### Proposition 5.1

In the zero-slack branch, the reverse digraph `D^R` is also a 19-vertex SSNC counterexample with outdegree eight at every vertex, and

`P_{D^R}(x)=U_D(x)`.

### Proof

Fix `x`. In `D`, exactly eight vertices point directly to `x` because `d^-(x)=8`. Exactly three vertices cannot reach `x` in one or two steps, namely `P_D(x)`, which has size three. The remaining

`18-8-3=7`

vertices reach `x` in exactly two steps.

In the reverse digraph, the eight direct in-neighbors of `x` in `D` become the eight direct out-neighbors of `x`, while those seven exact two-step predecessors become the exact second out-neighborhood of `x`. Hence

`|N^+_{D^R}(x)|=8`, `|N^{++}_{D^R}(x)|=7`.

Thus every vertex still fails Seymour's inequality.

Finally, `v in P_{D^R}(x)` means that in `D^R`, `v` cannot reach `x` in at most two steps. Reversing paths, this is equivalent to saying that in `D`, `x` cannot reach `v` in at most two steps, i.e.

`v in U_D(x)`.

Therefore `P_{D^R}(x)=U_D(x)`. QED.

Because the same target-capacity equality holds in `D^R`, the previous directed-triangle conclusion transfers back:

> **Every row triple `U_D(x)` also induces a directed 3-cycle in `D`.**

Thus both the three sources that fail to reach a target and the three targets unreachable from a source are directed triangles.

## 6. B1 — change category: a linear `(19_3)` incidence configuration

Let `M` be the `19 x 19` zero-one matrix

`M_{vx}=1  iff  x in U(v)`.

Every row and every column has sum three.

### Lemma 6.1 — row linearity

For distinct sources `u,v`,

`|U(u) intersect U(v)| <= 1`.    (6.1)

### Proof

Suppose `x in U(u) intersect U(v)`. Then `u,v in P(x)`. Since `P(x)` is a directed 3-cycle, `u,v` are adjacent; orient notation so `u->v`. Let `w` be the third member, so

`u->v->w->u`.

From the equality formulas above,

`N^+(u)=Q_x union {v}`,

`N^+(v)=Q_x union {w}`.

Consequently

`Q_x=N^+(u) intersect N^+(v)`,

and `w` is the unique element of `N^+(v)\N^+(u)`.

If a second target `y` also lay in `U(u) intersect U(v)`, then the same ordered pair `u->v` inside the directed triangle `P(y)` forces

`Q_y=N^+(u) intersect N^+(v)=Q_x`

and forces its third triangle vertex to be the same unique `w`. Hence

`P(y)=P(x)` and `T(y)=T(x)`.

Distinct targets cannot have the same trap. Indeed if `T(x)=T(y)` with `x!=y`, then `x notin T(x)=T(y)` implies `x->y`, while `y notin T(y)=T(x)` implies `y->x`, a forbidden digon.

Therefore `y=x`, proving (6.1). QED.

Applying the same lemma to the reverse digraph and using `U_{D^R}(x)=P_D(x)` gives the dual statement:

### Corollary 6.2 — column linearity

For distinct targets `x,y`,

`|P(x) intersect P(y)| <= 1`.    (6.2)

Hence `M` is the incidence matrix of a **linear symmetric `(19_3)` configuration**: 19 points, 19 blocks, each point in three blocks, each block containing three points, and no pair of points or blocks meeting twice.

### 6.3 Two 6-regular triangle-decomposed graphs

Define a graph `L` on the 19 sources: join `u,v` iff their rows of `M` meet. Every source lies in three blocks and each block supplies two distinct neighbors, so `L` is 6-regular. The 19 triples `P(x)` form 19 edge-disjoint triangles, and

`19 * 3 = 57 = 19*6/2`,

so these triangles partition `E(L)`. Because every `P(x)` is a directed 3-cycle, the orientation inherited from `D` has

`d_L^+(v)=d_L^-(v)=3`.

Dually define `R` by joining two targets when they lie in one row triple `U(v)`. Then `R` is also 6-regular, its edges are partitioned by the 19 directed triangles `U(v)`, and

`d_R^+=d_R^-=3`.

Both `L` and `R` are subgraphs of the underlying adjacency graph of `D`, because every corresponding triple is a directed triangle.

### 6.4 Exact matrix identities and a spectral filter

By row and column linearity,

`M M^T = 3 I + A_L`,

`M^T M = 3 I + A_R`.    (6.3)

Thus `L` and `R` are cospectral 6-regular graphs. In particular every eigenvalue of each is at least `-3`, because both matrices in (6.3) are positive semidefinite after adding `3I`.

The Levi graph of the incidence configuration is cubic bipartite on 38 vertices with 57 edges and has no 4-cycles.

This gives an exact finite classification target independent of the original second-neighborhood language.

### 6.5 Decomposition of the oriented adjacency graph

Let `H` be the missing-edge graph. From Corollary 4.2,

`deg_H=2`.

The underlying adjacency graph of `D` is `K_19 - H`, hence 16-regular. Since `L` is a 6-regular spanning subgraph, the remaining adjacency edges form a 10-regular spanning graph `K`.

The inherited orientations split the regular degrees exactly:

- on `L`: 3 out, 3 in;
- on `K`: 5 out, 5 in;
- missing graph `H`: degree 2.

So any `z=0` counterexample admits the exact structural decomposition

`K_19 = H (2-factor of missing pairs) + L (6-regular directed-triangle factor) + K (10-regular, 5-in/5-out factor)`.

This is now a substantially smaller exact-search space.

## 7. Relation to the nearest solved Eulerian class

Corollary 4.2 makes `D` Eulerian. Cary's 2019 theorem is therefore the nearest directly relevant solved class found in the fresh literature pass.

The subdigraph carried by `L` itself has an especially simple directed-cycle decomposition: its 57 arcs are partitioned into 19 directed 3-cycles, and row linearity implies any two such cycles share at most one vertex. Hence the **L-part alone** has a simple cycle-intersection structure of the kind Cary studies.

However, the full hypothetical counterexample contains the additional 5-in/5-out `K`-part. Cary's special-class theorem does not automatically survive addition of those arcs, because adding first-neighborhood arcs can simultaneously remove vertices from exact second neighborhoods. Therefore this is not a proof of the order-19 case.

It does identify the precise gluing obstruction:

> start from an Eulerian 3-in/3-out triangle-decomposed digraph in Cary's tractable class, add an Eulerian 5-in/5-out spanning orientation while preserving the 2-factor of missing pairs, and determine whether every Seymour vertex can be destroyed.

That is a more source-aligned next problem than repeating the unrestricted MILP.
