## 3. Recovered order-19 notation and exact slack identity

Assume for contradiction that `D` is a 19-vertex oriented graph with minimum outdegree eight and no Seymour vertex. Write

`d^+(v)=8+r_v`,

and define

`U(v)=V \ ({v} union N_1^+(v) union N_2^+(v))`.

Thus `U(v)` is the set of vertices not reachable from `v` in one or two directed steps. Since `v` is not Seymour,

`|N_2^+(v)| <= d^+(v)-1`,

hence

`|U(v)| >= max(0,3-2r_v)`.                                (3.1)

For a target `x`, write

`P(x)={v:x in U(v)}`,

let `a_x` be the number of vertices nonadjacent to `x`, and let

`T(x)={y!=x:y not->x}`.

Then

`|T(x)|=d^+(x)+a_x=8+r_x+a_x`.

Define

`q_x=r_x+a_x`,

so `|T(x)|=8+q_x`.

The first R7-03 run proved the target-capacity lemma:

> If `P(x)` is nonempty, then `q_x>=1` and
> 
> `|P(x)| <= 2q_x-1`.                                      (3.2)
>
> If equality holds, every source in `P(x)` has outdegree exactly eight, `P(x)` induces a tournament, and every possible arc from `P(x)` to `T(x)\P(x)` is present and directed out of `P(x)`.

For `q_x>=1`, define the nonnegative capacity slack

`c_x=(2q_x-1)-|P(x)|`,

and put

`C=sum_{q_x>=1} c_x`.

Also define

`z=#{x:q_x=0}`,

`h=#{v:r_v>=2}`,

`E=sum_{r_v>=2}(r_v-2)`,

and source slack

`L=sum_v (|U(v)|-max(0,3-2r_v))`.

The exact global identity is

`z = h + 2E + L + C`.                                      (3.3)

Earlier R7-03 work proved the complete `z=0` branch impossible. The immediately preceding recovery report separately eliminated H1, one of the three `z=1` arithmetic branches. The goal here is to understand the remaining defect by a local equality theorem rather than another global count.

## 4. B11 — DUALIZE: capacity equality is a regular-tournament / radius-two certificate

The previous report proposed testing whether L1 and C1 become exact duals under arc reversal. They do **not** in the naive sense: reversal sends

`d_D^-(x)=18-a_x-(8+r_x)=10-q_x`,

so the reversed outdegree can fall below eight when `q_x>2`. Therefore reversal does not preserve the order-19 minimum-outdegree-eight counterexample class in the branches we need.

The useful dualization is instead local: turn a tight target-capacity inequality into a statement about two-step reachability **inside the preimage tournament**.

### Lemma 4.1 — a tight target has a regular preimage tournament

Let `x` satisfy `q_x>=1` and `c_x=0`. Put `q=q_x` and `P=P(x)`. Then

1. `|P|=2q-1`;
2. every `p in P` has outdegree exactly eight;
3. `P` is a tournament;
4. every `p in P` points to every vertex of `Q:=T(x)\P`;
5. every `p in P` has exactly `q-1` outneighbors inside `P`.

In particular, `D[P]` is a regular tournament of order `2q-1`.

### Proof

Items 1--4 are exactly the equality case of the target-capacity lemma. Since

`|T(x)|=8+q`

and

`|P|=2q-1`,

we have

`|Q|=(8+q)-(2q-1)=9-q`.

Every `p in P` has total outdegree eight and points to every vertex of `Q`, so its internal outdegree is

`8-(9-q)=q-1`.

A tournament on `2q-1` vertices has average outdegree `q-1`, so this also matches regularity exactly. QED.

### Lemma 4.2 — every vertex of a regular tournament is a king

If `R` is a regular tournament and `p,y` are distinct vertices of `R`, then either `p->y` or there is `w in R` with

`p->w->y`.

### Proof

Suppose `y->p` and no outneighbor `w` of `p` points to `y`. Since `R` is a tournament, `y` then points to every outneighbor of `p`. If the regular tournament has order `2q-1`, then `p` has `q-1` outneighbors, so `y` points to `p` and to those `q-1` vertices, giving at least `q` outneighbors. But regularity gives exactly `q-1`, contradiction. QED.

The point of B11 is that equality in a *counting* inequality has become a metric statement: the source `p` reaches all of `P(x)` within directed distance at most two.

## 5. B25 — COUNT THE SAME THING TWICE: two tight unreachable targets cannot share a source

The local regular-tournament certificate immediately gives the missing one-defect propagation mechanism.

### Theorem 5.1 — pairwise tight-target obstruction

Let `p` be any vertex. Suppose `x!=y` are both in `U(p)`. If

`q_x>=1`, `q_y>=1`, `c_x=0`, and `c_y=0`,

then no such configuration exists.

Equivalently: **for every source `p`, at most one vertex of `U(p)` can be a positive-`q` target whose capacity inequality is tight.**

### Proof

Because `x in U(p)`, we have `p in P(x)`. Target `x` is tight, so Lemma 4.1 applies.

Partition

`V = {x} disjoint_union P(x) disjoint_union Q_x disjoint_union R_x`,

where

`Q_x=T(x)\P(x)`

and

`R_x=N^-(x)`.

The second unreachable target `y` is distinct from `x`.

- `y` cannot lie in `Q_x`, because Lemma 4.1 says `p` points directly to every vertex of `Q_x`.
- `y` cannot lie in `P(x)`, because Lemma 4.2 says `p` reaches every other member of the regular tournament `P(x)` in at most two steps.

Therefore

`y in R_x=N^-(x)`,

so

`y->x`.                                                     (5.1)

Now reverse the roles of `x` and `y`. Since `y` is also a tight positive target and `x in U(p)`, the identical argument gives

`x->y`.                                                     (5.2)

Equations (5.1) and (5.2) are impossible in an oriented graph. QED.

This theorem can also be read as an exact double count of reachability. A tight target `x` partitions every other unreachable target of one of its sources into the strict in-neighborhood of `x`; asking two targets to be simultaneously tight forces the same ordered pair to be oriented in both directions.

### Lemma 5.2 — zero-q targets never occur in an unreachable set

If `q_x=0`, then `P(x)=empty`. Hence no source `p` has `x in U(p)`.

### Proof

If `P(x)` were nonempty, the target-capacity lemma would require

`1 <= |P(x)| <= 2q_x-1=-1`,

impossible. QED.

The combination of Theorem 5.1 and Lemma 5.2 is the defect-propagation theorem missing from the first stuck diagnosis.

## 6. B30 — LOCALIZE THE DEFECT: every positive-z counterexample needs at least two slack targets

Call a positive target `x` **slack** if `q_x>=1` and `c_x>0`, and **tight** if `q_x>=1` and `c_x=0`.

### Theorem 6.1 — two-slack-target theorem

In any hypothetical order-19 minimum-outdegree-eight counterexample with `z>0`, there are at least two distinct slack positive targets. Consequently

`C>=2`.                                                     (6.1)

### Proof

Choose a vertex `s` with `q_s=0`. Because

`q_s=r_s+a_s=0`

and both terms are nonnegative,

`r_s=0`, `a_s=0`.

Thus `s` itself has outdegree eight. Since `s` is assumed non-Seymour, (3.1) gives

`|U(s)|>=3`.                                                (6.2)

By Lemma 5.2, no `q=0` target can belong to `U(s)`. Therefore every member of `U(s)` is a positive target.

By Theorem 5.1, at most one member of `U(s)` can be tight. Since `U(s)` contains at least three distinct vertices, at least two of them must be slack positive targets. They are distinct, and each contributes at least one to `C`. Hence `C>=2`. QED.

This is stronger than proving L1 and C1 separately. It says that the very existence of a universally adjacent `q=0` vertex forces **two independent target-capacity defects**.

### Corollary 6.2 — all z=1 branches are impossible

There is no hypothetical order-19 counterexample with `z=1`.

### Proof

If `z=1`, Theorem 6.1 gives `C>=2`. But the exact slack identity (3.3) gives

`1=z=h+2E+L+C>=C>=2`,

contradiction. QED.

Thus the two branches left by the previous report,

`(L1): (z,h,E,L,C)=(1,0,0,1,0)`

and

`(C1): (z,h,E,L,C)=(1,0,0,0,1)`,

are both impossible. H1 is also reproved more economically, because it has `z=1,C=0`.

Combined with the already-proved impossibility of `z=0`, every surviving order-19 counterexample must satisfy

`boxed: z>=2`.                                              (6.3)

This is the main mathematical result of the run.

## 7. The sharp next finite slice: exact structure when z=2

The new theorem makes the next unresolved case almost rigid before any solver is used.

### Proposition 7.1 — z=2 forces exactly two unit-slack targets

If a hypothetical counterexample has `z=2`, then

`h=0`, `E=0`, `L=0`, `C=2`.                                (7.1)

Moreover there are exactly two distinct slack positive targets `t_1,t_2`, and

`c_{t_1}=c_{t_2}=1`.                                       (7.2)

For each of the two `q=0` vertices `s`, there is a unique tight positive target `x_s` such that

`U(s)={t_1,t_2,x_s}`.                                      (7.3)

### Proof

Theorem 6.1 gives `C>=2`. Substitute into

`2=z=h+2E+L+C`.

All terms are nonnegative integers, so equality forces (7.1).

The proof of Theorem 6.1 shows there are at least two distinct slack targets. Since their positive integer slacks sum to `C=2`, there are exactly two and each has slack one, giving (7.2).

Now fix a `q=0` vertex `s`. From `L=0` and `r_s=0`,

`|U(s)|=3`.

No `q=0` target lies in `U(s)`. At most one positive tight target lies in `U(s)` by Theorem 5.1, while there are only the two slack targets `t_1,t_2`. Therefore both slack targets must occur, and the third member is one tight positive target `x_s`. This is (7.3). QED.

### Proposition 7.2 — orientation forced around each q=0 source

For each `q=0` source `s` and its tight target `x_s` from (7.3),

`t_1->x_s`, `t_2->x_s`,                                   (7.4)

and

`t_1->s`, `t_2->s`, `x_s->s`.                              (7.5)

### Proof

Because `x_s` is tight and `s in P(x_s)`, apply the proof of Theorem 5.1 only on the `x_s` side. Each other member of `U(s)`, namely `t_1,t_2`, can lie neither in the regular tournament `P(x_s)` nor in `T(x_s)\P(x_s)`. Therefore each lies in `N^-(x_s)`, proving (7.4).

Also `a_s=0`, so `s` is adjacent to every other vertex. Since every member of `U(s)` is not an outneighbor of `s`, each must point to `s`, proving (7.5). QED.

This is the recommended finite handoff point: two universal `q=0` sources, two unit-slack targets, and one tight target attached to each universal source.

## 8. Exact one-unit target deficit identity for the next owner

The two slack targets in the `z=2` slice each satisfy `c_t=1`. Their local capacity count has a useful exact refinement.

Fix such a target `t`, put `q=q_t`, and let

`P=P(t)`, `k=|P|=2q-2`, `Q=T(t)\P`.

In the `z=2` slice, `h=0`, so every source has `r_v in {0,1}`. Define

`R_P=sum_{v in P} r_v`,

`M_P=binom(k,2)-e(P,P)`,

and

`D_P=k|Q|-e(P,Q)`.

Thus `M_P` counts missing unordered pairs inside the induced oriented graph on `P`, while `D_P` measures failure of the complete domination `P->Q` that holds at a tight target.

All outneighbors of every `v in P` lie in `T(t)`, because otherwise a two-step path to `t` would exist. Hence

`e(P,T(t))=sum_{v in P} d^+(v)=8k+R_P`.                    (8.1)

On the other hand,

`e(P,T(t))=binom(k,2)-M_P + k|Q|-D_P`.                     (8.2)

Since

`|Q|=(8+q)-(2q-2)=10-q`,

a direct simplification gives

`binom(k,2)+k|Q|-8k=q-1`.                                  (8.3)

Combining (8.1)--(8.3) yields the exact unit-slack identity

`boxed: R_P + M_P + D_P = q-1`.                            (8.4)

Both `q=0` vertices belong to `P(t_1) cap P(t_2)` by (7.3). Equation (8.4), together with their universal adjacency, is a much smaller and more structured continuation problem than the original order-19 MILP.

No claim is made here that (8.4) alone eliminates `z=2`.
