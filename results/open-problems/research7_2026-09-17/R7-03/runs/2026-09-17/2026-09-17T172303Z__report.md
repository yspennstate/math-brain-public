## 4. B3 — prove something stronger: H1 has no degree-nine or q=1 vertex

The first strengthening is that the H1 bookkeeping forces much more than the raw identity shows.

### Lemma 4.1 — no degree-nine vertex occurs in H1

Under H1, every vertex has outdegree eight except for a unique vertex `t` of outdegree ten.

### Proof

Because `h=1` and `E=0`, there is exactly one vertex `t` with `r_t>=2`, and in fact `r_t=2`, so `d^+(t)=10`. Every other vertex initially has outdegree eight or nine.

Suppose `v` has outdegree nine. Since `L=0`, its source lower bound is tight:

`|U(v)|=1`.

Let `x` be the unique member of `U(v)`. Then `v in P(x)`. The target `x` cannot satisfy `q_x=0`, because the target-capacity lemma would make a nonempty `P(x)` impossible when `q_x=0`. Hence `q_x>=1`.

Since `C=0`, the target-capacity inequality at `x` is an equality. Equality forces every source in `P(x)` to have outdegree exactly eight, contradicting `d^+(v)=9`.

Thus no degree-nine vertex exists. QED.

Let `s` be the unique vertex with `q_s=0`. Since `q_s=r_s+a_s`, we have

`d^+(s)=8`, `a_s=0`.                                      (4.1)

In particular `s!=t`, and `s` is adjacent to all other 18 vertices.

The next strengthening eliminates target defect `q=1` altogether.

### Lemma 4.2 — no target has q=1 in H1

### Proof

Suppose `q_x=1`. By Lemma 4.1 the only possible positive `r` values are 2 at `t` and 0 elsewhere. Hence `x` has

`r_x=0`, `a_x=1`.

Thus `d^+(x)=8` and `x` has a unique nonneighbor `m`.

Because `C=0`, the capacity bound is tight:

`|P(x)|=2q_x-1=1`.

Write `P(x)={p}`. Equality in the fixed-target lemma forces `d^+(p)=8` and, since

`T(x)=N^+(x) union {m}`,

forces

`N^+(p)=T(x)\{p}`.                                        (4.2)

We split according to whether `p=m`.

#### Case A: p=m

Equation (4.2) becomes

`N^+(p)=N^+(x)=:A`.

Both `x` and `p` have outdegree eight, so `L=0` gives

`|U(x)|=|U(p)|=3`.

Because both `x` and `p` point to every vertex of `A`, no vertex of `A` can point back to either one. Therefore the two sources have the same exact two-step reachable set outside their common first neighborhood, and neither `x` nor `p` belongs to that second-step set. Consequently there are vertices `r,w` such that

`U(x)={p,r,w}`, `U(p)={x,r,w}`.                            (4.3)

Pick `r`. Then `x,p in P(r)`. The target `r` cannot be `s`, since `P(s)=empty`. Thus `q_r>=1`; because `C=0`, `P(r)` is a tournament. But `x` and `p=m` are nonadjacent, contradiction.

#### Case B: p!=m

Because `p in T(x)` but `p` is not the unique nonneighbor, necessarily

`x -> p`.

Put

`S=N^+(x)\{p}`.

Then (4.2) reads

`N^+(p)=S union {m}`.                                      (4.4)

We first claim `x in U(p)`. Indeed `p not->x`; every `y in S` satisfies `x->y` and hence, by orientedness, `y not->x`; and `m` is nonadjacent to `x`. Thus no outneighbor of `p` reaches `x` in one further step.

Since `d^+(p)=8` and `L=0`, write

`U(p)={x,r,w}`.

We claim `r,w in U(x)`. Consider `r`. Because `r in U(p)`, neither `p` nor any vertex of `S union {m}=N^+(p)` points to `r`. If `x->r`, then `r in S` (as `r!=p`), and (4.4) would imply `p->r`, contradiction. Hence `x not->r`. Also no member of `N^+(x)={p} union S` points to `r`. Thus `r in U(x)`. The same proof applies to `w`.

Therefore `x,p in P(r)`. Again `r!=s`, so equality holds in the target-capacity lemma at `r`. Let

`Q_r=T(r)\P(r)`.

Equality says, for every source `u in P(r)`,

`N^+(u)=Q_r union O_{P(r)}(u)`,                            (4.5)

where `O_{P(r)}(u)` is the set of outneighbors of `u` inside the tournament `P(r)`.

Since `x in P(r)`, equation (4.5) gives `Q_r subset N^+(x)`, so the nonneighbor `m` of `x` does not lie in `Q_r`. But `p in P(r)` and (4.4) gives `m in N^+(p)`. Equation (4.5) therefore forces `m in P(r)`.

Now both `x` and `m` lie in the tournament `P(r)`, contradicting that `x,m` are nonadjacent.

Both cases are impossible. QED.

### Corollary 4.3 — exact H1 degree and missing-edge structure

Under H1:

1. `s` has outdegree 8, no nonneighbors, and indegree 10;
2. `t` has outdegree 10, no nonneighbors, and indegree 8;
3. each of the other 17 vertices has outdegree 8, exactly two nonneighbors, and indegree 8;
4. the missing-edge graph is a 2-factor on those 17 ordinary vertices and has no edge incident to `s` or `t`.

### Proof

Lemma 4.1 gives total arc count

`18*8+10=154`.

There are `binom(19,2)=171` unordered pairs, so exactly

`171-154=17`

pairs are missing, and therefore

`sum_x a_x=34`.                                           (4.6)

We already know `a_s=0`. The high vertex `t` has `r_t=2`, while every one of the remaining 17 ordinary vertices has `r=0`. Since `s` is the unique `q=0` vertex, each ordinary vertex has `a_x>=1`; Lemma 4.2 strengthens this to `a_x>=2`.

Those 17 vertices already contribute at least 34 to (4.6). Hence equality is forced everywhere:

`a_t=0`, and `a_x=2` for every ordinary vertex `x`.        (4.7)

The indegree statements follow from

`d^-(x)=18-a_x-d^+(x)`.

The 17 missing pairs are therefore supported entirely on the 17 ordinary vertices, each of missing degree two, hence form a 2-factor. QED.

This is the first key recovery gain: H1 is not a diffuse one-unit perturbation. It is an Eulerian 17-vertex core plus an exact complete asymmetric pair `s,t` carrying the entire degree imbalance.

## 5. B12 — find what is conserved: reversal swaps the two defects exactly

The degree imbalance in Corollary 4.3 is localized:

`d^+(t)-d^-(t)=+2`,

`d^+(s)-d^-(s)=-2`,

and every ordinary vertex has zero imbalance.               (5.1)

The useful invariant is that reversing all arcs preserves the H1 shape and swaps `s` with `t`.

### Lemma 5.1 — exact H1 self-duality under arc reversal

Let `D^R` be obtained by reversing every arc. Then `D^R` is again a 19-vertex no-Seymour digraph satisfying H1, with the roles of `s` and `t` interchanged.

Moreover, in `D`:

- `P(s)=empty`;
- `|P(x)|=3` and `P(x)` is a directed 3-cycle for every `x!=s`;
- `U(t)=empty`;
- `|U(v)|=3` and `U(v)` is a directed 3-cycle for every `v!=t`.

### Proof

From Corollary 4.3, the reverse outdegrees are

- `d^+_{D^R}(s)=10`,
- `d^+_{D^R}(t)=8`,
- `d^+_{D^R}(x)=8` for every ordinary `x`.

The missing pairs are unchanged. Thus in the reverse graph the unique `q=0` vertex is `t`, while the unique degree-ten vertex is `s`.

Now use the exact path-reversal identities

`U_{D^R}(x)=P_D(x)`,                                       (5.2)

`P_{D^R}(x)=U_D(x)`.                                       (5.3)

In `D`, `C=0`. Since `q_s=0`, the capacity lemma gives `P_D(s)=empty`. Every other target has `q_x=2` by Corollary 4.3, so

`|P_D(x)|=3` for every `x!=s`.                             (5.4)

Also `L=0`, so

`U_D(t)=empty`, and `|U_D(v)|=3` for every `v!=t`.          (5.5)

Equations (5.2), (5.4), and the reverse outdegrees show that in `D^R` the source lower bounds are all tight. Equations (5.3), (5.5), and the reverse `q` values show that all reverse target-capacity bounds are tight. In particular the reverse graph satisfies the same H1 slack pattern.

It is also a no-Seymour graph: for `s`, reverse outdegree is 10 and `|U_{D^R}(s)|=|P_D(s)|=0`, so the exact reverse second neighborhood has size `18-10=8<10`; for every other vertex, the reverse outdegree is 8 and its reverse unreachable set has size three, so its exact reverse second neighborhood has size `18-8-3=7<8`.

Finally, capacity equality with `q=2` makes every nonempty `P` a three-vertex tournament whose every vertex has one internal outneighbor; hence every `P_D(x)`, `x!=s`, is a directed 3-cycle. Applying the same statement in `D^R` and using (5.3) shows every `U_D(v)`, `v!=t`, is a directed 3-cycle. QED.

This is the conserved one-defect flow sought in the stuck diagnosis: the unique positive degree defect and unique zero-`q` defect are not independent. They are a dual source/target pair exchanged by reversal, while the remaining 17 vertices are exactly balanced.

## 6. B15 — break symmetry at the defect pair and glue the ordinary core

The final step uses the distinguished complete pair `s,t` only to identify the 17 ordinary vertices on which the zero-slack routing mechanism survives intact.

Call a vertex **ordinary** if it is neither `s` nor `t`. By Corollary 4.3, every ordinary vertex has two nonneighbors, both ordinary.

### Lemma 6.1 — missing-neighbor routing survives on every ordinary vertex

Let `v` be ordinary and let `m` be one of its two nonneighbors. Then

`m notin U(v)`, and `m -> u` for every `u in U(v)`;         (6.1)

`m notin P(v)`, and `p -> m` for every `p in P(v)`.         (6.2)

Consequently

`P(v) subset N^+(v)`, and `U(v) subset N^-(v)`.             (6.3)

### Proof

Take `x in U(v)`. Then `v in P(x)`. The target `x` cannot equal `s`, because `P(s)=empty`. Hence Lemma 5.1 says `P(x)` is a directed 3-cycle and the target-capacity equality at `x` holds with `q_x=2`.

If `m in P(x)`, then the tournament `P(x)` would make `m` adjacent to `v`, contradicting that `m` is a nonneighbor of `v`. Thus `m notin P(x)`.

Write

`Q_x=T(x)\P(x)`.

For the source `v in P(x)`, equality gives

`N^+(v)=Q_x union {sigma_x(v)}`,                            (6.4)

where `sigma_x(v)` is the unique outneighbor of `v` inside the directed cycle `P(x)`.

Because `m` is nonadjacent to `v`, it is not in `N^+(v)`. We already know it is not in `P(x)`, so (6.4) implies `m notin Q_x`; hence `m notin T(x)`. If `x!=m`, the definition of `T(x)` therefore forces

`m -> x`.                                                   (6.5)

Suppose for contradiction that `m in U(v)`. The set `U(v)` is a directed 3-cycle by Lemma 5.1. Applying (6.5) to the other two members of this cycle makes `m` point to both of them, impossible in a directed 3-cycle. Therefore `m notin U(v)`. Now every `u in U(v)` is distinct from `m`, so (6.5) gives the first line (6.1).

Apply the just-proved statement to the reverse graph `D^R`. Ordinary vertices remain ordinary under the interchange of `s` and `t`, and missing pairs are unchanged. By (5.2),

`U_{D^R}(v)=P_D(v)`.

Therefore `m notin P_D(v)`, and `m ->_{D^R} p` for every `p in P_D(v)`. Reversing the arcs gives (6.2).

For (6.3), let `p in P(v)`. By (6.2), `p` is not a nonneighbor of `v`; also `p in P(v)` means `p not->v`. Hence orientedness forces `v->p`. Thus `P(v) subset N^+(v)`. The proof for `U(v) subset N^-(v)` is dual: a member of `U(v)` is not a missing neighbor by (6.1), and `v` cannot point to an unreachable target, so that target points to `v`. QED.

The local equality structure can now be glued exactly as in the former zero-slack branch, but crucially only at an ordinary vertex.

### Theorem 6.2 — the H1 branch is impossible

There is no 19-vertex minimum-outdegree-eight SSNC counterexample satisfying

`z=1, h=1, E=L=C=0`.

### Proof

Choose any ordinary vertex `v`; there are 17 of them. By Lemma 5.1, `P(v)` is a directed 3-cycle. Label it

`P(v)={p,sigma,tau}`

with orientation

`p -> sigma -> tau -> p`.                                  (6.6)

By Lemma 6.1, `P(v) subset N^+(v)`. Put

`A=N^+(v)\P(v)`.

Then `|A|=5`. Let `H(v)` be the two missing neighbors of `v`. Since

`T(v)=N^+(v) union H(v)`

and `P(v) subset N^+(v)`, the equality formula at target `v` for the source `p` gives

`N^+(p)=A union H(v) union {sigma}`.                        (6.7)

Because `p in P(v)`, by definition

`v in U(p)`.                                                (6.8)

Every source in a positive target equality set has outdegree eight, so `p!=t`. Lemma 5.1 therefore says `U(p)` is a directed 3-cycle.

Inside this directed triangle, besides `v`, exactly one vertex is an outneighbor of `v` and exactly one is an inneighbor of `v`. We identify the former.

The outneighbors of `v` are

`P(v) union A`.

Every member of `A` is a direct outneighbor of `p` by (6.7), so none can belong to `U(p)`. The vertex `sigma` is also a direct outneighbor of `p`, and `p` itself is excluded from its own unreachable set. The only remaining outneighbor of `v` that can lie in `U(p)` is therefore `tau`.

Hence

`tau in U(p)`.

Let `w` be the third member of `U(p)`. Since `v->tau` and `U(p)` is a directed 3-cycle, its orientation is

`v -> tau -> w -> v`.                                      (6.9)

In particular

`w in N^-(v)` and `tau -> w`.                              (6.10)

Now apply the same target equality at `v` to the source `tau`. In the cycle (6.6), the unique outneighbor of `tau` inside `P(v)` is `p`. Thus

`N^+(tau)=A union H(v) union {p}`.                          (6.11)

But the right side of (6.11) is disjoint from `N^-(v)`: every vertex of `A union {p}` is an outneighbor of `v`, while the two members of `H(v)` are nonadjacent to `v`.

Since `w in N^-(v)`, equation (6.11) says `tau not->w`, contradicting (6.10).

Therefore H1 is impossible. QED.
