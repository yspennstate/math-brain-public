## 1. A real gap in the first draft, and why it is avoidable

The first draft's distinct-prime product proposition used the following step. If the row-coset sequence was constant, it set

`W=(y^q-1)T`

and asserted that the tiling identity implied `A(x)W=0`, after which it tried to compare coefficients.

That implication is not justified for a bi-infinite tiling indicator. From

`A(x)B(y)T=1`

one only obtains

`A(x)B(y)(y^q-1)T=0`.

Cancelling the finite mask `B(y)` on arbitrary bi-infinite coefficient arrays is invalid in general: finite convolution operators can have nonzero bi-infinite kernel elements (the elementary alternating sequence is already annihilated by `1+y`). Therefore the equal-prime-to-distinct-prime formal-series generalization should not be used as written.

For the actual six-cell problem this gap is unnecessary. The surviving product has sizes exactly `3 x 2`, and a direct fibre argument proves periodicity with no formal-series cancellation and no extension of Khetan's product proposition.

## 2. Method B25: direct fibre theorem for the actual 3 x 2 product

### Proposition 2.1 (source-specific product periodicity)

Let `A,B subset Z` satisfy:

- `A` is a complete residue system modulo `3` and `gcd(A-A)=1`;
- `B={b_0,b_1}` with `|b_1-b_0|=1` (equivalently, `B` is a complete residue system modulo `2` with `gcd(B-B)=1`).

If `T subset Z^2` tiles `Z^2` by `A x B`, then `T` has a nonzero coordinate period. More precisely, either `(3,0)` or `(0,2)` is a period.

### Proof

For every integer `y`, let

`S_y={x in Z : (x,y) in T}`

be the row fibre. For every integer `x`, let

`T_x={y in Z : (x,y) in T}`

be the column fibre. From the unique tiling identity, for each `n in Z`,

`R_n := disjoint_union_{b in B} S_{n-b}`

is an `A`-tiling of `Z`; and for each `m in Z`,

`C_m := disjoint_union_{a in A} T_{m-a}`

is a `B`-tiling of `Z`.

By the prime-cardinality one-dimensional tiling theorem used by Khetan (his Lemma 3.3), together with `gcd(A-A)=1`, there exists `r_n in Z/3Z` such that

`R_n = 3Z+r_n`.

For the two-point tile no external theorem is needed. Translating `B`, its tiling equation is

`1_C(k)+1_C(k-1)=1`.

Hence `1_C(k)=1-1_C(k-1)` and the indicator alternates. Thus every `B`-tiling is one parity class, so there exists `s_m in Z/2Z` with

`C_m = 2Z+s_m`.

We now split only according to whether the parity sequence `s_m` is constant.

#### Case 1: `s_m` is constant

Suppose `s_m=s` for all `m`. If `(x,y) in T`, then for every `a in A`,

`y in T_x subset C_{x+a}=2Z+s`.

Hence every anchor row satisfies `y = s (mod 2)`.

Fix a nonempty row `S_y` and choose either `b in B`. Put `n=y+b`. In

`R_n = disjoint_union_{b' in B} S_{n-b'}`,

any nonempty summand must have row index congruent to `s mod 2`. Since the two elements of `B` are distinct modulo `2`, exactly one `b'` can have `n-b' = s (mod 2)`, namely the chosen `b`. Therefore

`S_y=R_n=3Z+r_n`.

Every nonempty row is a coset of `3Z`, and every empty row is trivially invariant by horizontal translation. Thus

`(3,0)+T=T`.

#### Case 2: `s_m` is not constant

Because `s_m` takes values in the two-element group `Z/2Z`, nonconstancy means that both residues occur. Therefore every row is nonempty. Indeed, given `y`, choose `m` with `s_m = y (mod 2)`. Then `y in C_m`, and since

`C_m = disjoint_union_{a in A} T_{m-a}`,

some anchor lies in row `y`.

Now fix `y`. Since `S_y` is nonempty and

`S_y subset R_{y+b_0}=3Z+r_{y+b_0}`,
`S_y subset R_{y+b_1}=3Z+r_{y+b_1}`,

these two residue classes must coincide. Hence

`r_{y+b_0}=r_{y+b_1}`.

Because `|b_1-b_0|=1` and `y` was arbitrary, `r_n` is constant, say `r_n=r` for all `n`.

It follows that every anchor column is congruent to `r mod 3`: if `(x,y) in T`, then for either `b in B`,

`x in S_y subset R_{y+b}=3Z+r`.

Fix a nonempty column `T_x`, so `x=r (mod 3)`, and choose any `a in A`. Put `m=x+a`. In

`C_m = disjoint_union_{a' in A} T_{m-a'}`,

any nonempty summand has column index `m-a' = r (mod 3)`. Since `A` contains exactly one element in each residue class modulo `3`, the unique `a'` satisfying this congruence is `a`. Therefore

`T_x=C_m=2Z+s_m`.

Thus every nonempty column is a parity class and every empty column is trivially invariant under vertical translation. Hence

`(0,2)+T=T`.

The two cases exhaust all possibilities. QED.

## 3. Method B11: dualize the at-most-one-rich branch completely

The first draft said that Khetan's at-most-one-rich argument is cardinality-free once the dilation equations are available. The following gives a direct proof of the key last step, reducing dependence on the exact wording of Khetan's irrational-phase lemma and periodic-cylinder theorem.

Let `X` be the orbit closure of an `F`-tiling, `mu` an ergodic invariant probability measure on `X`, and `f=1_{x(0)=1}` the origin-cylinder indicator in `L^2(X,mu)`. Let `nu` be its spectral measure for the `Z^2` action.

For `alpha` coprime to `6`, define

`P_alpha(xi)=sum_{a in F} chi_{alpha a}(xi)`.

The dilation tiling equations imply that, away from the trivial character, `nu` is carried by every zero set `Z(P_alpha)`. Put

`Z_* = intersection_{gcd(alpha,6)=1} Z(P_alpha)`.

Thus

`supp(nu) subset {0} union Z_*`.

Let `Delta` be a finite character-kernel cover of the nontrivial spectral support as in Khetan/Bhattacharya. Suppose at most one member `g_0 in Delta` is rich. For each `h in Delta\{g_0}`, non-richness means

`ker chi_h intersect Z_*`

is finite. Hence

`supp(nu) subset ker chi_{g_0} union S`

for a finite set `S` (the trivial character is already in every kernel). If there is no nontrivial spectral support, `f` is translation-invariant and the conclusion is immediate.

Some points of `S` may have `chi_{g_0}(s)` a root of unity. Let `K` be the least common multiple of their finite orders (take `K=1` if there are none) and set

`g=K g_0`.

All torsion atoms are now absorbed into `ker chi_g`. Removing them from `S`, we therefore have

`supp(nu) subset ker chi_g union S'`,

where `S'` is finite and every eigenvalue `chi_g(s)`, `s in S'`, is **not** a root of unity.

Group points of `S'` that give the same `g`-eigenvalue. The spectral theorem gives an orthogonal decomposition

`f = f_0 + phi_1+...+phi_m`,

where

`U_g f_0=f_0`,
`U_g phi_j = gamma_j phi_j`,

and `gamma_1,...,gamma_m` are pairwise distinct non-roots-of-unity on the unit circle.

The remaining claim follows from the finite-alphabet recurrence lemma below.

## 4. Method B19: finite-state recurrence rigidity

### Lemma 4.1 (finite alphabet plus finite non-torsion spectrum forces invariance)

Let `(U^n)_{n in Z}` be an invertible measure-preserving `Z`-action and let `f` take values in a finite set. Suppose in `L^2`

`f=f_0+sum_{j=1}^m phi_j`,

with

`Uf_0=f_0`,
`U phi_j=gamma_j phi_j`,

where the `gamma_j` are distinct nonzero complex numbers and none is a root of unity. Then every `phi_j=0` almost surely. In particular, `Uf=f`.

### Proof

Choose representatives and discard the countable union of null sets needed so that all translated eigenfunction identities hold pointwise for every integer `n`. Fix a remaining point `x` and write

`a_n=f(U^n x)=f_0(x)+sum_{j=1}^m gamma_j^n phi_j(x)`.

The sequence `a_n` takes values in a finite alphabet. It also satisfies the order `d=m+1` linear recurrence whose characteristic polynomial is

`Q(z)=(z-1) product_{j=1}^m (z-gamma_j)`.

Both the leading and constant coefficients of `Q` are nonzero. Therefore an actual length-`d` state

`(a_n,...,a_{n+d-1})`

determines uniquely both the next and the previous entry through the recurrence.

There are only finitely many possible length-`d` states because the alphabet is finite. Along the bi-infinite orbit, some two states therefore agree: for some `n_1<n_2`,

`(a_{n_1},...,a_{n_1+d-1})=(a_{n_2},...,a_{n_2+d-1})`.

Forward and backward determinism imply

`a_{n+L}=a_n` for every `n in Z`,

where `L=n_2-n_1>0`.

Subtracting the spectral formulas gives

`sum_{j=1}^m phi_j(x)(gamma_j^L-1) gamma_j^n=0`

for every `n`. Taking `n=0,...,m-1` gives a Vandermonde system. Since the `gamma_j` are distinct, the Vandermonde matrix is invertible, so

`phi_j(x)(gamma_j^L-1)=0`

for every `j`. No `gamma_j` is a root of unity, hence `gamma_j^L != 1`, and therefore `phi_j(x)=0` for all `j`.

This holds off a null set, proving the lemma. QED.

### Corollary 4.2 (the at-most-one-rich branch produces a periodic tiling)

Apply Lemma 4.1 to the origin-cylinder indicator `f`, whose alphabet is `{0,1}`. We obtain

`U_g f=f` almost surely for a nonzero `g in Z^2`.

Translations commute, so for every `v in Z^2`, the coordinate-cylinder function `U_v f` is also `g`-invariant. Intersecting the corresponding full-measure sets over the countable set of coordinates, for `mu`-almost every configuration `x in X` we have

`x(v+g)=x(v)` for every `v in Z^2`.

Thus `X` contains a one-periodic `F`-tiling. No separate transfer theorem is needed at this point.

## 5. Rigorous form of the C6 phase factorization

The first draft's `zeta^{v_j}` notation can be made unambiguous as follows. On a rich component of `ker chi_{nh}`, with `h` primitive, choose the component parameterization

`xi=rho h+t h^perp`,

where `rho` is rational and chosen so that `n rho ||h||^2` is an integer. The common section coefficient is

`exp(2 pi i rho <a,h>)`.

For two transverse families of parallel lines, their intersection points have affine product coordinates

`x_{ij}=x_*+alpha_j h+beta_i v`.

Therefore

`exp(2 pi i rho <x_{ij},h>)`
`= lambda_i z_j`,

where

`lambda_i=exp(2 pi i rho(<x_*,h>+beta_i<v,h>))`

and

`z_j=exp(2 pi i rho alpha_j ||h||^2)`.

In a `C6` incidence, after relabelling row `i` misses column `i`; cancellation of the nonzero `lambda_i` gives

`z_2+z_3=0`,
`z_1+z_3=0`,
`z_1+z_2=0`,

an immediate contradiction. Thus the earlier C6 exclusion survives with a literal exponential factorization and no ambiguous real exponent notation.
