## 5. B12 — find what is conserved: an exact edge likelihood ratio

### 5.1 Idea before proof

The dyadic transport matrix remembers degree scales but hides the exact simple-graph capacity of each edge. Instead sample a host incidence uniformly and compare its law with the independent size-biased laws of its two endpoints. The Radon–Nikodym derivative should expose exactly how much capacity an edge with endpoint degrees `(d_a,d_b)` consumes.

Let `nu` be uniform on the `E` edges:

```text
nu(a,b)=1/E    for ab in E(J).
```

Define the endpoint size-biased laws

```text
mu_A(a)=d_a/E,
mu_B(b)=d_b/E.
```

Set

```text
L(a,b)=E/(d_a d_b)    on edges,
L(a,b)=0              off edges.                         (5.1)
```

### Lemma 5.1 — exact likelihood-ratio identity

```text
d nu / d(mu_A tensor mu_B)=L,
E_{mu_A tensor mu_B}[L]=1.                               (5.2)
```

#### Proof

For an edge `ab`, the independent endpoint product law has mass

```text
d_a d_b/E^2.
```

Multiplying by `E/(d_ad_b)` gives exactly `1/E`, the uniform-edge mass. Off the edge set both sides are zero. Summation gives the expectation identity. `QED`

This immediately gives a universal tail inequality for the normalized degree product

```text
U(ab):=d_a d_b/E.                                       (5.3)
```

### Corollary 5.2 — double-hub mass is universally small

For every fixed `M>=1`,

```text
nu{U>=M} <= 1/M.                                        (5.4)
```

Indeed, on `{U>=M}` one has `L=1/U<=1/M`, so

```text
nu(S)=integral_S L d(mu_A tensor mu_B)<=1/M.
```

Thus no counterexample can hide a fixed positive fraction of its incidences at arbitrarily *high* endpoint-degree product.

### 5.2 Normalized-incidence operator

Let

```text
N_ab = 1/sqrt(d_a d_b)   if ab is an edge,
N_ab = 0                 otherwise.
```

Then

```text
Xi(J):=||N||_F^2
     =sum_{ab in E(J)}1/(d_a d_b)
     =E_nu[L].                                             (5.5)
```

The operator norm is exactly one for every nonempty `J`. The vectors `sqrt(d_A)` and `sqrt(d_B)` exhibit singular value one; Cauchy–Schwarz gives

```text
|x^TNy|
 <= [sum_{ab} x_a^2/d_a]^(1/2)
    [sum_{ab} y_b^2/d_b]^(1/2)
 =||x||_2||y||_2.
```

Hence

```text
Xi(J)=stable-rank(N).                                    (5.6)
```

Also

```text
Xi(J)
 =E_{mu_A tensor mu_B}[L^2]
 >= E[L]^2
 =1.                                                       (5.7)
```

**B12 outcome:** the exact invariant is the normalized-incidence likelihood ratio. It proves a universal `1/M` high-product tail and identifies `Xi` as a genuine spectral/stable-rank measure of the unresolved geometry.

## 6. B7 — take the limit: every fixed product-scale window is actually solvable

### 6.1 Dyadic product bands

Use the recovered dyadic bins

```text
R_i=2^i r_0d^2,
S_j=2^j s_0d,
A_i={a:R_i<=d_a<2R_i},
B_j={b:S_j<=d_b<2S_j}.
```

For a cell `(i,j)` define

```text
sigma_ij=floor(log_2(R_iS_j/E)).                         (6.1)
```

Since `R_iS_j=2^(i+j)R_0S_0`,

```text
sigma_ij=i+j+c_E                                         (6.2)
```

for an instance-dependent integer `c_E`. Therefore any band of `L` consecutive values of `sigma` meets at most `L` cells in each row and at most `L` cells in each column.

### Theorem 6.1 — fixed normalized degree-product window theorem

Fix constants

```text
0<delta<M<infinity,
eta>0.
```

If along a subsequence

```text
nu{delta <= d_a d_b/E <= M} >= eta,                      (6.3)
```

then `J_p` contains a simple cycle of length `Omega(d)` a.a.s. along that subsequence.

#### Proof

For an edge in cell `(i,j)`,

```text
R_iS_j <= d_ad_b < 4R_iS_j.                              (6.4)
```

Thus every edge in (6.3) lies in a cell whose base product satisfies

```text
delta/4 < R_iS_j/E <= M.                                 (6.5)
```

Those cells lie in one fixed-width `sigma`-band `S`, of width at most

```text
L=3+ceil(log_2(M/delta)).                                (6.6)
```

The band has total transport mass at least `eta`.

The subtle point is that the band may represent only a vanishing fraction of some rows or columns. Perform a weighted two-sided peeling with

```text
tau=eta/8.
```

Starting from `S`, repeatedly delete a current row `i` if its current surviving mass is below `tau P_i`, or a current column `j` if its current surviving mass is below `tau Q_j`. All row deletions together remove less than `tau sum_i P_i<=tau`; all column deletions remove less than `tau`. Therefore the terminal band `T` retains mass

```text
m_T>=eta-2tau=3eta/4.                                    (6.7)
```

Every surviving row and column obeys

```text
r_i>=tau P_i,
c_j>=tau Q_j.                                            (6.8)
```

Normalize `T` to a probability law. Every conditional row and column then has support size at most `L`; with `C=max(2,L)`,

```text
H_T(J|I)+H_T(I|J)<=2 log C.                              (6.9)
```

For the restricted conditionals

```text
tilde alpha_ij=P_ij/r_i,
tilde beta_ij=P_ij/c_j,
```

Markov's inequality applied to

```text
-log(tilde alpha_ij tilde beta_ij)
```

shows that at least half of the terminal mass lies in cells with

```text
tilde alpha_ij tilde beta_ij>=C^(-4).
```

Because both factors are at most one, each is at least `C^(-4)`. Combining with (6.8), every such cell satisfies the **global** balanced-cell inequalities

```text
alpha_ij=P_ij/P_i>=tau C^(-4),
beta_ij =P_ij/Q_j>=tau C^(-4).                            (6.10)
```

The good cells carry original transport mass at least `3eta/8`. There are only `O((log n)^2)` occupied dyadic cells, so one good cell has

```text
e_ij >= c eta E/(log n)^2
     >= c' eta n d/(log n)^2
     -> infinity.                                         (6.11)
```

The previously proved balanced-cell peeling lemma now leaves more than half those edges in a bipartite subgraph with fixed degree ratios and sidewise lower degrees `Omega(d^2)` and `Omega(d)`. Its order tends to infinity by (6.11). The previous bounded-ratio cross-core theorem then gives an `Omega(d)` simple cycle after percolation at the **source probability** `(1+epsilon)/d`. `QED`

Theorem 6.1 is stronger than the previous bounded-entropy criterion in a direction that matters here: global conditional entropy may diverge, yet any fixed positive mass trapped in one finite **product** band is enough to recover a bounded-ratio cell.

### Theorem 6.2 — product-scale escape

For every counterexample sequence to the current cross-core long-cycle statement,

```text
U=d_a d_b/E -> 0
```

in uniform-edge probability.

#### Proof

If not, there are fixed `delta,eta>0` and a subsequence with

```text
nu{U>=delta}>=eta.
```

Choose `M>max(1,2/eta)`. By the B12 tail bound,

```text
nu{U>=M}<=1/M<eta/2,
```

hence

```text
nu{delta<=U<M}>=eta/2.
```

Theorem 6.1 then solves that subsequence, contradiction. `QED`

Equivalently,

```text
log_2 U -> -infinity
```

in probability. In the compactified extended line, the only possible limiting law is the point mass at `-infinity`.

**B7 outcome:** the entire finite product-scale interior is eliminated. A counterexample sequence cannot merely have more and more degree scales; a typical edge must have endpoint degree product asymptotically negligible compared with the *total* cross-edge mass.

## 7. B29 — add the points at infinity: only the low-product face survives

### 7.1 Compactified log-degree geometry

Write, schematically,

```text
x=log_2(d_a/(r_0d^2)),
y=log_2(d_b/(s_0d)).
```

Then the centered product coordinate is

```text
x+y-log_2(E/(r_0s_0d^3))
 =log_2(d_ad_b/E)+O(1).                                  (7.1)
```

A fixed-width strip transverse to `(1,-1)` is exactly a fixed product-scale band. Theorem 6.1 solves every strip that carries nonvanishing incidence mass. The B12 `1/M` inequality rules out escape of nonvanishing mass to the high-product boundary. Thus every hypothetical counterexample sequence escapes entirely to the **low-product boundary face** of this compactified plane.

This corrects the working intuition from the previous report: a hub or complete-bipartite high-product boundary is not the remaining enemy. Such concentrated geometries are already handled by the bounded-ratio theorem. The only unresolved boundary is the opposite one.

### 7.2 Divergent stable rank is forced

Since `U->0` in edge probability,

```text
L=1/U=E/(d_ad_b)->infinity
```

in edge probability. Therefore, for every fixed `T>0`,

```text
Xi(J)=E_nu[L]
     >=T nu{L>=T}
     ->T.
```

Letting `T` be arbitrary gives

```text
boxed: Xi(J)=sum_edges 1/(d_ad_b)=stable-rank(N) -> infinity.   (7.2)
```

Thus any remaining counterexample sequence must have an increasingly high-rank normalized incidence operator, not a low-rank hub geometry.

### 7.3 A simple-graph capacity inequality explains the boundary

For the dyadic cells,

```text
|A_i| <= E P_i/R_i,
|B_j| <= E Q_j/S_j.
```

Since the graph is simple,

```text
E P_ij=e_ij
 <=|A_i||B_j|
 <=E^2 P_iQ_j/(R_iS_j).
```

Therefore

```text
P_ij/(P_iQ_j) <= E/(R_iS_j).                             (7.3)
```

The right side is the reciprocal product scale. At finite/high product scale the transport density relative to the independent endpoint law has finite capacity, and Theorem 6.1 extracts a balanced cell. Only at the low-product boundary can that admissible density become arbitrarily large while the transport simultaneously diffuses over more and more row and column scales.

**B29 outcome:** the compactification has only one unsolved face. The preceding generic phrase “multiscale irregularity” is now replaced by a precise necessary regime.
