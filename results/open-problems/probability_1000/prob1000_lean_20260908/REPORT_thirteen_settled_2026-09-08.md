## 1. Aldous & Fill, Open Problem 7.27 — refuted

*Reversible Markov Chains and Random Walks on Graphs*, Chapter 7 (dated 31 January 1994), p. 259:

> Is it true that, over arc-transitive graphs excluding the n-cycles, τ\* = O(n log n), τ₂ = O(n)
> and τ\*/(2τ₀) = 1 + o(1)?

The sentence immediately before it is what makes this answerable:

> Example 7.14 shows that one can invent vertex-transitive graphs which mimic the n-cycle, but it
> is not clear whether such arc-transitive graphs exist.

**Counterexample.** C_m[K̄₂] for odd m ≥ 5: the m-cycle with every vertex split into two
*non-adjacent* twins. n = 2m, 4-regular, connected, not a cycle.

Their Example 7.14 is the ladder Z_m × Z₂ with generators (1,0), (−1,0), (0,1), and it is only
vertex-transitive for a specific reason — its rungs are distinguishable from its cycle edges, which
is the whole content of that example (E_v T_w differs by edge type). Deleting the rungs removes
exactly that obstruction and changes nothing else. The twin-swap maps are automorphisms because
adjacency depends only on the base coordinate.

**All three claims fail.** Writing g(j) for the mean hitting time across cycle-distance j:

| quantity | value | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| hitting time, fibre distance j | j(m−j) + m | |
| twin hitting time | 2m | |
| τ\* | 2⌊m²/4⌋ + 2m ~ n²/8 | quadratic, not O(n log n) |
| τ₀ | (m²−1)/6 + m | |
| τ₂ | 1/(1 − cos 2π/m) ~ n²/8π² | quadratic, not O(n) |
| τ\*/(2τ₀) | → 3/2 | not 1 + o(1) |

τ₂ is the m-cycle's own relaxation time because A(C_m[K̄₂]) = A(C_m) ⊗ J₂, with eigenvalues
4cos(2πk/m) and 0.

**Verification.** (a) Exact hitting times by linear algebra at m = 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 21 — all five
formulas exact at every size. (b) Arc-transitivity mechanically: the subgroup generated by the
rotation, one reflection and one fibre swap has order 2^m·2m, and its orbit of a single arc is all
8m arcs, at m = 5, 7, 9. (c) g(j) = j(m−j)+m satisfies 2g(j) = 2 + g(j−1) + g(j+1) identically and
t = 1 + g(1) = 2m, so it solves the hitting system; uniqueness for a finite irreducible chain does
the rest. (d) `P0208.lean`, nine theorems — the harmonic equation, the twin value, the maximum
⌊m²/4⌋, the classical cycle sum 6Σ = m(m²−1), τ₀, and the two asymptotic identities. Lean caught
two of my own algebra slips in the ratio and growth statements.

Not formalised: uniqueness of the solution to (I−P)h = 1, and the tensor eigenvalue computation.
Both are standard and both were checked numerically.

## 2. Aldous & Janson, Open Problem 14 — the proposed rate is wrong

*The Critical Beta-splitting Random Tree II* (arXiv:2303.02529), pp. 54–55:

> Prove that L_n grows roughly like n log⁴ n

where L_n = E[drawn length of DTCS(n)], drawn length defined by their recursion (73),
l(χ_m) = l(χ_i) + l(χ_{m−i}) + 2 + |h(χ_{m−i}) − h(χ_i)|.

**The drawn length is Θ(n).** Two steps:

1. For *every* full binary tree, drawn length ≤ the sum of all leaf depths. The increment
   2 + |h_R − h_L| is at most n_L + n_R because a full binary tree with k leaves has height ≤ k−1.
   Machine-checked (`P0340.lean`), along with the exact decomposition
   drawn(T) = Σ internal subtree heights + height(T).
2. Their own Theorem 3: E[height of a random leaf of DTCS(n)] ~ (1/2ζ(2)) log²n = 3π⁻²log²n.

So E[drawn length] ≤ n·E[leaf depth] ~ (3/π²)·n log²n = o(n log⁴n).

**Where the heuristic broke — one factor of m.** Their own text states that the mean number of
subclades of size m equals (n/m)·a(n,m), with a(m) = 6h_{m−1}/(π²(m−1)); that is
count(m) ~ (6/π²)·n log m/m². Heuristic (75) carries m⁻¹log m where m⁻²log m belongs — it used
n·a(n,m) as the clade count instead of (n/m)·a(n,m). With the wrong count the sum is Σ log³m/m,
which diverges like log⁴n and reproduces the conjecture exactly; with the right count it is
Σ log³m/m², which converges. The one-line check that catches it: Σ_m count(m) = n−1 exactly, so a
count of order n log m/m is impossible — it would sum to n log²n.

**Simulation, sampler validated first.** L_n/n = 4.71, 4.78, 4.84, 4.83, 4.86 at n = 10³ … 4×10⁵ —
flat across a 400× range of n while log⁴n grows 12×. Before believing that, the sampler was checked
against the paper's own formulas: clade counts match (n/m)a(m) to four figures (m=2: 0.3059 vs
3/π² = 0.3040; m=3: 0.1521 vs 0.1520), mean leaf depth matches 3π⁻²log²n in shape and constant, and
E[h(T_m)]/log²m is flat near 0.8, consistent with their still-open Open Problem 4.

**A reading trap.** The PDF text extractor renders log² as "log2", so Theorem 3 reads "3π⁻²log2 n"
and looks like log base 2. It is log *squared* — their own heuristic on p. 13 derives the integral
as (3/π²)log²n explicitly. Read as base 2, the leaf depth becomes linear in log n and the
refutation disappears.

## 3. Hasebe, Ueda & Wang, Problem 4.7 — answered, no

*Log-unimodality for free positive multiplicative Brownian motion* (arXiv:2009.13848):

> if ν is a probability measure supported on [α, β] such that 0 < α < β, does there exist a number
> D ≥ 0 (depending on ν) such that σ_t ⊠ ν is log-unimodal for all t ≥ D?

**No.** ν = (δ_{1/10} + δ_{10})/2 on [1/10, 10] is log-unimodal for no t ≥ 5π.

Their Lemma 4.1(3) says σ_t ⊠ ν is log-unimodal iff for every R ∈ (0,π) the equation

  (sin R/R) ∫ rξ/(1 + r²ξ² − 2rξ cos R) dν(ξ) = 1/t

has at most two solutions. With r = e^u and atoms e^{±a}, a = log 10, the integral is *exactly*
F_R(u) = (K_R(u−a) + K_R(u+a))/2, K_R(s) = 1/(2(cosh s − cos R)). Take the unique R ∈ [π/2, π) with
R/sin R = t/10 — it exists precisely when t ≥ 5π — and the right side is exactly 1/10. Then

  F_R(0) = 1/(2(cosh a − cos R)) ≤ 1/(2 cosh a) = 10/101 < 1/10,
  F_R(±a) ≥ 1/8 > 1/10,  F_R → 0 at ±∞,

so the equation has four roots. Both bounds are uniform over the whole admissible range of R
(measured: max F(0) = 0.099010, min F(a) = 0.129901), so this is a proof and not a numerical
observation.

**In scope.** β⁴ − 3α⁴ = 9999.9997 is not < 2α³β = 0.02, so their Theorem 4.4 does not apply, which
is the point. Their Theorem 4.5 and Example 4.8 counterexamples have support accumulating at 0 or
unbounded — they say as much themselves — which is exactly why Problem 4.7 asks about the
bounded-away-from-zero case. The root structure also matches their own Theorem 4.4 proof: Θ_R is
monotone outside [1/β, 1/α], so two roots sit outside and the two extra ones sit inside the middle
interval, which is the interval the dropped hypothesis exists to control.

**Control, both ways.** On the three cases where Theorem 4.4 *applies* (α=1, β=1.1/1.2/1.3) the
same root-counter returns at most 2 roots at t = 1.01D and t = 10D, agreeing with their proved
theorem. One instrument trap worth recording: a uniform grid scan over R reports only 2 roots for
the candidate at t = 10⁵, because the R that matters sits within 3×10⁻⁴ of π and falls outside a
grid capped at π − 10⁻³. Solve for R; do not scan for it.

## 4. Agresti & Veraar, Problems 4 and 5 — both answered, no

*Nonlinear parabolic stochastic evolution equations in critical spaces*, journal version 2025,
p. 130.

> **Problem 4.** In the variational setting of Theorem 6.4, is there a constant C_T such that for
> all u₀ ∈ L²(Ω;H) one has E sup_{t≤T} ‖u(t)‖²_H ≤ C_T(1 + E‖u₀‖²_H)?

> **Problem 5.** If u₀ⁿ → u₀ in L²(Ω;H), does uⁿ → u in L²(Ω; L²(0,T;V) ∩ C([0,T];H))?

Both fail, on the model example they name as the one they cannot settle: Allen–Cahn on the
one-dimensional torus with one-dimensional quadratic noise, du = (Δu + u − u³)dt + γu²dW, at the
critical γ² = 2. Their words: "For γ² < 2, Section 6.3.2 gives an L²(Ω)-bound. But for γ² = 2 we do
not know whether it holds."

**The reduction.** With deterministic constant data u₀ = a > 0 the field stays spatially constant
(Δu = 0, one-dimensional noise) and solves dU = (U − U³)dt + √2U²dW. Then Y = 1/U gives
dY = (−Y + 3/Y)dt − √2dW; Y = e^{−t}V gives dV = 3e^{2t}/V dt − √2e^t dW; the time change
s = (e^{2t}−1)/2 gives dV = (3/V)ds − √2dW̃; and R = V/√2 satisfies dR = (3/2R)ds − dW̃. So R is a
**BES(4)** from R₀ = 1/(√2a) and

  U_t = e^t / (√2 R_s),  s = (e^{2t} − 1)/2.

Verified in the reverse direction by Itô as well: ∂_tU = U, ∂_RU = −U/R, ∂²_RU = 2U/R² give
dU = U dt − ½e^{2t}(U/R²)dt − (U/R)dβ, and 1/R = √2Ue^{−t} turns those into exactly −U³dt and
−√2U²dW.

**Why the expectation diverges.** sup_{t≤T}U_t² ≥ 1/(2 inf_{s≤S}R_s²). A BES(4) is the norm of a
4-dimensional Brownian motion, and the chance a 4-d BM enters a ball of radius x by a fixed time is
comparable to its Newtonian capacity, of order x². (Over the infinite horizon, exactly
P(inf R ≤ x) = (x/R₀)²; the finite-horizon probability is a positive fraction of it, since BES(4)
conditioned to hit small levels is BES(0), which reaches 0 in finite time a.s.) So
P(sup U² > y) ≍ 1/y — tail index one — and E sup_{t≤T}‖u(t)‖²_H = ∞ for every T > 0, while
E‖u₀‖²_H = a² is finite. Problem 4 refuted.

Problem 5 follows from the same example: u₀ⁿ = 1/n → 0, the limit solution is identically 0, and
convergence in L²(Ω; C([0,T];H)) would need E sup‖uⁿ‖² → 0, which is ∞ for every n.

**Instrument note.** A first simulation appeared to contradict the x² scaling, showing P/x² falling
(1.53, 1.24, 0.84, 0.24). That was the step size: at 3000 steps the path moves 0.033 per step and I
was probing balls of radius 0.025 and 0.0125. Refining the steps at fixed x, P/x² climbs toward the
exact constant 2.0 — 1.204, 1.579, 1.697 at x = 0.2 and 0.725, 1.285, 1.455 at x = 0.1. The scaling
is real.

Not Lean-checked: this one has no finite arithmetic core, being Itô calculus and a capacity
estimate. Of the six, these two have the best odds of being genuinely new, since the source is a
year old.

## 5. Aldous & Fill, Open Problem 4.30 — answered, no

Same book, p. 137. Writing S_t for the integral of a standardized g along a stationary chain and
sigma^2 for its asymptotic variance rate:

> Is there some explicit function psi(b, s) -> 0 as s -> infinity, not depending on the chain, such
> that for standardized g and continuous-time chains,
> sup_x |P(S_t/(sigma sqrt t) <= x) - P(Z <= x)| <= psi(||g||_inf, t/tau2)?

**No.** Two independent reversible chains on four states. Y is two-state with rates 0->1 = eps and
1->0 = 1-eps, so its stationary mass at 1 is eps and its gap is exactly 1; Z is a fair sign
flipping at rate R/2. With eps = s^-2, R = s^4,

  A = 2 eps + sqrt((1-eps)(1-4 eps)),  B = (2-A)/(1-eps),  g = A Z + B (Y - eps),

one has, exactly and at every s: E g = 0, E g^2 = 1, ||g||_inf = A + B(1-eps) = 2, and tau2 = 1.
Take t = s, so t/tau2 -> infinity with the sup norm pinned at 2.

**Why it stays non-normal.** Not because the normalized functional vanishes — its standard
deviation stays near 1, since the asymptotic variance rate has converged. The obstruction is shape.
The expected number of visits of Y to its rare state in [0,t] is eps*t = 1/s -> 0, so S_t is
compound-Poisson with vanishing intensity: with probability 1 - O(1/s) no visit occurs and S_t sits
at the deterministic value -B eps t plus negligible fast noise, while the entire variance is
carried by the rare single excursion. At s = 1000 about 99.9 percent of the mass lies within 0.001
of the point -0.022 in normalized units, so the distribution function jumps from ~0 to ~0.999 there
against Phi(-0.022) = 0.491.

**Measured.** Kolmogorov distance to N(0,1) = 0.478, 0.505, 0.509, 0.506 at s = 30, 100, 300, 1000
— flat at about 1/2, not decreasing, while t/tau2 rises from 30 to 1000. The identities were
checked to seven decimals at every s. So no psi(2, s) tending to 0 exists.

**What it says about the question.** tau2 governs the decay of correlations, but when the
function's variance lives on a state of stationary mass eps, the functional needs time of order
1/eps to see it, and 1/eps can be pushed arbitrarily far from tau2 while ||g||_inf stays at 2. Any
correct bound needs a third quantity — the smallest stationary probability, or a moment condition
beyond the sup norm.

## 6. Bradley, Question 7 — false as literally stated

*Basic properties of strong mixing conditions: a survey and some open questions*
(arXiv:math/0511078), Question 7, Section 4.2, p. 19:

> Suppose (b1, b2, b3, ...) is an arbitrary nonincreasing sequence of numbers in [0,1]. Does there
> exist a strictly stationary random sequence X such that for all n >= 1, phi(n) = bn?

**No**, and the refutation is one line from a result Bradley quotes in the same survey. Presenting
consequences of his Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 he states: "Suppose X is a strictly stationary sequence.
(No assumption of ergodicity.) ... If phi(n) < 1/2 for some n >= 1, then X is phi-mixing" — that
is, phi(n) -> 0. Take the constant sequence b_n = 1/4, which is nonincreasing and lies in [0,1].
Then phi(1) = 1/4 < 1/2 forces phi(n) -> 0, contradicting phi(n) = 1/4 for every n.

The same argument rules out every sequence that dips below 1/2 without tending to 0, so the
realisable sequences are confined to those staying at or above 1/2 until they begin a descent that
reaches 0. The contrast with the survey's own Theorem 4.5, which achieves exactness for alpha and
rho with no such constraint, is the content: phi has a rigidity those coefficients lack.

**Read this one carefully before repeating it.** Bradley almost certainly knows the constant
sequence fails; a survey question stated in its most general form is usually shorthand for
"characterise the realisable sequences", and that characterisation — along with his own stated
sub-question, whether some stationary sequence has 1 > phi(1) = phi(2) > 0 = phi(3) — is untouched.
So the honest claim is that the literal general form is refuted from the survey's own material, not
that a question its author believed open has been settled.

This one came from the intake agent rather than from me; I verified it by pulling the archived
source, reading Question 7 at the stated locator word for word, and locating the quoted dichotomy
in the survey myself rather than trusting the citation.

## 7. Pollett, Section 2 Conjecture (ii) — answered no (but probably known)

For a lambda-transient irreducible countable transient class with almost-sure absorption, must every
finite strictly positive limit of t^a e^(lambda t) p_t(i,j) factor as A x_i m_j?

**No.** Take the nearest-neighbour continuous-time walk on Z (rate 1 each side), killed at rate
kappa everywhere and at an extra rate v at the origin. The class is lambda-transient with
lambda = kappa, and

  t^(3/2) e^(kappa t) p_t(i,j)  ->  [ (|i| + 2/v)(|j| + 2/v) + i j ] / (4 sqrt(pi)).

The `i j` term is the obstruction: the limit is a rank-TWO form, and its 2x2 minor on rows and
columns {0,1} equals (2/v)^2/(16 pi) > 0 for every v. A rank-one factorisation cannot exist. The
reason in one sentence: the chain has two ends, so the limit kernel carries one degree of freedom
per end, and A x_i m_j can encode only one. Any conjecture of this shape needs a one-end
hypothesis.

**Measured.** I solved the Feynman-Kac semigroup directly — the free-walk generator minus a point
mass v at the origin, exponentiated on a lattice wide enough that the boundary is invisible — at
t = 100, 400, 1600, 6400. Against the claimed limits 0.141047, 0.282095, 0.705237, 0.423142 for
(i,j) = (0,0), (0,1), (1,1), (1,-1) at v = 2, the computed values converge monotonically to
0.141010, 0.282004, 0.705018, 0.422932, and the minor formed from the measured entries is 0.019879
against the predicted 0.019894.

**Caveat, and it is the campaign's own.** Unlike the other results here, this row's ledger entry
already carries a specific prior-art warning: that Foley and McDonald published a discrete two-end
construction from which the same continuous-time obstruction follows. That warning is the kind that
usually proves right — a two-ended killed walk is the obvious discrete analogue and the rank-two
structure is identical. Treat this as confirmed mathematics and probably known.

## 8. Burdzy & Scheutzow, Problem 7.5 — yes (but by a classical technique)

*Forward Brownian motion* (arXiv:1302.6958), Problem 7.5, p. 26:

> Is it true that for any process {X_t, t >= 0} whose law is equivalent to BM, we can find some
> random piece which we can put in front of X such that the new process is Brownian motion?

**Yes** — and under absolute continuity rather than equivalence, which is weaker than the problem
assumes.

The argument rests on a filling-scheme lemma: if theta is an invertible ergodic measure-preserving
transformation of a standard Borel space (E,P) and Q = fP, then there is an a.s. finite integer
N >= 0 with law(theta^N Z) = Q for Z ~ P. Apply it with E = C_0(R) under two-sided Wiener measure
and (theta w)(t) = w(t+1) - w(1), lifting the one-sided density of X's law to two-sided paths. The
lemma returns a finite N for which {Z(N+t) - Z(N), t >= 0} has X's law, while {Z(t) - Z(0), t >= 0}
is a Brownian motion whose first N units are exactly the prefix.

**What I checked.** I implemented the filling construction on a finite ergodic system (Z/n with the
+1 shift) for n = 5, 7, 12, 60 with random densities: total accepted mass 1.000000000000 every
time, the law of theta^N Z matching fP to about 1e-16, E[N] finite. The negative control is the
informative one — with a *non-ergodic* theta (shift by 2 on an even cycle, f supported on the odd
class) exactly half the mass is ever accepted and half survives forever. Ergodicity is load-bearing
in the lemma, not decorative, and the construction correctly fails without it. I did not verify the
L1 ergodic-theorem step in the infinite-dimensional setting; I read it and it is standard, but that
step is checked by eye only.

**Novelty is the weak point here, and the campaign's own proof file volunteers it**: the lemma "is a
filling construction related to classical work of Chacon-Ornstein, Rost, and Thorisson", and
Thorisson's shift-coupling theory is precisely about reaching an absolutely continuous law by a
random shift along an ergodic flow. Sound mathematics; very likely a known consequence.

## 9. Salez, Problem 2.4 — the literal statement falls

*Modern aspects of Markov chains: entropy, curvature and the cutoff phenomenon* (arXiv:2508.21055),
Problem 2.4, p. 27:

> Prove that cutoff is equivalent to the product condition for random walks on Cayley graphs of
> Abelian groups.

Salez presents this as a workshop prediction reiterated as [52, Conjecture 1], and says confirming
it "would represent a substantial progress" on two of his other problems. No degree bound appears
in the question as printed.

**I first recorded this as "flagged, not verified" and that was wrong** — I had assumed the ledger's
fields were all the argument there was, and a complete written proof was sitting in the campaign
tree the whole time. Having read it and checked its algebra, the construction is sound.

**The construction.** G_d = (Z/2)^d x (Z/m_d) with m_d = 1 + ceil(2^d log d / 4), generating set
S_d = {(e_i, k) : i <= d, k != 0} together with {(x, 0) : x in the cube, x != 0}. Disjoint,
inverse-closed, generating — so this is a simple, connected, undirected, vertex-transitive,
*unweighted* Cayley graph on an abelian group.

**The mechanism, in one line.** The second family of generators adds a uniform nonzero *cube*
element, which is a reset: Q_d = (2^d Pi_d - I)/(2^d - 1). A reset commutes with the cube
semigroup, so the projected total variation obeys the exact identity
q_d(t) = e^(-beta_d t) c_d(alpha_d t) with beta_d = 2^d/D_d. The size of m_d is chosen so that
beta_d T_d -> 1 where T_d is the cube's mixing scale — the survival factor e^(-s) therefore bites
exactly on the scale where the cube mixes, and the profile becomes e^(-s) on 0 < s < 1 and 0
beyond. Continuous decay: no cutoff.

**What I re-derived before believing it.** alpha_d -> 1 and beta_d ~ 4/(d log d), so
beta_d T_d -> 1; the character computation puts the slowest sector at a = 1, giving gap
= 2 alpha_d/d + beta_d ~ 2/d; hence gap x t_mix ~ (1/2) log d -> infinity, so the product condition
holds; and the profile yields t(3/4)/t(1/4) -> log(4/3) = 0.288, not 1. The whole-graph error bound
|v_d - q_d| <= e^(-alpha_d t) + 1/m_d is correct and both terms vanish on the relevant scale.

**Two caveats decide what it is worth.** First, the degree is unbounded — of order sqrt(|G|) times
a log. Salez's Problem 2.4 as printed carries no degree bound, so the literal statement falls; but
whether the workshop question and [52, Conjecture 1] carry one is unchecked, and that is exactly
what determines the significance. Second, the reset mechanism is classical (the proof file names
the Pak-style counterexamples itself); the work here is making one live inside an *unweighted*
abelian Cayley graph. A scoped independent review by another agent is on file, checking the full
character spectrum, the coupling and the mixing-time brackets, and found no gap in scope.

## 10. Lyons & Peres, Question 17.18 — answered, no

*Probability on Trees and Networks* (version 19 August 2026), Question 17.18, p. 575: must a
consistent equivariant flow rule admitting a stationary measure *equivalent* to Galton-Watson
measure induce an ergodic Markov chain? Their Proposition 17.17 proves it when all child weights
are strictly positive; Exercise 17.22 gives a non-ergodic example whose stationary measure is only
absolutely continuous — equivalence is the point.

**No.** Take GW with P(D=2) = 3/5, P(D=3) = 2/5 (no leaves, mean 12/5), and define an
isomorphism-invariant colour in {0,1} by AND at two-child vertices and OR at three-child vertices.
Both rules preserve unanimity, so every vertex has at least one child of its own colour; let the
flow choose uniformly among those. GW is stationary, the root colour is invariant along the chain
and takes each value with probability 1/2 — a non-constant invariant function, hence no ergodicity,
with the stationary measure equal to GW and so trivially equivalent to it. The rule gives weight
zero to wrong-coloured children, placing it outside Proposition 17.17 exactly as it must.

**That the colour exists is the technical content.** Truncate at level n, colour the boundary
Bernoulli(1/2), propagate up, and let q_n be the probability the root is 1. The map
F(p) = (3/5)p² + (2/5)[1-(1-p)³] fixes 1/2 (3/20 + 7/20), and the root formula is read-once, hence
multilinear, so q_n is a bounded martingale of mean 1/2. Writing r_n = E[q_n²] as the probability
that two independent colourings both give root 1: AND contributes r², OR contributes
1 - [1/8 + 1/8 - r³] = 3/4 + r³ (a pair with marginals 1/2 has P(00) = P(11) = r). So r_0 = 1/4 and
r_{n+1} = 3/10 + (3/5)r² + (2/5)r³, whose increment factors exactly as
(r - 1/2)[(2/5)r² + (4/5)r - 3/5]. On [1/4, 1/2] the quadratic is negative (-0.375 at 1/4, -0.1 at
1/2) and so is (r - 1/2), so r_n increases; the competing root (sqrt(10)-2)/2 = 0.581 lies above
1/2, so r_n rises to 1/2. Then E[q(1-q)] = 0 and the colour is genuinely two-valued.

**The step the proof leaves implicit, which I checked myself:** that GW really is stationary. It is
not obvious, since the flow's choice depends on a tail function of the tree. It holds because,
conditioned on the children's colours, the child subtrees are independent, each distributed as GW
conditioned on its *own* colour. Every admissible child carries the root's colour c, so the chosen
subtree is exactly GW(·|colour = c) — the uniform choice adds no bias, those children being
exchangeable given the colour vector — and mixing over c returns GW.

Prior art unchecked; the tree-automaton and factor-of-iid literature is where to look, since an
AND/OR read-once formula on a Galton-Watson tree with a fixed point at 1/2 is a natural object.

## 11. Hasebe & Ueda, Problem 3.3 — the constant is 1

*Large time unimodality for classical and free Brownian motions with initial distributions*,
ALEA 15 (2018) 353-374, Problem 3.3: what is the optimal universal C such that mu (+) S(0,t) is
unimodal for all t >= C D_mu^2 and all compactly supported mu? They prove C <= 4 and print
"2 <= C <= 4".

**C = 1**, and it is attained.

**Upper bound.** F_R(u) = integral dmu(x)/((x-u)^2 + R^2) has no local minimum of value at most
1/D^2. At a stationary point, reweight by dQ proportional to ((x-u)^2+y)^(-2) dmu with y = R^2. Two
elementary moment inequalities for a centred variable supported in an interval of length D —
a <= D^2/4 and b + 3a^2 <= D^2 a — combine with the exact identity
1/F_R(u) = (b + 2ay + y^2)/(a+y) and the second-derivative condition (which via Jensen forces
y <= 3a) to give 1/F_R(u) - D^2 <= [(y-3a)(y+a) + (4a-D^2)y]/(a+y) <= 0.

I re-derived each step: the first inequality from E[(X-l)(h-X)] >= 0 with centring; the second from
(X-X')^4 <= D^2 (X-X')^2 for an independent copy, where E(X-X')^4 = 2b + 6a^2 and E(X-X')^2 = 2a
give the coefficient 3 exactly; the second-derivative formula
F_R''(u)/(2Z) = 3 - 4y E_Q[1/((X-u)^2+y)] from scratch; and the factorisation, which expands
correctly term by term.

**The equality case is tight.** For symmetric Bernoulli at u = 0, F_R'' is proportional to
3 - 4y/(1+y), vanishing exactly at y = R^2 = 3 = 3a with 4a = 4 = D^2 — precisely the equality
condition. There F_R(0) = 1/4 = 1/D^2 and F_R(s) < F_R(0) for all s != 0, so the point is a strict
maximum, never a local minimum. For y < 3 it is a local minimum of value 1/(1+y) > 1/4.

**On the printed lower bound, said carefully.** The prior on "a refereed paper slipped" is that I
misread it, so I re-derived the Bernoulli threshold inside their frame with their own criterion
(unimodal iff xi_R(u) = 1/t has at most two solutions for every R). For Bernoulli, xi_R has a local
minimum at 0 of value 1/(1+R^2) when R^2 < 3, and a third solution appears exactly when
t < 1 + R^2 < 4 — so unimodal iff t >= 4, their stated threshold, with D_mu = 2. But the condition
is t >= C D_mu^2 = 4C, so t >= 4 forces only C >= 1, not 2. Either the printed 2 divided by D_mu
instead of D_mu^2, or there is a reading I am missing — and if their C >= 2 is right under some
reading, then this theorem contradicts the paper and one of the two is wrong. That fork, not the
slip, is what to settle first.

Note this is the second Hasebe-Ueda problem settled here (result 3 is their 2020 multiplicative
paper). Two results against one group's stated problems in one session is itself a reason to look
hard at the later literature before claiming either.

## 12. Aldous & Janson, Ansatz 20 — false in both parts

Inside Open Problem 8 of arXiv:2303.02529, which asks for explicit bounds on |a(n,m) − a(m)|, the
authors write "In particular, prove the following Ansatz." **Ansatz 20:** for nonnegative f with
f(j) = O(j^k) for some finite k, (i) if sum a(i)f(i) converges then sum_{i<=n} a(n,i)f(i) tends to
it; (ii) if it diverges then sum_{i<=n} a(n,i)f(i) is asymptotic to sum_{i<=n} a(i)f(i). Here
a(n,j) is the probability the tagged harmonic descent chain from n visits j, and
a(j) = 6H_{j-1}/(pi^2 (j-1)) its fixed-j limit.

**Both parts fail, on one observation: a(n,n) = 1 exactly.** The chain starts at n, so it visits n
surely; a test function that charges the point j = n along a subsequence contributes something
every fixed-j limit is blind to.

For (i), take f(j) = 1{j = 2^r}, bounded and so trivially of polynomial growth. Since
a(2^r) = O(r 2^-r), the limiting series S converges. At n = 2^K the sum is sum_{r<=K} a(2^K, 2^r),
whose top term is a(2^K, 2^K) = 1, so for fixed M < K it is at least 1 + sum_{r<=M} a(2^K, 2^r);
letting K and then M go to infinity gives liminf at least 1 + S, against a predicted S.

For (ii), take g(j) = j·1{j = 2^r}, so g(j) <= j. Since a(j)·j is asymptotically (6/pi^2) log j,
the term a(2^r)2^r is of order r and the truncated limiting sum at n = 2^K is of order K^2, while
the actual sum is at least its root term 2^K. The ratio diverges.

I re-derived each piece: the asymptotics of a(j) and a(j)j, the summability of a(2^r), the
polynomial-growth hypothesis for both test functions, and a(n,n) = 1 from the definition. No rate
of convergence or numerical evaluation is needed anywhere.

**Why this reaches beyond the Ansatz.** Aldous and Janson *invoke* Ansatz 20 to pass from the
fixed-j limits to balance-index asymptotics — "we seek to deduce the asymptotics of E[I(n)] from
the limits a(m). At this point we need to invoke Ansatz 20." So those derivations lean on a
statement that is false in general. That is not a claim their index results are wrong: the indices
they treat use regular, non-sparse weights, exactly the class the counterexample avoids. It does
mean the Ansatz cannot be used as stated, and any application needs a hypothesis excluding sparse
root-charging test functions.

**Scope.** This refutes the displayed Ansatz, not Open Problem 8 as a whole — explicit bounds on
|a(n,m) − a(m)| remain open, and the right regularity class for a true version is undetermined.
Note this is the second Aldous-Janson item here: both are cases of a stated auxiliary claim failing
while the surrounding programme stands.

## Also: P0189 (Brown measure from strong convergence) — half confirmed, gap located

Not settled, but the work is now split cleanly. The candidate counterexample uses q×q clock/shift
pairs with U^q = I, V^q = −I, so that U+V is nilpotent and every empirical eigenvalue measure is
δ₀, against a limiting Brown measure that is not δ₀.

The concrete half is confirmed exactly: at q = 3, 4, 5, 8, 13, 32 the identities U^q = I, V^q = −I
and (U+V)^q = 0 all hold to machine precision, so det(zI − U − V) = z^q and the empirical measure
is δ₀ at every q. (The apparent growth in `max|eig|` with q is the standard ill-conditioning of
eigenvalues of a nilpotent matrix, not a real spectrum.) Operator norms ‖U+V‖ converge to 2.

The load-bearing step that is **not** checked is the other one: whether the clock/shift pair
converges *strongly* — norm convergence for every noncommutative polynomial, not just for U+V — to
the limiting pair, and whether that limit's Brown measure for u+v is what the argument claims. The
pair converges to a rotation-algebra pair, not a free pair, and the two have different Brown
measures, so the argument's citation needs to match the limit it actually has. That is where the
next hour should go.

character eigenvalues, so its autocorrelation is exp(-delta s)cos(s + phi) rather than a monotone

monotonicity in the modulation speed; a reversible environment could not. What I did not check is
the quantitative half — whether the oscillating term, of order epsilon^2/c, really dominates the
uniform remainder bound of order epsilon^3 in the regime used. It should for small epsilon and
moderate c, but "should" is not checked. Promising, not settled.
