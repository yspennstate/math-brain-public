# F0057 — total variation for every fixed order statistic in Janson's conditioned allocation model

## Source and exact target

Svante Janson, *Simply generated trees, conditioned Galton–Watson trees, random allocations and condensation*, Probability Surveys 9 (2012), DOI `10.1214/11-PS188`, arXiv `1112.0510`.

The intake/campaign labels the target **Problem 18.10**; in the published Probability Surveys section numbering the same statement appears as **Problem 19.10**. Under the assumptions of Theorem 18.7/19.7, if

- `Y_(1) >= Y_(2) >= ...` are the ordered occupancies in the conditioned allocation,
- `xi_(1) >= xi_(2) >= ...` are the corresponding order statistics of independent reference variables (or the source's tuned triangular-array variables `xi^(n)` in that formulation),

prove that for every fixed integer `j >= 1`,

\[
 d_{\rm TV}(Y_{(j)},\xi_{(j)})\longrightarrow 0.
\tag{F0057}
\]

Janson proves the case `j=1`, proves Kolmogorov convergence for every fixed `j`, and states that the total-variation extension should follow by a modification of the `j=1` proof.

## 1. Inputs inherited exactly from Janson's theorem/proof

Write `zeta_n` for the independent reference variable used at size `n`: in the fixed-law formulation, `zeta_n = xi`; in the tuned formulation, `zeta_n = xi^(n)`. Let `zeta_{n,(j)}` denote the `j`-th largest of `n` iid copies of `zeta_n`.

We use only the following facts already established in the source proof of Theorem 18.7/19.7 and its total-variation part for the maximum.

### Input J1 — order-statistic Kolmogorov convergence

For every fixed `j`,

\[
 d_K\!\left(Y_{(j)},\zeta_{n,(j)}\right)\to0.
\tag{J1}
\]

### Input J2 — a global cutoff

There exists an integer cutoff `B_n` such that

\[
 \mathbb P(Y_{(1)}>B_n)=o(1),
 \qquad
 n\,\mathbb P(\zeta_n>B_n)=o(1).
\tag{J2}
\]

Consequently, if `\zeta_{n,1},...,\zeta_{n,n}` are iid copies of `\zeta_n`,

\[
 \mathbb P\!\left(\max_{i\le n}\zeta_{n,i}>B_n\right)=o(1).
\tag{J2'}
\]

In the fixed-law proof one may choose `B_n=o(\sqrt n)`; in the tuned triangular-array proof the source takes `B_n=O(\log n)`, which is also `o(\sqrt n)`.

### Input J3 — uniform fixed-coordinate local ratio

For every fixed integer `q>=1`, uniformly over supported integers

\[
0\le k_1,\ldots,k_q\le B_n,
\]

the exact conditioned-allocation formula and the local limit ratio used by Janson imply

\[
\mathbb P(Y_1=k_1,\ldots,Y_q=k_q)
=
\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)
\prod_{r=1}^q \mathbb P(\zeta_n=k_r).
\tag{J3}
\]

The `o(1)` depends on fixed `q` but is uniform over all supported `k_r<=B_n`. This is the same uniformity used in the source to obtain joint Poisson limits for occupancy counts and the maximum total-variation theorem. In the fixed-law formulation `qB_n=o(\sqrt n)`; in the tuned formulation `qB_n=O(\log n)=o(\sqrt n)`, so the source's local-ratio estimate remains uniform after changing finitely many coordinates.

No assumption beyond the corresponding source version of Theorem 18.7/19.7 is introduced below.

## 2. New uniform moving-set Poissonization lemma

The previous campaign proof used Janson's joint Poisson limits on fixed/moving tail intervals but left one bookkeeping obligation: the source truncation event had not been inserted uniformly across every interval in the partition. The following lemma eliminates that gap.

### Lemma 2.1 — uniform factorial moments for moving tail sets

Fix `r,q>=1`. For each `n`, let

\[
 E_{1,n},\ldots,E_{r,n}\subseteq \{0,1,\ldots,B_n\}
\]

be pairwise disjoint sets. Define conditioned counts

\[
 N_{s,n}:=\#\{1\le i\le n:Y_i\in E_{s,n}\},
\]

and independent counts

\[
 \bar N_{s,n}:=\#\{1\le i\le n:\zeta_{n,i}\in E_{s,n}\}.
\]

For every fixed multi-index `a=(a_1,...,a_r)` with `|a|:=a_1+...+a_r=q`,

\[
\mathbb E\prod_{s=1}^r (N_{s,n})_{a_s}
=
\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)
\prod_{s=1}^r
\left(n\,\mathbb P(\zeta_n\in E_{s,n})\right)^{a_s},
\tag{2.1}
\]

uniformly over the choice of the moving sets `E_{s,n}`. Here `(x)_a=x(x-1)\cdots(x-a+1)`.

#### Proof

Expand the left side over ordered `q`-tuples of pairwise distinct coordinate indices. By exchangeability of the conditioned allocation, every term is the same. It therefore equals

\[
(n)_q
\sum_{(k_1,\ldots,k_q)\in \mathcal E_n}
\mathbb P(Y_1=k_1,\ldots,Y_q=k_q),
\]

where `\mathcal E_n` is the appropriate Cartesian product of the sets `E_{s,n}`, with `E_{s,n}` repeated `a_s` times. Every `k_i<=B_n`. Hence J3 gives, uniformly on the whole summation domain,

\[
\mathbb P(Y_1=k_1,\ldots,Y_q=k_q)
=(1+o(1))\prod_i\mathbb P(\zeta_n=k_i).
\]

Because the relative error is uniform and all summands are nonnegative, it factors through the entire sum. Thus

\[
\mathbb E\prod_s (N_{s,n})_{a_s}
=(1+o(1))(n)_q
\prod_s \mathbb P(\zeta_n\in E_{s,n})^{a_s}.
\]

Since `q` is fixed, `(n)_q/n^q\to1`, yielding (2.1). ∎

### Lemma 2.2 — joint Poissonization for moving sets

Assume in addition that along a subsequence

\[
 n\mathbb P(\zeta_n\in E_{s,n})\to\lambda_s<\infty,
 \qquad s=1,\ldots,r.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Then

\[
(N_{1,n},\ldots,N_{r,n})
\Rightarrow
(Z_1,\ldots,Z_r),
\tag{2.3}
\]

where `Z_1,...,Z_r` are independent Poisson variables with means `\lambda_1,...,\lambda_r`. The iid count vector `(\bar N_{1,n},...,\bar N_{r,n})` has the same limit.

#### Proof

Lemma 2.1 gives convergence of every joint factorial moment to the corresponding product `\prod_s\lambda_s^{a_s}`. These are exactly the joint factorial moments of independent Poisson variables. Since the total means stay bounded, the count vectors are tight; the multivariate method of factorial moments gives (2.3). The iid statement is standard multinomial-to-independent-Poisson convergence and follows identically. ∎

### Lemma 2.3 — the truncation error is globally uniform

Let `E_{1,n},...,E_{r,n}` now be arbitrary moving subsets of `\mathbb Z_+`, not necessarily truncated, and replace each by

\[
 E'_{s,n}=E_{s,n}\cap[0,B_n].
\]

Any event determined by the conditioned count vector changes only on

\[
\{Y_{(1)}>B_n\},
\]

and any corresponding iid event changes only on

\[
\left\{\max_{i\le n}\zeta_{n,i}>B_n\right\}.
\]

Therefore **simultaneously for every moving family of sets**,

\[
\bigl|P(\mathcal E(N))-P(\mathcal E(N'))\bigr|=o(1),
\qquad
\bigl|P(\mathcal E(\bar N))-P(\mathcal E(\bar N'))\bigr|=o(1),
\tag{2.4}
\]

with the same two global error terms from J2/J2'. In particular, if a partition uses only `O_\delta(1)` intervals, **there is no interval-by-interval truncation loss to sum**.

This is exactly the missing `P30H-F001` bookkeeping step. ∎

## 3. The finite partition reduction (#8 DISCRETIZE)

Fix `j>=1`. Put

\[
P_n=\mathcal L(Y_{(j)}),
\qquad
Q_n=\mathcal L(\zeta_{n,(j)}).
\]

Let the positive set for total variation be

\[
S_n:=\{k\in\mathbb Z_+:P_n\{k\}>Q_n\{k\}\}.
\]

Then

\[
d_{\rm TV}(P_n,Q_n)=P_n(S_n)-Q_n(S_n).
\tag{3.1}
\]

Fix `\delta\in(0,1)`. We partition `\mathbb Z_+` into consecutive integer intervals as follows.

1. Every atom `k` with `Q_n\{k\}>\delta/2` is made a singleton block.
2. The remaining consecutive integers are greedily grouped into maximal intervals `J` satisfying
   \[
   Q_n(J)\le\delta.
   \tag{3.2}
   \]

The standard greedy construction used in Janson's `j=1` proof gives at most

\[
L_\delta\le \frac4\delta+1
\tag{3.3}
\]

blocks. Every nonsingleton block satisfies (3.2).

This converts the arbitrary set `S_n` into finitely many pieces `S_n\cap J`, with a block count depending only on `\delta`, not on `n`.

## 4. Singleton blocks

By J1, `d_K(P_n,Q_n)\to0`. For any integer `k`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
|P_n\{k\}-Q_n\{k\}|
&=\left|
(P_n(({-}\infty,k])-Q_n(({-}\infty,k]))
-
(P_n(({-}\infty,k-1])-Q_n(({-}\infty,k-1]))
\right|\\
&\le 2d_K(P_n,Q_n)=o(1),
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
\]

uniformly in `k`. Since there are `O_\delta(1)` singleton blocks, their total positive contribution to (3.1) is `o(1)`.

## 5. Good nonsingleton blocks and the asymmetric count sandwich (#23)

Let

\[
J=[a,b]
\]

be a nonsingleton block. Call it **D-good** if

\[
\mu_n(a):=n\,\mathbb P(\zeta_n\ge a)\le D,
\tag{5.1}
\]

and **D-bad** otherwise. Here `D>0` will be sent to infinity only after `n\to\infty` and `\delta\downarrow0`.

For a good block define the disjoint sets

\[
A_n:=S_n\cap J,
\qquad
C_n:=J\setminus S_n,
\qquad
B_n^+:=[b+1,\infty).
\tag{5.2}
\]

Define the tail intensities

\[
\alpha_n=nP(\zeta_n\in A_n),
\quad
\gamma_n=nP(\zeta_n\in C_n),
\quad
\beta_n=nP(\zeta_n\in B_n^+).
\tag{5.3}
\]

Then

\[
\alpha_n+\gamma_n+\beta_n
=nP(\zeta_n\ge a)\le D.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Pass to an arbitrary subsequence. By compactness of `[0,D]^3`, pass further so that

\[
(\alpha_n,\gamma_n,\beta_n)\to(\alpha,\gamma,\beta).
\tag{5.5}
\]

By Lemmas 2.2 and 2.3, the conditioned and iid count triples on `(A_n,C_n,B_n^+)` both converge to independent Poisson variables

\[
X\sim{\rm Po}(\alpha),
\qquad
Z\sim{\rm Po}(\gamma),
\qquad
R\sim{\rm Po}(\beta).
\tag{5.6}
\]

### 5.1 Thinness forces a small total interval intensity

Let

\[
s:=\alpha+\gamma.
\]

The iid event

\[
\{R=0,\ X+Z=j\}
\]

forces the `j`-th largest iid observation to lie in `J`. Hence, because `Q_n(J)\le\delta`,

\[
\delta
\ge
 e^{-(\beta+s)}\frac{s^j}{j!}.
\tag{5.7}
\]

Since `\beta+s\le D`,

\[
 s\le \eta_j(D,\delta)
 :=\bigl(j!e^D\delta\bigr)^{1/j}.
\tag{5.8}
\]

In particular, `\alpha,\gamma\le\eta_j(D,\delta)`.

### 5.2 Exact order-statistic implications

If the conditioned order statistic satisfies

\[
Y_{(j)}\in A_n,
\]

then for some `r\in\{0,1,\ldots,j-1\}` there are exactly `r` coordinates in `B_n^+`, at least one in `A_n`, and enough coordinates in `A_n\cup C_n` to reach rank `j`:

\[
N_{B_n^+}=r,
\qquad
N_{A_n}\ge1,
\qquad
N_{A_n}+N_{C_n}\ge j-r.
\tag{5.9}
\]

Conversely, in the iid sample, if for some `r<j`

\[
\bar N_{B_n^+}=r,
\qquad
\bar N_{C_n}=0,
\qquad
\bar N_{A_n}\ge j-r,
\tag{5.10}
\]

then necessarily

\[
\zeta_{n,(j)}\in A_n.
\]

These implications are exact and allow arbitrary ties; no continuity of the discrete reference distribution is used.

Consequently, after taking the subsequential limit in (5.9)-(5.10), the positive excess contributed by this block is at most

\[
\sum_{r=0}^{j-1}P(R=r)
\left[
P(X\ge1,\ X+Z\ge j-r)
-e^{-\gamma}P(X\ge j-r)
\right].
\tag{5.11}
\]

For every integer `k>=1`,

\[
P(X\ge1,X+Z\ge k)
\le
P(X\ge k)+P(X\ge1,Z\ge1).
\tag{5.12}
\]

Thus the bracket in (5.11) is at most

\[
(1-e^{-\gamma})P(X\ge k)
+P(X\ge1)P(Z\ge1)
\le
\gamma+\alpha\gamma.
\tag{5.13}
\]

A slightly sharper bound useful after summation is obtained by observing that the first term vanishes when `X<k`, while for a positive-set contribution the difference can be charged to either one `A` arrival or one simultaneous `A/C` arrival. The elementary bounds `1-e^{-x}\le x` yield

\[
\text{block excess}
\le \gamma\min\{1,\alpha\}+\alpha\gamma
\le 2\eta_j(D,\delta)\,\gamma.
\tag{5.14}
\]

(If one prefers not to optimize this line, the cruder `\gamma+\alpha\gamma` is also sufficient after replacing the partition by the source's alternating thin-block construction. Formula (5.14) is the direct positive-set form.)

### 5.3 Sum over all good blocks

The `C_n` parts of distinct blocks are disjoint. Once a block is D-good, every block to its right is D-good because `a\mapsto nP(\zeta_n\ge a)` is decreasing. Therefore the limiting sum of their `\gamma`-intensities is at most the tail intensity at the first good block, hence at most `D`.

There are only `O_\delta(1)` blocks, so diagonal subsequence extraction is harmless. Summing (5.14),

\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\sum_{J\ {m good}}
\bigl(P_n(S_n\cap J)-Q_n(S_n\cap J)\bigr)_+
\le
2D\eta_j(D,\delta).
\tag{5.15}
\]

## 6. Bad blocks

Singleton bad blocks are already `o(1)` by Section 4. Suppose there is at least one bad nonsingleton block, and let

\[
J_*=[a_*,b_*]
\]

be the **rightmost** such block.

Because `J_*` is nonsingleton,

\[
Q_n(J_*)\le\delta.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Hence its positive contribution is at most

\[
P_n(J_*)
\le Q_n(J_*)+2d_K(P_n,Q_n)
\le \delta+o(1).
\tag{6.2}
\]

Every earlier bad block lies strictly below `a_*`. Thus all its positive-set mass is bounded by

\[
P_n(Y_{(j)}<a_*).
\tag{6.3}
\]

By J1,

\[
P_n(Y_{(j)}<a_*)
\le
Q_n(\zeta_{n,(j)}<a_*)+o(1).
\tag{6.4}
\]

Set

\[
p_n=P(\zeta_n\ge a_*),
\qquad
\mu_n=np_n.
\]

Since `J_*` is D-bad,

\[
\mu_n>D.
\tag{6.5}
\]

The event `\zeta_{n,(j)}<a_*` means that at most `j-1` of the `n` iid variables lie in `[a_*,\infty)`, i.e.

\[
Q_n(\zeta_{n,(j)}<a_*)
=P({\rm Bin}(n,p_n)\le j-1).
\tag{6.6}
\]

For fixed `j`, choose `D>=2(j-1)+2`. A standard multiplicative Chernoff estimate gives uniformly for `\mu_n>D`

\[
P({\rm Bin}(n,p_n)\le j-1)
\le e^{-\mu_n/8}
\le e^{-D/8}.
\tag{6.7}
\]

(Any explicit fixed-`j` Poisson/Chernoff tail tending to zero with `D` would suffice.) Therefore all bad nonsingleton blocks together contribute at most

\[
\delta+e^{-D/8}+o(1).
\tag{6.8}
\]

## 7. Conclusion

Combining the singleton, good-block and bad-block estimates gives

\[
\boxed{
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
 d_{\rm TV}(Y_{(j)},\zeta_{n,(j)})
\le
2D\bigl(j!e^D\delta\bigr)^{1/j}
+\delta+e^{-D/8}.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

This displayed ordering of limits should be read with `D` chosen first and `\delta` then chosen depending on `D`: for every fixed `D`, let `\delta\downarrow0`; the first two terms vanish. We obtain

\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}d_{\rm TV}(Y_{(j)},\zeta_{n,(j)})
\le e^{-D/8}.
\]

Then let `D\to\infty`. Hence

\[
\boxed{
 d_{\rm TV}(Y_{(j)},\zeta_{n,(j)})\to0
\qquad\text{for every fixed }j.
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

This proves the source statement in both source versions covered by Theorem 18.7/19.7, because the only inputs were J1-J3 and the cutoff/local-ratio estimates that the source itself establishes in the corresponding fixed-law and tuned-law proofs.

## 8. Counterexample-first attempts and the three new methods

Before committing to the proof route I tried to break the statement in three places.

### #8 — DISCRETIZE

**Attack.** Total variation can hide on wildly moving subsets of the integer line even when Kolmogorov distance vanishes. A candidate failure would make the maximizing positive set oscillate too much for the source's interval argument.

**Outcome.** The heavy-singleton plus thin-interval partition converts the arbitrary positive set into `O_\delta(1)` interval-local pieces. On each thin piece the `j`-th order statistic depends only on three counts `(N_A,N_C,N_B)`. The continuum-looking TV issue is therefore reduced to a finite-dimensional count problem.

### #10 — DERANDOMIZE

**Attack.** The prior campaign proof could fail because every moving interval had its own truncation event, and `o(1)` errors might not be uniform when the partition itself depends on `n`.

**Outcome.** Lemma 2.3 shows that all intervals share the same two global bad events: one conditioned maximum above `B_n` and one iid maximum above `B_n`. Their probabilities are `o(1)` independently of the interval. This closes `P30H-F001` without a per-block union bound.

### #23 — DO WHAT THE PROPOSER DID NOT EXPECT

**Attack.** Janson's `j=1` proof uses a multiplicative comparison of one tail-count event. Repeating that formula literally for `j>1` creates cumbersome tie and overcounting cases.

**Outcome.** Use an asymmetric pair of implications instead: a necessary conditioned-count event and a sufficient iid-count event, equations (5.9)-(5.10). Joint Poissonization then controls their gap directly. This avoids reproducing the maximum proof line by line and works uniformly for arbitrary fixed rank `j`.

No counterexample survived these tests.
