# A stronger planar word lemma with coefficients

The starting point is Lemma `planar:main` in OpenAI, *A group without fixed price* (5 October 2026), [planar.tex](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/A-group-without-fixed-price-October-5-2026/build/planar.tex). The lemma below improves its minimum exterior length from seven to four and its matching length from \(m-3\) to \(m-2\). The extra observation is that the graph on inner relator disks is bipartite. No assertion about the rank theorem or fixed price is made here.

## Statement

Let \(H\) be a group generated by a finite alphabet \(S\), let \(U\) be a disjoint finite alphabet, and set

\[
 L=H*F(U),\qquad D=L/\langle\!\langle\mathcal R\rangle\!\rangle.
\]

An exterior occurrence is an occurrence of a letter from \(U^{\pm1}\). A coefficient gap is the value in \(H\) of the \(S^{\pm1}\)-word between two successive exterior occurrences. For a cyclic word this includes the closing gap. A word is cyclically \(H\)-reduced if no two cyclically successive exterior letters are inverse with coefficient gap equal to \(1\).

An interval matches a relator interval if its signed exterior letters agree in order and its intervening coefficient gaps agree as elements of \(H\). It uses no exterior position of that relator more than once. A full cyclic match also agrees on the complete cyclic list and the closing gap.

Assume the following:

1. Every \(r\in\mathcal R\) has \(m(r)\ge4\) exterior occurrences, all positive, with distinct underlying generators.
2. Different relators share at most one generator from \(U\).
3. The natural map \(H\to D\) is injective.

If a cyclically \(H\)-reduced word \(W\) has an exterior occurrence and is trivial in \(D\), then either it has a full cyclic match with a relator or its inverse, or it has two exterior-disjoint intervals, each matching an interval of a cyclic relator or its inverse with at least

\[
 m(r)-2                                                    \tag{1}
\]

exterior occurrences. The relators furnishing the two intervals may be the same. Only values of coefficient gaps are compared; their spelling lengths are unrestricted.

## 1. A finite punctured-sphere filling

The normal form in \(H*F(U)\) shows that \(W\) is nontrivial in \(L\). Indeed, first evaluate the coefficient gaps. Blocks of exterior letters separated by identity coefficients are freely reduced, and the nonidentity coefficients separate the other blocks. The cyclic reduction assumption prevents any such block from disappearing by adjacent inverse cancellation.

Since \(W\) is trivial in \(D\), write it in \(L\) as a product of conjugates of relators and their inverses, with the smallest possible number \(N\) of factors. Then \(N>0\).

Present \(H\) on \(S\) using all true \(H\)-relations. The chosen equality uses only finitely many of them: an element of a normal closure is a finite product of conjugates. Let \(K_H\) be the presentation complex for a sufficient finite subset of these relations, and let \(K\) be its wedge with the exterior generator circles. There is a homomorphism \(\pi_1(K)\to L\), but it need not be injective.

Realize the chosen equality by a map of a sphere with \(N+1\) open disks removed into \(K\). Let \(O\) be the distinguished missing disk and \(B_1,\ldots,B_N\) the relator disks. Reading boundaries in their disk orientations, \(O\) reads \(W^{-1}\), and each \(B_i\) reads a chosen \(r\) or \(r^{-1}\). One construction cuts the punctured sphere along spokes from \(O\) to the other boundary circles, labels the spokes by the conjugators, and fills the remaining equality using free cancellations and the finitely many true \(H\)-relations. Conversely, cutting along spokes recovers an expression with those \(N\) factors.

All complexes and maps in use can be subdivided to be piecewise linear. A mapping-cylinder model for each relation gives a finite triangulable model if needed. Additional true \(H\)-relations may be included during replacements. The minimality of \(N\) always refers to expressions in the actual group \(L\), rather than to its finite presentation approximations.

For each \(u\in U\), choose a point \(q_u\) inside its circle edge away from the images of subdivision vertices. The target is an interval near this point. Its preimage consists of finitely many disjoint cooriented arcs and circles. Each exterior occurrence on a boundary supplies exactly one arc endpoint. An arc is mapped constantly to \(q_u\), and its two endpoints have opposite signed crossings when read in disk orientations. This follows by orienting the surface: both disk orientations reverse the induced boundary orientation of the punctured surface, so the two crossing signs remain opposite.

Collapse each boundary disk to a vertex and take the arc tracks as the edges of a graph \(G\) embedded in the sphere. Closed tracks are retained in the surface map but are not edges of \(G\). An inner vertex coming from \(r\) has degree exactly \(m(r)\), counted with incidence multiplicity.

## 2. Connectivity and the inner graph

The graph \(G\) is connected. Otherwise choose a component not containing \(O\) and a small regular neighborhood of its disks and arc tracks. Its boundary can avoid every other track, including closed tracks. Choose the boundary circle \(\gamma\) facing the region containing \(O\). The disk side of \(\gamma\) away from \(O\) contains at least one relator disk, and may contain further components.

The image of \(\gamma\) avoids every \(q_u\). Cutting each exterior circle at \(q_u\) leaves a space retracting onto \(K_H\). Thus, after a basepoint change along this cut space, \(\gamma\) represents an element of \(H\). Fill every enclosed relator hole in a presentation space for \(D\). This shows that the element is trivial in \(D\), hence trivial in \(H\) by injectivity. It is therefore trivial in \(L\). Replace the disk side of \(\gamma\) with a filling using true \(H\)-relations and free cancellations, adding finitely many such relations if necessary. This removes at least one relator disk and contradicts minimality of \(N\). No individual closed track has been assumed to bound a null-homotopic loop in the target.

There is no loop at an inner vertex: all its exterior letters have the same sign, whereas an arc pairs opposite signs.

An edge cannot join two disks labelled by the same relator. Such disks have opposite orientations and the edge meets the unique occurrence of some generator \(u\) on each. Base both relator loops at \(q_u\). If the positive occurrence is split as \(u=ps\), the two based boundary loops have the forms

\[
 sTp\quad\hbox{and}\quad p^{-1}T^{-1}s^{-1}.
\]

These are inverse loops. The joining track is constant, so introduces no conjugator. A small disk neighborhood of the two relator disks and this one track has boundary trivial in \(L\). Filling that boundary without relator disks removes two factors, a contradiction. Other tracks may cross the boundary of the replaced region; their continuation imposes no condition on the replacement map, since only its boundary map is fixed.

Two disks from different relators have at most one joining edge, by the shared-generator assumption and the uniqueness of each occurrence. Consequently there are no parallel edges between inner vertices.

Finally, colour every inner vertex by the sign of its relator disk. Every inner-to-inner edge joins opposite colours. Thus the subgraph on inner vertices is bipartite. In particular, any face walk avoiding \(O\) has even length, including a walk with repeated vertices or edges. This is the additional restriction used below.

## 3. Face equations and matching gaps

A regular neighborhood of a connected graph embedded in the sphere has complementary components that are disks. Their boundary curves follow the face walks, with multiplicity where an edge or vertex repeats. These disks still carry the original map to \(K\), including any closed tracks inside them.

Collapse all exterior circles to the basepoint and evaluate the remaining loops in \(H\). Tracks become constant, and each sector between successive exterior occurrences contributes its coefficient gap. Since a complementary disk fills the resulting loop, its cyclically ordered sector product is \(1\) in \(H\).

There are no monogon faces. An inner monogon would require an inner loop. A monogon at \(O\) would have opposite exterior signs at its two endpoints and a trivial coefficient gap by the face equation. The corresponding successive exterior letters of \(W\) would cancel, contrary to cyclic \(H\)-reduction.

Every bigon incident to an inner vertex consists of two distinct edges joining that vertex to \(O\). Parallel inner-to-inner edges were excluded above. A length-two face walk traversing the same edge twice requires a degree-one endpoint at every inner vertex on that walk: in the cyclic rotation there can be no intervening half-edge. This contradicts degree at least four. Bigons wholly at \(O\), if present, will have weight zero and need no interpretation.

An inner-to-outer bigon supplies a match of two successive exterior letters and their gap. The signs agree when \(W\), rather than \(W^{-1}\), is read along the outer sector. If the inner segment is \(uAv\) and the corresponding outer segment in \(W\) is \(uBv\), the face equation, in one orientation, is

\[
 [A]_H[B]_H^{-1}=1.
\]

Indeed the two sides of each track map to the same point \(q_u\) or \(q_v\); after collapsing the exterior circles there is no intervening basepoint path. Hence \([A]_H=[B]_H\), with equality of elements rather than merely conjugacy. Reversing the orientation gives the inverse equation and the same conclusion. Consecutive bigons concatenate these matches in order. No equality of the spelling lengths of \(A\) and \(B\) was used.

## 4. Corner weights

Give weight zero to all corners at \(O\) and to all corners of bigons. At any other inner corner give weight

\[
 \begin{cases}
 1,&\text{if both incident edges lead to }O,\\
 \tfrac12,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}                                               \tag{2}
\]

Count corner occurrences along face walks, even if a vertex repeats. A face of length \(\ell\) has total weight at most \(\ell-2\):

* For a bigon the total weight is zero.
* If a nonbigon face avoids \(O\), its walk has even length and cannot have length two, so \(\ell\ge4\). Each corner has weight \(1/2\), giving \(\ell/2\le\ell-2\).
* If a face has exactly one occurrence of \(O\), then \(\ell\ge3\). Every inner corner has at most one incident edge leading to \(O\), so its total weight is \((\ell-1)/2\le\ell-2\).
* If a face has at least two occurrences of \(O\), it has at most \(\ell-2\) inner corners, each of weight at most one.

These cases also cover outer loops and faces traversing bridges twice.

Write \(c(v)\) for the weight sum at an inner vertex, and \(E,F\) for the numbers of edges and faces. The face lengths sum to \(2E\). Euler's formula with \(N+1\) vertices gives

\[
 \sum_{v\ne O}c(v)\le2E-2F=2N-2,
 \qquad
 \sum_{v\ne O}(2-c(v))\ge2.                               \tag{3}
\]

Call a vertex positive when \(2-c(v)>0\). A vertex with no edge to \(O\) has no bigon corners and has

\[
 c(v)=\frac{m(r)}2\ge2.
\]

Thus every positive vertex has an edge to \(O\).

Fix such a vertex \(v\). List its edges to \(O\) cyclically. Between successive such edges, a gap containing no inner edge costs zero if it is a bigon corner, and otherwise costs one. A gap containing \(h\ge1\) inner edges consists of two mixed corners and \(h-1\) inner-inner corners. None belongs to a bigon, and each has weight \(1/2\). Its cost is

\[
 \frac12+\frac{h-1}2+\frac12=\frac{h+1}2.                  \tag{4}
\]

In particular every gap that is not a bigon costs at least one.

If all gaps at \(v\) are bigons, there are no inner edges at \(v\). Successive edges at \(v\) are successive at \(O\) in the opposite rotation. These incidences exhaust the rotation at \(O\): any further half-edge would interrupt a required bigon. Connectedness then leaves only \(v\) and \(O\). The bigon equations give a full cyclic match, including the closing coefficient gap.

In the remaining case a positive vertex has exactly one nonbigon gap. Two would cost at least two. If that unique gap contains inner edges, it has at most two of them, since \(h\ge3\) in (4) already costs at least two. All remaining gaps are a single string of bigons. If \(m=m(r)\), the string therefore joins at least \(m-2\) edges to \(O\). It gives one matching interval in \(W\) of that many exterior occurrences, using distinct positions of the relator. When the exceptional gap has no inner edge, the interval uses all \(m\) exterior positions and omits only the exceptional closing gap.

There is at least one nonbigon gap in this case, so \(c(v)\ge1\). Every positive vertex consequently contributes at most one to the sum in (3). Unless a full cyclic match has already occurred, (3) forces at least two distinct positive vertices. An outer occurrence is the endpoint of just one arc, so the matching intervals from those vertices have disjoint exterior occurrences. This proves (1) and the theorem.

## 5. The loss of two is necessary

For every integer \(m\ge4\), take \(H=1\). Choose distinct generators \(a,b,c,d\) and four disjoint private alphabets, also disjoint from these four. Let \(A_i\) be a positive word of length \(m-2\) using each letter of private alphabet \(i\) exactly once. Put

\[
 r_0=adA_0,\qquad r_1=A_1ab,\qquad
 r_2=cbA_2,\qquad r_3=A_3cd.                              \tag{5}
\]

Every relator has \(m\) distinct positive letters. Neighboring relators in the cycle \(0,1,2,3,0\) share respectively \(a,b,c,d\); opposite ones share none. Thus every pair shares at most one generator. The coefficient embedding holds since \(H=1\).

In the quotient by these relators,

\[
 A_0=d^{-1}a^{-1},\qquad A_1^{-1}=ab,\qquad
 A_2=b^{-1}c^{-1},\qquad A_3^{-1}=cd.
\]

Consequently the cyclic word

\[
 W=A_0A_1^{-1}A_2A_3^{-1}                                 \tag{6}
\]

is trivial: its value is

\[
 d^{-1}a^{-1}ab\,b^{-1}c^{-1}cd=1.
\]

All private generators are distinct, so \(W\) is freely and cyclically reduced. Its length is \(4(m-2)>m\), precluding a full cyclic match. The only generators of \(r_i\) that occur in \(W\) are the \(m-2\) private letters of \(A_i\). A matching interval for \(r_i\) or \(r_i^{-1}\), with no relator position used twice, therefore has at most \(m-2\) letters. In particular there is no matching interval of length \(m-1\), let alone two. Each of the four displayed blocks in (6) does give a match of length \(m-2\). This proves that the bound (1) cannot be increased uniformly, even with trivial coefficients and arbitrarily large \(m\).

For a direct check of the smallest instance, let \(A_i=p_iq_i\), with eight different private generators. The relators become \(adp_0q_0\), \(p_1q_1ab\), \(cbp_2q_2\), and \(p_3q_3cd\). The word is \(p_0q_0q_1^{-1}p_1^{-1}p_2q_2q_3^{-1}p_3^{-1}\). The four substitutions above cancel to the empty word. Each relator has just two of its four generators in this word, independently ruling out every three-letter match.

## 6. Checks and the rank application

The Euler constant can be checked by a spanning tree. A spanning tree of \(G\) has \(N\) edges and one complementary face. Each of the remaining \(E-N\) edges splits a face, so \(F=1+E-N\). Consequently \(2E-2F=2N-2\), agreeing with (3), including outer loops and bridges.

The loss of two in (1) can be checked by counting corners directly: a gap with \(h\) inner edges has \(h+1\) corners of weight \(1/2\). Positive contribution requires \(h+1<4\), or \(h\le2\). The borderline \(h=3\) gives contribution zero and cannot be selected. A vertex with \(m=4\) and no outer edge also has contribution zero, which suffices: no strict negative contribution was needed there.

For a coefficient check, take \(H=\langle s\mid s^2=1\rangle\) and the single relator \(r=u_1s u_2u_3u_4\). Eliminating \(u_4\) identifies the quotient with \(H*F(u_1,u_2,u_3)\), so the coefficient embedding holds. The word \(W=u_1s^3u_2u_3u_4\) is cyclically \(H\)-reduced, is trivial in the quotient, and has a full cyclic match with \(r\). Its gap spellings have different lengths, but their values agree. This checks that the conclusion is about elements of \(H\).

The argument does not require a finite presentation, torsion-freeness or commutativity of \(H\). Injectivity is used when excluding a component detached from \(O\). Positivity of exterior letters is used both to exclude inner loops and to make the inner graph bipartite. Dropping that sign hypothesis would permit inner triangles and invalidate the weight \(1/2\) in (2).

For the mixed rows of the rank argument, \(m\ge91\). In its two-interval alternative, this lemma gives exterior-disjoint intervals of length at least \(89\), rather than \(88\). If a subsequent seam argument preserves one such interval, its matching segment contains at least 89 original letters of a length-100 row, leaving at most 11 in the complementary word. The full-match alternative must also be handled in that application. These are conditional numerical consequences, not a verification of the seam argument or of the graph-shortening theorem.
