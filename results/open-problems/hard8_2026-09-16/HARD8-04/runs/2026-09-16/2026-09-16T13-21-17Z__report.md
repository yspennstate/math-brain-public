## 3. Complete quotient reduction for `d=6`

Assume `6|v` and write

\[
v=6s,\qquad s\ge1.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Project each putative block in (1.1) from `Z_v` to `Z_6`. Let

\[
n_i=(n_i(0),\ldots,n_i(5))\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^6
\]

be the six fiber occupancies. Each quotient fiber has size `s`, so a genuine occupancy certificate must satisfy

\[
0\le n_i(r)\le s.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The row sums are

\[
\sum_rn_1(r)=3s-1,
\qquad
\sum_rn_i(r)=3s\quad(i=2,3,4).
\tag{3.3}
\]

For `a=1,2,3`, define

\[
Q_a(n)=\frac12\sum_{r\in\mathbb Z_6}(n(r)-n(r+a))^2.
\tag{3.4}
\]

If

\[
C_n(a)=\sum_rn(r)n(r+a),
\]

then `Q_a(n)=C_n(0)-C_n(a)`.

For a valid difference family, every nonzero quotient shift has `s` lifts to `Z_v`, all nonzero. Therefore

\[
A_a:=\sum_iC_{n_i}(a)=s(6s-2)=6s^2-2s
\qquad(a=1,\ldots,5).
\tag{3.5}
\]

For quotient shift zero, one of the `s` lifts is the zero difference and the other `s-1` lifts are nonzero, giving

\[
A_0=(12s-1)+(s-1)(6s-2)=6s^2+4s+1.
\tag{3.6}
\]

Thus the complete quotient conditions imply

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i=1}^4Q_1(n_i)
=
\sum_{i=1}^4Q_2(n_i)
=
\sum_{i=1}^4Q_3(n_i)
=6s+1.
}
\tag{3.7}
\]

Conversely, (3.3) and (3.7) imply the complete quotient autocorrelation system. Indeed, symmetry gives `A_5=A_1` and `A_4=A_2`; (3.7) makes all five nonzero `A_a` equal to `A_0-(6s+1)`. Also

\[
\sum_{a=0}^5A_a
=(3s-1)^2+3(3s)^2
=36s^2-6s+1.
\]

Hence

\[
6A_0-5(6s+1)=36s^2-6s+1,
\]

which solves to (3.6), and then every nonzero `A_a` is (3.5).

Therefore order six is exactly the bounded-integer problem (3.2), (3.3), (3.7).

## 4. One-exception block lemma

For integers `N,a` with

\[
a\equiv N\pmod6,
\]

put

\[
b=\frac{N-a}{6}
\]

and define

\[
B_N(a)=(b+a,b,b,b,b,b).
\tag{4.1}
\]

Then

\[
\sum_rB_N(a)_r=N
\]

and, because exactly one coordinate differs from the other five by `a`, for every nonzero shift class represented by `1,2,3`,

\[
\boxed{Q_1(B_N(a))=Q_2(B_N(a))=Q_3(B_N(a))=a^2.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Thus a `d=6` quotient certificate follows whenever one can find integers

\[
a_1\equiv3s-1\pmod6,
\qquad
a_2,a_3,a_4\equiv3s\pmod6,
\tag{4.3}
\]

such that

\[
a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2+a_4^2=6s+1,
\tag{4.4}
\]

and every entry of the four vectors `B_{3s-1}(a_1),B_{3s}(a_2),B_{3s}(a_3),B_{3s}(a_4)` lies in `[0,s]`.

The arithmetic below proves this for all but finitely many `s`; the finite residue is handled by explicit exact certificates in Section 7.

## 5. Even `s`: reduction to Legendre three squares

Write

\[
s=2t.
\]

Set

\[
a_1=6k-1,
\qquad
a_j=6x_j\quad(j=2,3,4).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Then (4.4) is equivalent to

\[
\boxed{
t=k(3k-1)+3(x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2).
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Let

\[
\mathcal F=\{4^a(8b+7):a,b\ge0\}
\]

be the Legendre-forbidden set. If two positive integers in `F` have the same parity, then their difference is divisible by `8` when they are odd and divisible by `4` when they are even. Consequently two members of `F` can never differ by an even number congruent to `2 mod 4`; in particular they cannot differ by `2`, `10`, or `14`.

We choose `k` by `t mod 3`.

### Case 5.1: `t=3u`

Use either

\[
k=3,\quad R=u-8,
\]

or

\[
k=-3,\quad R=u-10.
\]

For `u>=10` both candidates are nonnegative and differ by `2`, so at least one is not in `F`. Legendre then gives

\[
R=x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2.
\]

### Case 5.2: `t=3u+1`

Use either

\[
k=-1,\quad R=u-1,
\]

or

\[
k=2,\quad R=u-3.
\]

For `u>=3` both are nonnegative and differ by `2`, so one is a sum of three squares.

### Case 5.3: `t=3u+2`

Use either

\[
k=1,\quad R=u,
\]

or

\[
k=4,\quad R=u-14.
\]

For `u>=14` both are nonnegative and differ by `14`, so one is a sum of three squares.

In all three cases `R<=t/3`. Therefore every Legendre representation obeys

\[
|x_j|\le\sqrt{R}\le\sqrt{t/3}\le t/5
\]

throughout the stated ranges (all have `t>=10`). For a regular block,

\[
B_{6t}(6x)=(t+5x,t-x,t-x,t-x,t-x,t-x),
\]

and `|x|<=t/5` places every coordinate in `[0,2t]=[0,s]`.

For the first block,

\[
B_{6t-1}(6k-1)=(t+5k-1,t-k,t-k,t-k,t-k,t-k).
\]

The allowed values `k in {-3,3,-1,2,1,4}` together with the corresponding thresholds `t>=30`, `t>=10`, `t>=44` make these coordinates lie in `[0,2t]` directly.

Hence every even `s` is covered except the finite set obtained before these thresholds:

\[
\begin{aligned}
E_{\rm fin}=\{&2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16,18,22,24,28,30,34,36,40,42,46,48,52,54,58,64,70,76,82\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Section 7 gives exact certificates for all of them.

## 6. Odd `s`: a triangular-number corollary of Legendre

Write

\[
s=2t+1.
\]

Set

\[
a_1=6k+2,
\qquad
a_j=6x_j+3\quad(j=2,3,4).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Then (4.4) is equivalent to

\[
\boxed{
t=3k^2+2k+2+6\sum_{j=2}^4T_{x_j},
\qquad
T_x=\frac{x(x+1)}2.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Choose `k` from the following table.

| `t mod 6` | `k` | `B(k)=3k^2+2k+2` | first admissible `t` |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
|0|2|18|18|
|1|1|7|7|
|2|0|2|2|
|3|-1|3|3|
|4|-2|10|10|
|5|-3|23|23|

For every `t` at or above the displayed threshold in its residue class,

\[
L=\frac{t-B(k)}6
\]

is a nonnegative integer.

Now `8L+3` is congruent to `3 mod 8`, so it is not of Legendre's forbidden shape. Hence

\[
8L+3=y_2^2+y_3^2+y_4^2.
\]

Modulo eight, three squares can sum to `3` only if all three are odd. Write `|y_j|=2x_j+1` with `x_j>=0`. Then

\[
8L+3
=\sum_j(8T_{x_j}+1),
\]

and therefore

\[
L=T_{x_2}+T_{x_3}+T_{x_4}.
\]

This derives the needed three-triangular-number representation from Legendre rather than taking it as an additional dependency.

The regular block has

\[
B_{6t+3}(6x+3)=(t+5x+3,t-x,t-x,t-x,t-x,t-x).
\]

Since `B(k)>=2`,

\[
T_x\le L\le\frac{t-2}{6}.
\]

For `x>=1`, this gives

\[
5x\le3x(x+1)\le t-2,
\]

and the `x=0` case is immediate. Thus all regular-block entries lie in `[0,2t+1]=[0,s]`.

The first block is

\[
B_{6t+2}(6k+2)=(t+5k+2,t-k,t-k,t-k,t-k,t-k).
\]

At the first admissible `t` in each table row, and hence for every larger `t` in that residue class, both displayed coordinates lie in `[0,2t+1]`.

The only odd `s` not covered by this uniform argument are

\[
O_{\rm fin}=\{1,3,9,11,13,23,25,35\}.
\tag{6.3}
\]

Section 7 supplies exact certificates for all eight.

## 8. Main theorem of this run

### Theorem 8.1 — universal feasibility of the complete `d=6` quotient system

For every positive integer `s`, the complete quotient occupancy equations modulo six for the bordered cyclic parameter family

\[
(6s;3s-1,3s,3s,3s;6s-2)
\]

have a feasible integer solution satisfying the genuine fiber bounds

\[
0\le n_i(r)\le s.
\]

Equivalently, quotient order six can never by itself obstruct the stronger bordered cyclic route.

#### Proof

Section 3 proves that the complete quotient system is equivalent to (3.2), (3.3), (3.7). Sections 5 and 6 construct one-exception certificates for every `s` outside the finite sets (5.3), (6.3). Section 7 gives explicit exact certificates for every member of those finite sets. Therefore every `s>=1` has a complete valid quotient certificate. `□`

Combining Theorem 8.1 with the preceding verified checkpoint gives

\[
\boxed{
\text{The complete single-quotient occupancy systems of orders }d=2,3,4,5,6
\text{ are universally feasible for the bordered target.}
}
\tag{8.1}
\]

This is a universal theorem about the projected integer systems, not a finite-range empirical observation.
