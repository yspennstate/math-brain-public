## 2. Black-box pair-inequality gadgets

For `d>0`, call a finite planar unit-distance graph `F_d^(5)` a **5-colour inequality gadget at separation d** if it has distinguished vertices `s,t` with Euclidean distance `d`, is 5-colourable, and every proper colouring using at most five colours satisfies
\[
c(s)\ne c(t).
\]

The phrase *black-box pair model* below means that a copy of such a gadget is used only through this guaranteed terminal relation. Interactions among internal vertices of several copies could in principle impose extra constraints; those extra interactions are not credited in the counting lower bounds below. Thus the lower bounds are exact for the pairwise-terminal certificate architecture, not for every imaginable geometric composition.

## 3. Exact six-vertex optimum: any pairwise terminal proof needs at least six non-unit constraints

Let `P={p_1,...,p_6}` be six distinct planar marked points, and let `U(P)` be the graph on these six terminals whose edges are exactly the terminal pairs at Euclidean distance 1.

Suppose we attach 5-colour inequality gadgets to some non-unit terminal pairs. Let `F` be the set of such certified terminal inequalities. If the argument is to rule out a 5-colouring **solely from the terminal pair constraints**, then the graph
\[
C=(P,E(U(P))\cup F)
\]
must have chromatic number at least 6.

But `C` has exactly six vertices. Therefore
\[
\chi(C)\ge6 \iff C=K_6.
\]
Hence every non-unit terminal pair must be certified different, and the number of required gadget pairs is exactly
\[
\binom 62-|E(U(P))|=15-|E(U(P))|.
\]
By the exact small unit-distance value `u(6)=9`,
\[
|E(U(P))|\le9.
\]
Therefore:

### Theorem 3.1 — six-gadget lower bound for every six-terminal black-box scaffold

Every six-terminal black-box pair-inequality construction proving non-5-colourability needs at least
\[
\boxed{15-9=6}
\]
non-unit terminal inequality constraints.

This lower bound is **attained** by an explicit exact planar configuration in Section 4. Thus six is optimal within the entire six-terminal pairwise-gadget architecture.

This is stronger than the preceding checkpoint's regular-hexagon scaffold, which had only six unit terminal pairs and therefore required nine non-unit inequalities.

## 4. An optimal exact scaffold with nine unit pairs

Take a regular hexagon of side length one, with outer vertices
\[
v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_5
\]
in cyclic order, and center `o`. Retain only the six marked points
\[
S=\{o,v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4,v_5\};
\]
in other words delete the single outer vertex `v_0`.

Because the circumradius of a regular unit hexagon is one, the following terminal pairs are already at unit distance:

- the five spokes `ov_i`, `1<=i<=5`;
- the four consecutive outer edges `v_1v_2`, `v_2v_3`, `v_3v_4`, `v_4v_5`.

Thus the six marked points determine exactly
\[
\boxed{9}
\]
unit terminal pairs, meeting the extremal value `u(6)=9`.

The remaining six terminal pairs split into exactly two distances:

### separation `sqrt(3)` — four pairs
\[
v_1v_3,\qquad v_1v_5,\qquad v_2v_4,\qquad v_3v_5;
\]

### separation `2` — two pairs
\[
v_1v_4,\qquad v_2v_5.
\]

The squared-distance multiset among the six terminals is therefore
\[
\boxed{1:9,\qquad 3:4,\qquad 4:2.}
\]

### Theorem 4.1 — improved finite-gadget reduction

If both a 5-colour inequality gadget `F_sqrt(3)^(5)` and a 5-colour inequality gadget `F_2^(5)` exist, then a finite planar unit-distance graph with chromatic number at least six exists. It is enough to use only
\[
\boxed{4\text{ copies of }F_{\sqrt3}^{(5)}\quad\text{and}\quad2\text{ copies of }F_2^{(5)}}.
\]

#### Proof

Start with the six marked points `S` above. Keep their nine unit-distance edges. Attach a copy of `F_sqrt(3)^(5)` to each of the four terminal pairs at separation `sqrt(3)`, and a copy of `F_2^(5)` to each of the two terminal pairs at separation `2`, identifying each gadget's distinguished terminals with the prescribed marked pair.

Every edge in every gadget is represented by a Euclidean unit segment after the corresponding isometry. If vertices from different copies happen to coincide geometrically, identify the coincident points; this can only add identifications/constraints and does not invalidate the terminal implication inherited from each gadget.

Assume the resulting finite unit-distance graph had a proper 5-colouring. Restrict it to the six marked points. The nine unit pairs have different colours because they are edges. Each of the other six pairs has different colours by the attached 5-colour inequality gadget. Therefore every pair among the six marked points has different colours. That would require six colours, contradiction.

Hence the resulting finite unit-distance graph is not 5-colourable. QED.

### Optimality inside the six-terminal black-box model

Theorem 3.1 shows that no six-terminal pairwise-inequality scaffold can use fewer than six non-unit pair constraints. Therefore the construction above is not merely an improvement from 9 gadgets to 6; it is **optimal in gadget count among all possible six-terminal black-box pairwise constructions**.

Furthermore, for this particular extremal geometry every optimal six-gadget completion necessarily constrains all six missing terminal pairs, so the distance-type demand is rigid:
\[
\boxed{4\times \sqrt3\quad+\quad2\times 2.}
\]
A gadget at only one of those two separations cannot complete this scaffold to the required terminal `K_6`.

## 5. Seven terminals cannot beat six gadgets in the same black-box architecture

A priori, adding a seventh marked point could allow a 6-chromatic terminal constraint graph without completing a `K_6`, and might therefore reduce the number of attached gadgets. The next exact argument shows that it cannot reduce the count below six.

### Lemma 5.1 — exact chromatic criterion on seven vertices

Let `G` be a graph on seven vertices. Then
\[
\boxed{\chi(G)\ge6}
\]
if and only if its complement `\bar G` is a subgraph of a star (including the empty graph).

#### Proof

A 5-colouring of a seven-vertex graph saves at least two colours relative to giving every vertex its own colour. Such a saving is possible exactly when either:

1. there are two disjoint nonedges of `G`, which can be made into two independent colour-pairs; or
2. there is an independent triple of `G`, which can be made into one colour class of size three.

In the complement, these conditions are respectively a matching of size two and a triangle. Hence `G` is not 5-colourable exactly when `\bar G` has neither a two-edge matching nor a triangle.

A family of 2-element sets with no two disjoint members is pairwise intersecting. Such a graph is either contained in a star, or contains a triangle: if edges `ab` and `ac` are present and some edge does not contain `a`, pairwise intersection forces that edge to be `bc`, producing a triangle. Excluding triangles leaves precisely star subgraphs. QED.

Now let `P` be seven planar terminals and `U(P)` their unit-distance graph. Let `N=\overline{U(P)}` be the graph of non-unit terminal pairs. In the black-box model choose `F\subseteq E(N)` to certify as additional inequalities. The complement of the terminal constraint graph is `N-F`.

By Lemma 5.1, the best possible strategy is to leave unconstrained all non-unit pairs incident with one selected terminal `v`, and constrain every other non-unit pair. Therefore the exact minimum number of black-box pair constraints is
\[
\boxed{|E(N)|-\Delta(N)}.
\]
Since
\[
|E(N)|=21-|E(U)|,
\]
and
\[
\Delta(N)=6-\delta(U),
\]
this becomes
\[
\boxed{15-|E(U)|+\delta(U).}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Choose a minimum-degree terminal `v` in `U`. Deleting `v` leaves six planar points and exactly
\[
|E(U)|-\delta(U)
\]
unit pairs among them. The exact value `u(6)=9` therefore gives
\[
|E(U)|-\delta(U)\le9.
\]
Substituting into (5.1):
\[
\boxed{15-|E(U)|+\delta(U)\ge6.}
\]

### Theorem 5.2 — seven-terminal optimum

No seven-terminal black-box pair-inequality scaffold can certify non-5-colourability using fewer than six non-unit terminal constraints.

Combined with Theorem 4.1, six is therefore optimal across **all black-box pairwise scaffolds with at most seven marked terminals**.

This does not rule out a construction in which a single finite gadget enforces a genuinely higher-arity relation among several marked points. Such a gadget would lie outside the black-box pair model and is a legitimate way around the theorem.

## 6. Relation to the seven-point three-distance classification

Masashi Shinohara's 2004 classification of planar three-distance sets with at least five points proves that a maximum planar three-distance set has seven points and that, up to similarity, the two seven-point examples are:

1. the regular heptagon `R_7`;
2. the regular hexagon together with its center `R_6^+`.

Reference: M. Shinohara, *Classification of three-distance sets in two dimensional Euclidean space*, European Journal of Combinatorics 25 (2004), 1039–1058, DOI 10.1016/j.ejc.2003.12.009.

For these two geometries the exact black-box counts from (5.1) are:

- regular heptagon, scaling any one of its three distance classes to unit: `|E(U)|=7`, `delta(U)=2`, hence **10** added pair constraints;
- `R_6^+` with side/radius as the unit distance: `|E(U)|=12`, `delta(U)=3`, hence **6** added pair constraints;
- `R_6^+` with its `sqrt(3)` class scaled to unit: `|E(U)|=6`, `delta(U)=0`, hence **9** added pair constraints;
- `R_6^+` with its diameter class scaled to unit: `|E(U)|=3`, `delta(U)=0`, hence **12** added pair constraints.

Thus the unit regular-hexagon-plus-center geometry is the unique best choice among the classified maximum planar three-distance sets under this pairwise criterion. Its optimal six-constraint certificate is exactly the six-point construction of Section 4 obtained by sacrificing one outer hexagon vertex.

## 7. What this changes in the search target

The preceding checkpoint's lower-bound route asked for enough 5-colour forced-difference information to complete a regular hexagon, requiring nine gadget copies. The optimal reduction is now strictly smaller:

\[
\boxed{4\text{ forced-different }\sqrt3\text{ pairs}+2\text{ forced-different distance-2 pairs}.}
\]

This does **not** weaken the qualitative requirement that both prescribed separations be available, but it reduces the number of copies by one third and proves that no six- or seven-terminal black-box pair construction can do better in total gadget count.

The next finite searches should therefore prioritize:

1. finding a 5-colour forced-different pair at distance `sqrt(3)` in an exact known 5-chromatic geometry;
2. finding a 5-colour forced-different pair at distance `2`;
3. if one of those two pair types appears structurally impossible, abandoning pairwise gadgets and searching directly for a **higher-arity** 5-colour terminal relation, since Theorems 3.1 and 5.2 show exactly where pairwise scaffolds hit their finite combinatorial floor.

For a candidate pair `(s,t)` in a 5-colourable finite unit-distance graph, the exact certificate remains: add the equality constraint `c(s)=c(t)` to the 5-colouring CNF and certify UNSAT (preferably DRAT/LRAT), while separately checking the planar coordinates and every claimed unit edge by exact algebra.
