## 2. A terminal-criticality theorem

The direct pair-mining strategy from the previous checkpoint has a strong obstruction that was not made explicit there.

### Definition

Let `G` be `k`-colorable. Distinct nonadjacent vertices `u,v` form a **forced-equal pair under k colors** (a `k`-mono pair) if every proper coloring
\[
c:V(G)\to [k]
\]
satisfies `c(u)=c(v)`. Colors are available labels; the coloring need not use every color.

### Theorem 2.1 — forced-equal terminals are noncritical

If `u,v` are a forced-equal pair under `k` colors in a `k`-colorable graph `G`, then
\[
\chi(G)=k,
\qquad
\chi(G-u)=k,
\qquad
\chi(G-v)=k.
\]
In particular, neither terminal can be a vertex whose deletion lowers the chromatic number below `k`.

#### Proof

First suppose `chi(G)<=k-1`. Take a proper coloring of `G` with colors `1,...,k-1`. Recolor only `u` with the fresh color `k`. This remains proper, because no other vertex has color `k`, and now `c(u) != c(v)`. This contradicts forced equality. Hence `chi(G)=k`.

Now suppose `chi(G-u)<=k-1`. Properly color `G-u` using colors `1,...,k-1`, then assign the fresh color `k` to `u`. Again the extension is proper and has `c(u)!=c(v)`, contradiction. Therefore `chi(G-u)>=k`; since `G-u` is a subgraph of the `k`-colorable graph `G`, equality holds. The argument for `v` is identical. QED.

### Corollary 2.2 — no equality pair inside a vertex-critical core

A vertex-critical `k`-chromatic graph contains no forced-equal pair under `k` colors.

This is immediate from Theorem 2.1.

### Corollary 2.3 — no equality pair inside an edge-critical k-chromatic graph

Assume `G` is `k`-chromatic and deleting any edge makes it `(k-1)`-colorable. Then `G` contains no forced-equal pair under `k` colors.

#### Proof

A forced-equal terminal `u` cannot be isolated: if it were, recolor it arbitrarily to a color different from `v`. Thus `u` has an incident edge `e`. By edge-criticality, `G-e` is `(k-1)`-colorable. But `G-u` is a subgraph of `G-e`, so `G-u` is also `(k-1)`-colorable, contradicting Theorem 2.1. QED.

This is directly relevant to the 509-vertex search target. The independently verified 509-vertex/2259-edge reduced graph is reported edge-critical. Therefore **that reduced graph cannot contain any forced-equal vertex pair under five colors**. The pairwise SAT mining proposed in checkpoint 2 is mathematically unnecessary for this graph: every pair must fail.

The conclusion is not that equality gadgets cannot be built from the 509 geometry. It says they must live in a **noncritical overgraph** of a 5-chromatic core, rather than wholly inside an edge-critical/vertex-critical core.

## 3. Degree and rainbow constraints on any 5-color equality gadget

Theorem 2.1 has a local strengthening.

### Theorem 3.1 — terminal minimum degree

If `u,v` form a forced-equal pair under `k` colors in a `k`-colorable graph, then
\[
\deg(u),\deg(v)\ge k-1.
\]

#### Proof

By Theorem 2.1, `G-u` is `k`-chromatic and therefore has at least one proper `k`-coloring `c`. If `deg(u)<=k-2`, then the neighbors of `u` use at most `k-2` colors. Consequently at least two colors are absent from `N(u)`. At most one of those two equals `c(v)`, so another available color `gamma != c(v)` can be assigned to `u`. This extends `c` to a proper `k`-coloring of `G` with `c(u)!=c(v)`, contradiction. QED.

For the Hadwiger–Nelson `k=5` target, each equality terminal must therefore have degree at least four.

### Theorem 3.2 — degree-(k-1) terminals force a universal rainbow neighborhood

Under the same hypotheses, if `deg(u)=k-1`, then in **every** proper `k`-coloring of `G-u`:

1. the `k-1` neighbors of `u` receive `k-1` distinct colors; and
2. the unique color missing from `N(u)` is exactly the color of `v`.

#### Proof

Fix a proper `k`-coloring of `G-u`. Let `A` be the colors absent from `N(u)`. Every color in `A` can be assigned to `u`. Forced equality requires every such extension to give `u` the color of `v`; hence `A` is the singleton `{c(v)}`. Therefore exactly one color is absent and the `k-1` neighbors collectively use all other `k-1` colors. Since there are only `k-1` neighbors, they use those colors exactly once each. QED.

Thus a degree-4 terminal in a 5-color equality gadget must see a set of four vertices that is forced to be rainbow in every five-coloring of the terminal-deleted graph, with the partner terminal always carrying the missing fifth color. This supplies a much sharper SAT/geometric filter than arbitrary pair testing.

## 4. Geometric obstruction to the naive local equality gadget

For abstract graph coloring, the most obvious equality gadget is `K_{k+1}` with the edge between the two terminals removed: the other `k-1` vertices use all other colors, forcing the terminals equal. At `k=5`, this would require two terminals with four common neighbors.

### Lemma 4.1 — two planar unit-distance vertices have at most two common unit neighbors

Let `u != v` be distinct points in the Euclidean plane. A common unit-distance neighbor `w` must satisfy
\[
|w-u|=|w-v|=1,
\]
so `w` lies in the intersection of the two unit circles centered at `u` and `v`. Two distinct circles have at most two intersection points. Hence `u,v` have at most two distinct common unit neighbors in any planar unit-distance embedding. QED.

Therefore the direct `K_6-uv` mechanism is geometrically impossible. More generally, any 5-color equality construction that tries to force equality solely through four shared unit neighbors cannot work in the plane. A successful gadget must propagate color information through a larger nonlocal unit-distance structure.

This helps explain the qualitative jump from the familiar 3-color rhombus gadget (`K_4` minus an edge), where two shared common neighbors are geometrically possible, to the present 5-color frontier.

## 5. Exact core-attachment criterion

The previous facts suggest a constructive architecture: keep a 5-chromatic core and add noncritical terminals around it.

### Theorem 5.1 — exact criterion for two terminals attached to a fixed k-chromatic core

Let `C` be a `k`-chromatic graph. Form `G` by adding two nonadjacent new vertices `u,v`, with all their neighbors lying in `C`; write
\[
A=N(u)\cap V(C),\qquad B=N(v)\cap V(C).
\]
For a proper `k`-coloring `c` of `C`, define the available-color sets
\[
L_A(c)=[k]\setminus c(A),
\qquad
L_B(c)=[k]\setminus c(B).
\]
Then `u,v` are forced equal in every proper `k`-coloring of `G` if and only if, for every proper `k`-coloring `c` of `C`, there is a color `gamma(c)` such that
\[
L_A(c)=L_B(c)=\{\gamma(c)\}.
\]

#### Proof

For a fixed coloring `c` of `C`, a color can be assigned to `u` exactly when it lies in `L_A(c)`, and similarly for `v`. Because `u,v` are nonadjacent and have no other new neighbors, their choices are independent. Thus the possible terminal-color pairs extending `c` are exactly
\[
L_A(c)\times L_B(c).
\]
Every such pair lies on the equality diagonal if and only if both nonempty sets are the same singleton. Since this must hold for every coloring `c` of `C`, the stated condition is necessary and sufficient. QED.

### Consequences for a geometric search

At `k=5`, each proposed terminal neighborhood must, in every 5-coloring of the core, hit exactly four colors and omit one. Two terminals are an equality pair exactly when their **missing-color functions agree for every core coloring**.

This converts the next computational search into a smaller, structured problem:

1. start with an exact 5-chromatic unit-distance core `C`;
2. enumerate geometric points `p` outside the core that are at unit distance from at least four core vertices;
3. for each such point, set `A_p` equal to its unit-neighbor set in the core;
4. test whether `A_p` has singleton available color in every 5-coloring of `C`;
5. identify two geometrically distinct candidate points whose singleton missing-color functions agree identically.

Any point having at least two core unit-neighbors is an intersection point of two unit circles centered at core vertices, so candidate generation can be done from exact pairwise circle intersections and then incidence-counted. This is far smaller than an unconstrained search over arbitrary coordinates.

## 7. Strategic correction to checkpoint 2

The previous report proposed direct forced-equality pair mining inside the 509-vertex Parts graph. For the independently verified **edge-critical 509-vertex/2259-edge reduction**, Corollary 2.3 proves in advance that this search has no positive answer.

So the next lower-bound search should not spend SAT budget on all internal pairs of that critical core. Instead it should deliberately introduce **noncritical vertices** around a 5-chromatic core.

This is also consistent with the earlier equivalence theorem. If `H` is edge-minimal 6-chromatic and `xy` is a deleted critical edge, then `H-xy` is a 5-color equality gadget. Its equality terminals are necessarily noncritical by Theorem 2.1, while the graph still contains 5-chromatic structure after either terminal is deleted. In other words, a plausible equality gadget naturally looks like a 5-chromatic core plus terminal constraint apparatus, not like a minimal 5-chromatic core by itself.

## 9. Next attack

The highest-value lower-bound attack is now a **noncritical overgraph search**.

1. Use an exact 5-chromatic core with certified geometry (the 509-vertex Parts core is a natural starting point).
2. Generate exact candidate points from intersections of unit circles centered at core vertices; keep only candidates incident to at least four core vertices.
3. For each candidate neighborhood `A`, determine by SAT whether every 5-coloring of the core uses exactly four colors on `A`; when yes, record the induced missing-color function.
4. Search for two distinct geometric candidates with identical missing-color functions. Theorem 5.1 then gives a genuine 5-color equality gadget, and checkpoint 2's spindle composition converts it into a finite non-5-colorable unit-distance graph, proving `chi(R^2)>=6`.
5. Separately inspect Haugland's 2131-vertex graph for noncriticality before any internal-pair search. If it is not vertex/edge-critical, only its noncritical vertices are viable equality terminals.
6. Keep the independent golden-ratio route from checkpoint 2 alive: a pure unit-distance gadget forcing inequality at terminal separation `phi` would still convert Parts's 31-vertex `{1,phi}` 6-chromatic skeleton into a pure unit-distance lower-bound construction.

The central change in this run is therefore not another finite graph record but a rigorous search-space reduction: **forced-equality terminals must sit outside a critical 5-chromatic core, and any direct attachment to that core is characterized exactly by matching singleton missing-color functions.**
