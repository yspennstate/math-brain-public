## 2. General one-distance substitution theorem

The previous checkpoint's optimal six-terminal scaffold made it look as though **both** a `sqrt(3)` gadget and a distance-2 gadget might be needed. That is not true once known six-chromatic two-distance skeletons are used directly.

For `d>0`, call a finite planar unit-distance graph `F_d^(5)` a **five-colour forced-different gadget at separation d** if it has distinguished terminals `s,t` embedded at Euclidean distance `d` and every proper colouring using at most five colours satisfies
\[
c(s)\ne c(t).
\]
If `F_d^(5)` itself is not five-colourable, then it already proves `chi(R^2)>=6`, so the interesting case is when it is five-colourable.

### Theorem 2.1 — distance-edge substitution

Let `G` be a finite planar distance graph whose every edge has Euclidean length either `1` or `d`, and suppose
\[
\chi(G)\ge6.
\]
If a five-colour forced-different unit-distance gadget `F_d^(5)` exists, then a finite planar unit-distance graph that is not five-colourable exists. Consequently
\[
\boxed{\chi(\mathbb R^2)\ge6.}
\]

#### Proof

Start from the fixed geometric embedding of `G`. Retain every length-1 edge. For every length-`d` edge `uv`, place an isometric copy of `F_d^(5)` whose distinguished terminals are identified with `u,v`.

Every edge inside every attached gadget has Euclidean length exactly 1, so the geometric union is a finite unit-distance graph. If different copies produce coincident vertices, identify those geometric points. Incidental extra unit-distance pairs may be included as edges; either operation can only add colouring constraints and does not destroy the terminal implication supplied by any gadget copy.

Suppose the resulting unit-distance graph had a proper five-colouring. Restrict it to the original vertices of `G`. Original unit edges have differently coloured endpoints because they remain unit edges. Every former length-`d` edge has differently coloured endpoints because its attached `F_d^(5)` forces this relation. Thus the restriction is a proper five-colouring of `G`, contradicting `chi(G)>=6`. QED.

### Consequence

A **single** terminal separation can be sufficient. One does not need to discover two different gadget types simultaneously. This is a qualitative strengthening of the previous `4 times sqrt(3) + 2 times 2` reduction, although that six-copy construction remains quantitatively smaller if both gadget types happen to exist.

## 3. Exact `sqrt(3)` skeleton: only 32 copies are needed

The most attractive currently published one-distance target is supplied by De Neve et al.'s 33-vertex graph for
\[
d=\sqrt3.
\]
Their accepted manuscript states that the minimized graph has 33 vertices and 113 total edges and gives exact coordinates in the form
\[
(x,y)=\left(\frac{a_0+a_1\sqrt5}{8},\frac{a_2\sqrt3+a_3\sqrt{15}}{8}\right),
\qquad a_i\in\mathbb Z.
\]

I transcribed all 33 coordinate quadruples from their Table 1 and recomputed every one of the `binom(33,2)=528` squared distances using exact integer arithmetic. If two coordinate quadruples differ by
\[
(p,q,r,s),
\]
then
\[
64\,\|\Delta\|^2
=
(p+q\sqrt5)^2+3(r+s\sqrt5)^2
=A+B\sqrt5,
\]
where
\[
A=p^2+5q^2+3r^2+15s^2,
\qquad
B=2pq+6rs.
\]
Hence distance `1` is exactly the condition `(A,B)=(64,0)`, while distance `sqrt(3)` is exactly `(A,B)=(192,0)`.

The exact recomputation gives
\[
\boxed{81\text{ unit edges}+32\text{ }\sqrt3\text{-edges}=113\text{ total edges}.}
\]
This exactly matches the paper's total edge count, providing a useful transcription check.

### Corollary 3.1 — 32-copy single-gadget reduction

If there exists a five-colour forced-different unit-distance gadget with terminal separation `sqrt(3)`, then attaching it to the 32 `sqrt(3)` edges of De Neve et al.'s exact 33-vertex skeleton yields a finite unit-distance graph that is not five-colourable. Thus
\[
\boxed{F_{\sqrt3}^{(5)}\text{ alone is sufficient, using 32 copies}.}
\]

This is a materially sharper search target than the preceding checkpoint: that checkpoint needed two gadget species; the present reduction needs only the `sqrt(3)` species.

## 4. All 32 `sqrt(3)` constraints are individually essential in that exact skeleton

I then asked whether the 32-copy count can be reduced simply by dropping some `sqrt(3)` edges while retaining all 81 unit edges.

Using deterministic exact graph-colouring backtracking on the 113-edge abstract graph reconstructed from the coordinates:

1. a proper six-colouring was found;
2. exhaustive DSATUR backtracking found **no** proper five-colouring of the full graph, independently reproducing its chromatic lower bound at the abstract-graph level;
3. for every one of the 32 `sqrt(3)` edges `e`, deleting only `e` produced an explicit proper five-colouring.

In every deletion witness the endpoints of the omitted edge receive the same colour, as they must: otherwise that witness would extend to a five-colouring of the full 113-edge graph.

Therefore:

### Theorem 4.1 — relation-criticality of every `sqrt(3)` edge

For the published 33-vertex De Neve et al. skeleton, every one of its 32 `sqrt(3)` edges is individually necessary for the five-colour contradiction when the other 112 edges are kept. Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{G-e\text{ is 5-colourable for every }\sqrt3\text{-edge }e.}
\]

Thus the direct distance-edge-substitution method on this **exact** skeleton cannot use fewer than 32 `sqrt(3)` gadget copies merely by omitting selected non-unit constraints. A smaller one-species substitution proof would need a different six-chromatic `{1,sqrt(3)}` skeleton or additional unit-distance structure coupling several former `sqrt(3)` constraints at once.

The explicit 32 deletion colourings are being archived as a separate text certificate in this run.

## 6. Palette-saturation obstruction for terminal relations

There is a simple but useful reason Haugland's `sqrt(3)` terminal relation at the four-colour level does not automatically solve the new target.

### Lemma 6.1 — a nontrivial binary relation at level `k` requires chromatic number `k`

Let `G` be a graph with nonadjacent terminals `s,t`, and let `k>=2`. If
\[
\chi(G)\le k-1,
\]
then among proper `k`-colourings of `G` there exists both
\[
c(s)=c(t)
\]
and
\[
c(s)\ne c(t).
\]

#### Proof

Take a proper `(k-1)`-colouring and introduce a fresh kth colour. Recolour both nonadjacent terminals with the fresh colour; this gives a proper `k`-colouring with equality. Alternatively recolour only `s` with the fresh colour; this gives a proper `k`-colouring with inequality. QED.

Hence a five-colour forced-different gadget that is itself five-colourable must have
\[
\boxed{\chi(G)=5.}
\]
The same is true for a nontrivial five-colour forced-same gadget.

This explains exactly why a graph that is merely four-colourable cannot enforce a terminal relation against the full five-colour palette. Haugland's 2026 `sqrt(3)` pair forced different in every **four-colouring** is therefore a valuable lower-level template, not the required `F_{sqrt(3)}^(5)`.

There are also convenient exact graph tests. For a five-colourable graph with nonadjacent terminals `s,t`:

- `s,t` are forced **same** in every five-colouring iff adding the edge `st` makes the graph not five-colourable;
- `s,t` are forced **different** in every five-colouring iff contracting `s,t` makes the graph not five-colourable.

These formulations make pair mining on known exact five-chromatic unit-distance graphs a clean SAT/backtracking target.

## 7. A broader forced-same spindle route

The earlier checkpoint proved that any distinct-terminal five-colour forced-same unit-distance gadget can be chained until its terminal separation is at least `1/2`, then doubled into a contradiction. The following direct form is useful computationally.

### Lemma 7.1 — direct spindle for a forced-same pair

Suppose a finite unit-distance graph `U` is five-colourable and has terminals `s,t` at Euclidean separation `r>=1/2`, with
\[
c(s)=c(t)
\]
in every proper five-colouring. Then `chi(R^2)>=6`.

#### Proof

Take two isometric copies of `U`, identify their `s` terminals, and rotate one copy around that common point so the two images `t_1,t_2` satisfy `|t_1-t_2|=1`. This is possible because a chord of length one exists on a circle of radius `r` exactly when `2r>=1`. Add the unit edge `t_1t_2`. Every five-colouring would force
\[
c(t_1)=c(s)=c(t_2),
\]
contradicting the new unit edge. QED.

So the finite search should not focus only on prescribed forced-different separations. A **forced-same pair at any separation at least `1/2`** in an exact five-chromatic unit-distance graph would immediately prove the six-colour lower bound.

The next lower-bound attack should therefore run two searches in parallel conceptually:

If pair gadgets continue to resist, the previous checkpoint's higher-arity-terminal direction remains valid: a multi-terminal gadget can encode several missing inequalities at once and evade the black-box pair-count lower bounds.
