## 3. Carry-forward reduction: the reciprocal trace hard core

As proved in the prior checkpoint, failure of Lehmer would yield a sequence of monic irreducible noncyclotomic reciprocal polynomials
\[
Q(x)=x^D R(x+x^{-1})\in\mathbf Z[x],
\]
with \(R\in\mathbf Z[y]\) monic irreducible of degree \(D\), \(\deg Q=2D\to\infty\), and \(M(Q)\downarrow1\).

Define
\[
C_0(y)=2,\qquad C_1(y)=y,\qquad C_{j+1}(y)=yC_j(y)-C_{j-1}(y).
\]
Then if \(y=x+x^{-1}\),
\[
C_j(y)=x^j+x^{-j}.
\]
For a root \(t\) of \(R\), define
\[
T_m(t)=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m}\varepsilon_j C_j(t):\varepsilon_j\in\{0,1\}\right\}.
\]
The previous run proved that exponential growth of these one-dimensional trace subset sums is equivalent, up to a square-root loss in the growth constant, to the Breuillard–Varjú Bernoulli-sum growth for the reciprocal root \(\alpha\).

# NEW RESULT I — exact collision threshold and a finite exceptional-prime certificate

## 4. Maximal binary trace growth below the trace degree

### Theorem 4.1

Let \(R\in\mathbf Z[y]\) be monic irreducible of degree \(D\), and let \(t\) be any complex root. Then for every \(0\le m<D\),
\[
\boxed{|T_m(t)|=2^{m+1}.}
\]
In other words, **there are no binary trace-subset collisions at any depth strictly below \(\deg R\)**.

### Proof

Suppose two binary vectors \(\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\in\{0,1\}^{m+1}\) produce the same sum. Put
\[
c_j=\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon'_j\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
Then
\[
B_c(t)=0,
\qquad
B_c(y):=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_j C_j(y).
\]
If \(c\ne0\), let \(e\) be the largest index with \(c_e\ne0\). Since \(C_e\) is monic of degree \(e\) for \(e\ge1\), and \(C_0=2\), the polynomial \(B_c\) is nonzero and has degree exactly \(e\le m<D\). But the minimal polynomial of \(t\) is \(R\) of degree \(D\), so no nonzero polynomial of degree less than \(D\) can vanish at \(t\). Hence \(c=0\), so \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon'\). There are therefore all \(2^{m+1}\) distinct sums. ∎

This is only an **initial** maximal-growth window. It does not by itself lower-bound the asymptotic growth rate as \(m\to\infty\), which is the Lehmer-relevant quantity.

## 5. Transfer modulo primes with an exact resultant certificate

For \(m<D\) and a nonzero vector
\[
c=(c_0,\ldots,c_m)\in\{-1,0,1\}^{m+1},
\]
define
\[
B_c(y)=\sum_{j=0}^m c_j C_j(y),
\qquad
N_c:=\operatorname{Res}_y(R,B_c)\in\mathbf Z\setminus\{0\}.
\]
The nonvanishing follows immediately from \(\deg B_c<D=\deg R\) and irreducibility of \(R\).

Define the finite exceptional-prime set
\[
\mathcal E_m(R)
=
\left\{p:\ p\text{ divides at least one }N_c\right\}.
\]

### Theorem 5.1 — collision-free reduction outside \(\mathcal E_m(R)\)

If \(p\notin\mathcal E_m(R)\) and \(\bar t\in\overline{\mathbf F}_p\) is **any** root of \(R\bmod p\), then
\[
\boxed{|T_m(\bar t)|=2^{m+1}.}
\]

### Proof

A collision modulo \(p\) gives a nonzero difference vector \(c\in\{-1,0,1\}^{m+1}\) with
\[
B_c(\bar t)=0.
\]
Thus \(R\) and \(B_c\) have a common root over \(\overline{\mathbf F}_p\), so their resultant is zero modulo \(p\):
\[
p\mid \operatorname{Res}(R,B_c)=N_c.
\]
This contradicts \(p\notin\mathcal E_m(R)\). ∎

## 6. Quantitative bound on the exceptional-prime set

The reciprocal structure gives a particularly clean bound on each \(N_c\).

Let \(e=\deg B_c\le m\) and set
\[
F_c(x)=x^eB_c(x+x^{-1}).
\]
Using \(C_j(x+x^{-1})=x^j+x^{-j}\),
\[
F_c(x)=\sum_{j=0}^e c_j\bigl(x^{e+j}+x^{e-j}\bigr),
\]
where the \(j=0\) contribution is \(2c_0x^e\). Therefore
\[
\|F_c\|_1\le2(e+1)\le2(m+1).
\]

For each root \(\beta_i=\alpha_i+\alpha_i^{-1}\) of \(R\), the corresponding reciprocal pair \(\alpha_i,\alpha_i^{-1}\) is a root pair of \(Q\), and
\[
F_c(\alpha_i)F_c(\alpha_i^{-1})
=B_c(\beta_i)^2.
\]
Hence the exact square-resultant identity gives
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_x(Q,F_c)
=\operatorname{Res}_y(R,B_c)^2.
}
\]
Since \(Q\) is monic of degree \(2D\),
\[
|\operatorname{Res}(Q,F_c)|
=\prod_{Q(\rho)=0}|F_c(\rho)|
\le \|F_c\|_1^{2D}M(Q)^{2e}.
\]
Taking square roots yields
\[
\boxed{
|N_c|
\le [2(m+1)]^D M(Q)^m.
}
\]
Therefore, writing \(\omega(n)\) for the number of distinct rational prime divisors,
\[
\omega(N_c)\le \log_2|N_c|
\le D\log_2(2(m+1))+m\log_2 M(Q),
\]
and a union bound over the at most \(3^{m+1}-1\) nonzero sign vectors gives
\[
\boxed{
|\mathcal E_m(R)|
\le
(3^{m+1}-1)
\left[D\log_2(2(m+1))+m\log_2M(Q)\right].
}
\]

This is an exact finite certificate: outside an explicitly computable finite set of rational primes, *every* reduction root of \(R\) has maximal binary trace growth through depth \(m<D\).

### Why this is potentially useful

For \(m=O(\log D)\), the upper bound is polynomial in \(D\) (up to logarithmic factors) rather than exponential in \(D\). Thus short trace collisions cannot occur modulo most rational primes merely by accident; they must lie over prime divisors of a controlled collection of exact integer resultants.

The missing global step is distributional: to attack Lehmer, one needs enough reduction primes with the required residue/Frobenius behavior **uniformly as \(D\to\infty\)**, at scales where this exceptional set can be avoided. Existing Chebotarev estimates through the normal closure may lose too much in the growing degree/discriminant.

## 7. Exact benchmark: Lehmer's degree-ten polynomial

Take
\[
Q_L(x)=x^{10}+x^9-x^7-x^6-x^5-x^4-x^3+x+1.
\]
Its trace polynomial is
\[
R_L(y)=y^5+y^4-5y^3-5y^2+4y+3.
\]
Thus \(D=5\). Theorem 4.1 forces all binary trace sums to be distinct through \(m=4\), giving sizes
\[
2,4,8,16,32.
\]
An exact exhaustive check shows that \(m=5\) is still collision-free, while the **first collision occurs at \(m=6\)**. It is explained by the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
C_6-C_5-C_4+C_2=(y-2)R_L(y).
}
\]
Equivalently, at a root \(t\) of \(R_L\),
\[
C_4(t)+C_5(t)=C_2(t)+C_6(t).
\]
So the degree lower bound \(m\ge D\) is sharp only as a universal threshold, not necessarily at \(m=D\) for a given polynomial.

For the fully protected range \(m=4\), exhaustive resultant factorization gives the following exceptional-prime union:
\[
\mathcal E_4(R_L)=
\{2,3,5,7,13,23,29,31,37,47,53,59,67,79,103,109,157,163,173,193,241,311,349,463,769,821\}.
\]
There are 26 primes. The largest absolute collision resultant among the \(3^5-1=242\) nonzero sign vectors is \(821\).

For comparison, at smaller depths the exact unions are:

- \(m=0\): \(\{2\}\);
- \(m=1\): \(\{2,3\}\);
- \(m=2\): \(\{2,3,13,157\}\);
- \(m=3\): \(\{2,3,5,13,23,29,31,79,109,157,163,349\}\).

These are finite benchmark facts only; they are not evidence of a universal gap by themselves.

# NEW RESULT II — exact Chebyshev–Frobenius resultant factorization

## 8. Prime-power congruence in the trace coordinate

### Lemma 8.1

For every prime \(p\),
\[
\boxed{C_p(y)\equiv y^p\pmod p}
\]
as polynomials in \(\mathbf Z[y]\).

### Proof

In the Laurent polynomial ring \(\mathbf Z[x,x^{-1}]\), under the injective substitution \(y=x+x^{-1}\),
\[
C_p(y)=x^p+x^{-p}.
\]
By the freshman's-dream congruence,
\[
(x+x^{-1})^p\equiv x^p+x^{-p}\pmod p.
\]
Therefore \(C_p(y)-y^p\) maps into \(p\mathbf Z[x,x^{-1}]\). The substitution \(\mathbf Z[y]\hookrightarrow\mathbf Z[x,x^{-1}]\) is injective, so every coefficient of \(C_p(y)-y^p\) is divisible by \(p\). ∎

For any monic \(R\in\mathbf Z[y]\), Fermat's congruence on its integer coefficients gives
\[
R(C_p(y))\equiv R(y^p)\equiv R(y)^p\pmod p.
\]
Hence
\[
A_{p,R}(y):=\frac{R(C_p(y))-R(y)^p}{p}\in\mathbf Z[y].
\]

## 9. Frobenius resultant theorem

### Theorem 9.1

Let \(Q(x)=x^D R(x+x^{-1})\) be monic, irreducible, reciprocal and noncyclotomic, with \(R\) monic irreducible of degree \(D\). Then for every prime \(p\),
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_y(R,R\circ C_p)
=p^D\operatorname{Res}_y(R,A_{p,R})
e0.
}
\]
Moreover,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_x(Q(x),Q(x^p))
=\operatorname{Res}_y(R(y),R(C_p(y)))^2
=p^{2D}\operatorname{Res}_y(R,A_{p,R})^2.
}
\]
In particular,
\[
p^D\mid \operatorname{Res}_y(R,R\circ C_p)
\quad\text{and}\quad
p^{2D}\mid \operatorname{Res}_x(Q,Q(x^p)).
\]

### Proof of the first identity

Let \(\beta\) be a root of \(R\). Since \(R(\beta)=0\),
\[
R(C_p(\beta))
=R(\beta)^p+pA_{p,R}(\beta)
=pA_{p,R}(\beta).
\]
Taking the product over all \(D\) roots \(\beta_i\) of the monic polynomial \(R\),
\[
\operatorname{Res}(R,R\circ C_p)
=\prod_iR(C_p(\beta_i))
=p^D\prod_iA_{p,R}(\beta_i)
=p^D\operatorname{Res}(R,A_{p,R}).
\]

It remains to prove nonvanishing. Suppose \(R(C_p(\beta))=0\) for a root \(\beta\) of \(R\). Write
\[
\beta=\alpha+\alpha^{-1}
\]
with \(Q(\alpha)=0\). Then
\[
C_p(\beta)=\alpha^p+\alpha^{-p}
\]
is another root \(\beta'\) of \(R\). Hence \(\alpha^p\) is a root of
\[
z^2-\beta'z+1,
\]
so \(\alpha^p\) is one of the reciprocal roots of \(Q\), i.e. a Galois conjugate of \(\alpha\). Absolute logarithmic height is invariant under conjugacy and satisfies \(h(\alpha^p)=p\,h(\alpha)\). Therefore
\[
p\,h(\alpha)=h(\alpha),
\]
so \(h(\alpha)=0\). Because \(\alpha\) is an algebraic integer unit, Kronecker's theorem then makes \(\alpha\) a root of unity, contradicting noncyclotomicity. Thus the resultant is nonzero. ∎

### Proof of the square lift

For each reciprocal root pair \(\alpha_i,\alpha_i^{-1}\) of \(Q\), let
\[
\beta_i=\alpha_i+\alpha_i^{-1}.
\]
Since
\[
Q(z)=z^D R(z+z^{-1}),
\]
we have
\[
Q(\alpha_i^p)=\alpha_i^{Dp}R(C_p(\beta_i)),
\]
\[
Q(\alpha_i^{-p})=\alpha_i^{-Dp}R(C_p(\beta_i)).
\]
Their product is exactly \(R(C_p(\beta_i))^2\). Multiplying over \(i=1,\ldots,D\),
\[
\operatorname{Res}_x(Q,Q(x^p))
=\prod_{Q(\rho)=0}Q(\rho^p)
=\prod_{i=1}^D R(C_p(\beta_i))^2
=\operatorname{Res}_y(R,R\circ C_p)^2.
\]
Combining with the first identity finishes the theorem. ∎

## 11. Failed route quantified: bare Frobenius divisibility is far too weak

The new divisibility looks strong, but by itself it cannot give a useful degree-uniform Lehmer bound.

Indeed, for a root \(\rho\) of \(Q\),
\[
|Q(\rho^p)|\le \|Q\|_1\max(1,|\rho|)^{2Dp}.
\]
Therefore
\[
|\operatorname{Res}(Q,Q(x^p))|
\le \|Q\|_1^{2D}M(Q)^{2Dp}.
\]
Using the elementary coefficient bound
\[
\|Q\|_1\le 2^{2D}M(Q),
\]
and taking square roots,
\[
|\operatorname{Res}(R,R\circ C_p)|
\le 2^{2D^2}M(Q)^{D(p+1)}.
\]
Together with \(p^D\mid\operatorname{Res}(R,R\circ C_p)\ne0\), this yields only
\[
\boxed{
\log M(Q)
\ge
\frac{\log p-2D\log2}{p+1}.
}
\]
This is positive only once \(p>4^D\), at which point the denominator is already exponentially large. So the one-prime norm argument is exponentially too weak. Any successful Frobenius/resultant proof must exploit substantially more structure: many primes simultaneously, normalized resultants, cancellation, auxiliary polynomials, or a finite-field expansion argument rather than only absolute-value bounding.

This explicit failure is useful because it prevents spending future runs on the unnormalized one-prime inequality.

## 14. Next attack

The highest-value next step is now sharply defined.

For a reciprocal hard-core polynomial \(Q=x^DR(x+x^{-1})\), choose a depth
\[
m\asymp c\log D<D.
\]
The new theorem says all characteristic-zero trace subset sums are distinct and that only an explicitly bounded polynomial-size collection of rational primes can create a short collision after reduction. The desired bridge is to combine this with prime/residue distribution:

1. find many rational primes \(p\) at a controlled scale for which \(R\) has a residue root \(\bar t\) (or, better, for which the reciprocal quadratic extension realizes inversion Frobenius);
2. force enough of those primes outside \(\mathcal E_m(R)\);
3. use the resulting large trace subset set as the seed for finite-field sum-product amplification;
4. feed that back into the Breuillard–Varjú bad-prime equivalence.

The precise obstruction is **uniformity in growing degree and normal-closure complexity**. A future run should therefore quantify the best unconditional prime-ideal/root-distribution statement that depends only on \(D\), \(M(Q)\), and the discriminant of the trace field, rather than immediately passing to the full normal closure.

A parallel algebraic route is to study the quotient resultants
\[
\operatorname{Res}(R,A_{p,R}),
\]
whose small values in the Lehmer benchmark suggest asking whether many primes can have unusually small quotient simultaneously. Any useful theorem here must improve materially on the failed one-prime absolute-value estimate of §11.
