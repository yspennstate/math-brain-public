# 2. Setup: SI automata and right-action convention

Call a letter \(e=e_{s\to t}\) a **simple idempotent** if
\[
e(s)=t,\qquad e(q)=q\quad(q\neq s),\qquad s\neq t.
\]
Thus \(e\) has rank \(n-1\), defect one, and \(e^2=e\).

In this report an **SI automaton** means an automaton whose alphabet splits as
\[
\Sigma=P\sqcup E,
\]
where every \(p\in P\) acts as a permutation and every \(e\in E\) is a simple idempotent.

We use right actions: if transformations \(f,g\) are concatenated, then
\[
q(fg)=(qf)g.
\]
Let
\[
G=\langle P\rangle\le \operatorname{Sym}(Q)
\]
be the finite permutation group generated by the permutation letters. Because the generators are permutations, the positive monoid they generate is already the group \(G\).

For \(g,h\in G\), define the directed generator distance
\[
d_P(g,h)=\min\{|u|:u\in P^*,\ gu=h\}.
\tag{1}
\]
This is finite for every pair.

If \(e=e_{s\to t}\in E\) and \(g\in G\), define its conjugate
\[
e^g:=g e g^{-1}.
\]
A direct calculation gives
\[
e^g=e_{s g^{-1}\to t g^{-1}}.
\tag{2}
\]
We call
\[
\alpha(g,e):=(s g^{-1},t g^{-1})
\tag{3}
\]
the **conjugated defect arc**.

Let \(\Gamma=\Gamma(P,E)\) be the directed graph on \(Q\) containing all arcs \(\alpha(g,e)\) for \(g\in G\), \(e\in E\).

# 3. Exact conjugation normal form

The first lemma removes the apparent complication caused by arbitrary rank-preserving simple-idempotent uses.

## Lemma 1 (telescoping conjugation factorization)

Write an arbitrary word as
\[
w=u_0e_1u_1e_2\cdots e_m u_m,
\tag{4}
\]
where each \(u_i\in P^*\) and each \(e_i\in E\). Let \(\bar u_i\in G\) denote the permutation induced by \(u_i\), and define
\[
g_i=\bar u_0\bar u_1\cdots \bar u_{i-1}\qquad(1\le i\le m),
\]
\[
g_{m+1}=\bar u_0\bar u_1\cdots\bar u_m.
\]
Then as transformations,
\[
\boxed{
 w=(g_1e_1g_1^{-1})(g_2e_2g_2^{-1})\cdots(g_me_mg_m^{-1})g_{m+1}.
}
\tag{5}
\]

### Proof

Multiply the first two conjugates:
\[
(g_1e_1g_1^{-1})(g_2e_2g_2^{-1})
=
\bar u_0e_1\bar u_1e_2g_2^{-1}.
\]
Continuing telescopically cancels every intermediate \(g_i^{-1}g_{i+1}\), leaving (4) after the final factor \(g_{m+1}\). \(\square\)

Because the trailing factor \(g_{m+1}\) is a permutation, it does not affect rank. Therefore every rank calculation can be done on the product of conjugated simple idempotents in (5).

# 4. Hole dynamics for a general defect arc

For an image \(S\subseteq Q\), write its hole set as
\[
H=Q\setminus S.
\]
For a simple idempotent \(e_{r\to t}\), direct inspection gives
\[
\boxed{
H e_{r\to t}=\begin{cases}
H,&r\in H,\\[1mm]
(H\setminus\{t\})\cup\{r\},&r\notin H.
\end{cases}}
\tag{6}
\]
where the notation means “the hole set after acting on the complementary live set.”

Thus a simple idempotent has exactly three possible effects on holes:

1. if its source \(r\) is already a hole, nothing happens;
2. if \(r\) is live and \(t\) is a hole, the hole moves from \(t\) to \(r\), so rank is unchanged;
3. if both \(r,t\) are live, a new hole \(r\) is created and rank drops by one.

Equation (6) is the general version of the normalized cyclic operation from checkpoint 3.

# 5. Last-establishment forest theorem

The cyclic “ordered chain” becomes a rooted forest.

Consider a sequence of conjugated simple idempotents acting on the full set \(Q\), so initially
\[
H_0=\varnothing.
\]
Suppose the final image has size \(k\), hence the final hole set \(H_*\) has size \(n-k\). Put
\[
F:=Q\setminus H_*,\qquad |F|=k.
\]

For every final hole \(r\in H_*\), let \(\tau(r)\) be the **last** operation at which \(r\) enters the hole set.

## Lemma 2 (last-establishment ordering)

At time \(\tau(r)\), the acting conjugated idempotent has an arc
\[
r\to p_r.
\]
If \(p_r\in H_*\) is also a final hole, then
\[
\boxed{\tau(r)<\tau(p_r).}
\tag{7}
\]

### Proof

At time \(\tau(r)\), the source \(r\) is live immediately before the operation and becomes a hole. By (6), immediately after this operation the target \(p_r\) is **not** a hole: if it was a hole it is removed, and if it was live it stays live. If \(p_r\) is a final hole, it must therefore enter the hole set again later. Its last entrance is strictly later than \(\tau(r)\). \(\square\)

## Theorem 3 (selected last-establishment arcs form a rooted spanning forest)

The \(n-k\) arcs
\[
\{r\to p_r:r\in H_*\}
\tag{8}
\]
form a directed spanning forest on \(Q\), oriented toward the root set \(F\). Every \(r\in H_*\) has outdegree one, every \(f\in F\) has outdegree zero, and every directed path ends in \(F\).

Moreover, ordering the selected arcs by increasing \(\tau(r)\) gives a **child-before-parent** order: whenever \(r\to p_r\) and \(p_r\notin F\), the arc leaving \(r\) appears before the arc leaving \(p_r\).

### Proof

Every final hole has exactly one selected last-establishment arc, so every \(r\in H_*\) has outdegree one in (8), while vertices of \(F\) have none. By Lemma 2, along every edge whose target is also in \(H_*\), the \(\tau\)-value strictly increases. Hence no directed cycle can lie in \(H_*\). Starting from any final hole and repeatedly following its selected outgoing edge must therefore terminate at a vertex with no selected outgoing edge, necessarily in \(F\). This is precisely a rooted spanning forest. The child-before-parent statement is (7). \(\square\)

This is the exact higher-dimensional analogue of the consecutive-site subsequence in checkpoint 3.

# 6. Exact metric/arborescence formula for every rank-from-\(Q\) threshold

For \(1\le k\le n\), define
\[
\rho_k(\mathcal A)
=
\min\{|w|:|Qw|\le k\},
\tag{9}
\]
with \(\rho_k=\infty\) if rank \(k\) is unreachable.

Because permutation letters preserve rank and simple idempotents lower rank by at most one, whenever \(\rho_k<\infty\) a shortest prefix first reaching rank at most \(k\) has rank exactly \(k\).

Call a sequence
\[
(g_1,e_1),\ldots,(g_{n-k},e_{n-k})
\tag{10}
\]
**\(k\)-admissible** if the conjugated arcs
\[
\alpha(g_i,e_i)
\]
are exactly the arcs of a directed spanning forest with \(k\) roots, and the sequence order is child-before-parent.

Define its permutation-travel cost
\[
\operatorname{cost}_P(g_1,\ldots,g_{n-k})
=
 d_P(1,g_1)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k-1}d_P(g_i,g_{i+1}).
\tag{11}
\]

## Theorem 4 (exact forest metric formula)

For every SI automaton and every reachable rank \(k\),
\[
\boxed{
\rho_k(\mathcal A)
=
(n-k)+
\min_{\text{\(k\)-admissible sequences}}
\operatorname{cost}_P(g_1,\ldots,g_{n-k}).
}
\tag{12}
\]

In particular, if \(\mathcal A\) is synchronizing,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rt}(\mathcal A)
=
(n-1)+
\min_{\text{rooted arborescence schedules}}
\left[d_P(1,g_1)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}d_P(g_i,g_{i+1})\right].
}
\tag{13}
\]

### Lower bound

Take any word \(w\) of rank \(k\), factor it as in Lemma 1, and examine the conjugated idempotent sequence. Theorem 3 selects \(n-k\) singular occurrences whose arcs form a rooted spanning forest in child-before-parent order.

Suppose their original occurrence indices are
\[
i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_{n-k},
\]
and their cumulative permutation prefixes are
\[
g_{i_1},\ldots,g_{i_{n-k}}.
\]
The permutation letters before the first selected occurrence constitute a positive generator word taking \(1\) to \(g_{i_1}\), so there are at least \(d_P(1,g_{i_1})\) of them. Between the selected occurrences \(i_j\) and \(i_{j+1}\), the permutation letters constitute a word taking \(g_{i_j}\) to \(g_{i_{j+1}}\), so that interval contains at least
\[
d_P(g_{i_j},g_{i_{j+1}})
\]
permutation letters.

These intervals are disjoint. Also, reducing rank from \(n\) to \(k\) requires at least \(n-k\) simple-idempotent letters. Hence
\[
|w|\ge
(n-k)+d_P(1,g_{i_1})+
\sum_{j=1}^{n-k-1}d_P(g_{i_j},g_{i_{j+1}}).
\]
Minimizing over the selected admissible sequence gives the lower bound in (12).

### Upper bound

Conversely, take any \(k\)-admissible sequence (10). For each \(i\), choose a shortest permutation word \(v_0\in P^*\) taking \(1\) to \(g_1\), and for \(i\ge1\) choose a shortest \(v_i\in P^*\) taking \(g_i\) to \(g_{i+1}\). Form
\[
w=v_0e_1v_1e_2\cdots v_{n-k-1}e_{n-k}.
\tag{14}
\]
Its conjugation factorization has exactly the chosen forest arcs, followed by a final permutation.

Process the forest in the prescribed child-before-parent order. When an arc \(r\to p\) is applied, neither \(r\) nor its parent \(p\) has yet been deleted: \(r\) has not appeared as a source before, and if \(p\) is not a root its own source event occurs later. Therefore both endpoints are live, so the simple idempotent deletes exactly \(r\) and decreases rank by one. After the \(n-k\) selected arcs, precisely the \(k\) roots remain. The trailing permutation implicit in the factorization preserves rank.

Thus (14) has rank \(k\) and length exactly the right side of (12). \(\square\)

# 7. Consequences: all noncompressing singular letters are globally dispensable

Theorem 4 gives a stronger version of checkpoint 3’s “wasted defect-letter” elimination.

## Corollary 5 (canonical shortest rank word)

For every SI automaton and every reachable rank \(k\), there exists a shortest word attaining rank \(k\) that contains **exactly \(n-k\) simple-idempotent letters**. Every one of those singular letters lowers the current rank by exactly one.

In particular, every synchronizing SI automaton has a shortest reset word containing exactly
\[
\boxed{n-1}
\]
singular letters.

So arbitrary extra rank-preserving or no-op simple-idempotent occurrences can never improve the optimum rank-from-\(Q\) threshold. They may appear in some reset words, but an equally short or shorter canonical word exists without them.

This statement is global: it applies to any number of permutation letters, any number of simple-idempotent letters, and arbitrary permutation groups.

# 8. Exact minimum-rank graph certificate

The metric formula immediately yields a graph-theoretic rank certificate.

## Corollary 6 (minimum rank = number of sink SCCs of the defect-orbit graph)

Let \(c(\Gamma)\) be the number of sink strongly connected components of the conjugated defect graph \(\Gamma(P,E)\). Then the minimum rank of the SI automaton is exactly
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal A)=c(\Gamma).
}
\tag{15}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal A\text{ is synchronizing}
\iff
\Gamma(P,E)\text{ has exactly one sink SCC}
\iff
\Gamma(P,E)\text{ contains a spanning in-arborescence.}
}
\tag{16}
\]

### Proof

By Theorem 4, rank \(k\) is attainable exactly when \(\Gamma\) contains a spanning directed forest with \(k\) roots.

Every such forest needs at least one root in every sink SCC, because a vertex in a sink SCC has no directed path leaving that SCC. Hence \(k\ge c(\Gamma)\).

Conversely, choose one root in each sink SCC. Inside every SCC choose a directed in-arborescence toward an appropriate exit/root vertex. In the condensation DAG, every non-sink SCC has a directed path to some sink SCC; choose one outgoing condensation edge per non-sink component along such a path. Combining the internal arborescences with these chosen inter-component edges gives a spanning directed forest with exactly one root per sink SCC. Thus \(k=c(\Gamma)\). \(\square\)

This is closely aligned with the established Rystsov-graph viewpoint, but here it drops directly out of the last-establishment certificate and is paired with the exact generator-metric length formula (12).

# 9. Regular permutation group + one simple idempotent: exact classification

The formula becomes completely explicit for the class emphasized in Rystsov’s Černý-type work.

Assume:

* \(G\) acts **regularly** on \(Q\), so \(|G|=|Q|=n\);
* there is a single simple idempotent \(e=e_{s\to t}\);
* the permutation alphabet \(P\) generates \(G\).

Use the regular action to identify \(Q\) with \(G\): choose \(s\) as the identity state and write
\[
t=s h
\]
for the unique \(h\in G\setminus\{1\}\).

Under this identification, the conjugated defect arc corresponding to a prefix \(g\in G\) is
\[
g^{-1}\longrightarrow h g^{-1}.
\tag{17}
\]
Thus the defect-orbit graph is exactly the permutation digraph of **left multiplication by \(h\)**. Its components are directed cycles of length \(\operatorname{ord}(h)\).

## Theorem 7 (exact minimum rank in the regular one-idempotent case)

The minimum rank is
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal A)
=
\frac{n}{\operatorname{ord}(h)}.
}
\tag{18}
\]

### Proof

The graph (17) has exactly \(n/\operatorname{ord}(h)\) directed cycles, each of which is a sink SCC. Apply Corollary 6. \(\square\)

## Corollary 8 (synchronizability classification)

The automaton is synchronizing if and only if
\[
\operatorname{ord}(h)=n.
\tag{19}
\]
If it is synchronizing, then \(G\) itself is cyclic, generated by \(h\).

Indeed, an element of order \(|G|=n\) generates the entire group.

This is a useful structural clarification: a synchronizing automaton generated by **one** simple idempotent and a regular permutation group necessarily reduces to a cyclic regular group, although its chosen permutation alphabet need not consist of a single cycle letter.

# 10. Exact generator-sensitive reset threshold in the regular synchronizing case

Assume now \(\operatorname{ord}(h)=n\). Define
\[
\ell=d_P(1,h^{-1}),
\tag{20}
\]
the shortest positive permutation-generator word representing \(h^{-1}\).

## Theorem 9

For a synchronizing regular one-idempotent automaton,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{rt}(\mathcal A)
=(n-1)+(n-2)\ell.
}
\tag{21}
\]

### Proof

The defect graph is the directed cycle
\[
x\to hx.
\]
Any spanning in-arborescence is obtained by deleting one cycle edge. Its child-before-parent order is then forced along the remaining directed path.

Choose the root so that the first source is the identity state \(1\). The source sequence is
\[
1,h,h^2,\ldots,h^{n-2}.
\]
The unique prefix group element whose conjugated defect arc has source \(h^j\) is
\[
g_j=h^{-j}.
\]
Therefore the first prefix is \(g_0=1\), with zero initial cost, and each consecutive prefix transition satisfies
\[
g_j^{-1}g_{j+1}=h^{-1}.
\]
By right-invariance of the directed Cayley metric,
\[
d_P(g_j,g_{j+1})=d_P(1,h^{-1})=\ell.
\]
There are \(n-2\) such transitions and \(n-1\) singular letters. The exact formula (13) gives (21). \(\square\)

Since the directed Cayley graph of a group of order \(n\) has a simple path of length at most \(n-1\) between any two vertices,
\[
\ell\le n-1.
\]
Hence
\[
\operatorname{rt}(\mathcal A)
\le
(n-1)+(n-2)(n-1)
=(n-1)^2.
\tag{22}
\]
Moreover the exact slack is
\[
\boxed{
(n-1)^2-\operatorname{rt}(\mathcal A)
=(n-2)(n-1-\ell).
}
\tag{23}
\]
Thus this gives a short exact derivation of the Černý bound for the regular one-idempotent class and identifies precisely when its chosen generator metric is extremal.

# 12. Relation to the Černý–Pin rank conjecture

The exact rank formula (12) isolates a clean metric target.

Let the minimum rank of an SI automaton be \(r\). The Černý–Pin rank conjecture would follow for this automaton if one can find an \(r\)-rooted defect forest and a child-before-parent witness schedule satisfying
\[
\boxed{
 d_P(1,g_1)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-r-1}d_P(g_i,g_{i+1})
\le
(n-r)(n-r-1).
}
\tag{24}
\]
Indeed, adding the \(n-r\) singular letters then gives
\[
\rho_r\le(n-r)^2.
\]

A crude sufficient condition is that the directed Cayley diameter \(\Delta_P\) obeys
\[
\Delta_P\le n-r-1,
\]
because there are \(n-r\) permutation-travel segments in (11). This is far from sharp, and in the extremal Černý family the Cayley diameter is \(n-1\), so the useful content must come from choosing the forest/order/witnesses jointly rather than bounding every jump independently.

This is the main unresolved step exposed by the current theorem.

# 13. Concrete obstruction / failed route

The arborescence formula is an exact normalization, not by itself a proof of the unrestricted conjecture.

A tempting move is to bound every group-prefix jump by a global Cayley diameter. This loses too much: in general permutation groups the generator diameter can be much larger than \(n\), and even in cyclic extremal examples the diameter can be \(n-1\). The square bound is recovered there only because:

1. the first selected singular arc can be taken at group prefix \(1\), costing zero;
2. the remaining \(n-2\) jumps are all the **same** displacement \(h^{-1}\);
3. only \(n-2\), rather than \(n-1\), nonzero permutation jumps are needed.

Therefore the next useful step is not a global diameter estimate. It is an **amortized traveling-forest bound** exploiting overlap between the defect graph and the permutation Schreier/Cayley geometry.

# 16. Next attack

The next bounded research target should use the exact formula rather than return to raw subset compression.

1. **Schreier compression of witnesses.** Quotient group prefixes by stabilizers of defect arcs, so that the metric optimization is performed on ordered-pair or defect-orbit Schreier graphs rather than the full permutation group.
2. **Exchange lemma for arborescence schedules.** Given a long jump \(g_i\to g_{i+1}\), try to exchange the parent choice or reorder independent subtrees to replace it by two shorter jumps without increasing the number of singular letters.
3. **Amortized potential.** Seek a potential on the remaining forest plus current group prefix for which every permutation letter pays down future tree depth. The exact target is total permutation cost at most \((n-r)(n-r-1)\), not a bound on each jump.
4. **Minimal-counterexample extraction.** If (24) fails for an SI automaton, use the exact dynamic program to output a smallest witness consisting of a defect-orbit graph, generator Schreier graph, and certified optimal metric forest. Such a finite certificate would identify the first genuine obstruction to the SI square.
5. **Comparison with Ferens–Szykuła / Rystsov extension methods.** Translate their short-extending-word arguments into constraints on the metric forest schedule and look for an amortized saving of roughly one factor of two over the known SI quadratic bounds.
