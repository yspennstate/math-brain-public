# HARD8-05 — Erdős–Straus conjecture research checkpoint

**Run status:** PARTIAL / FULL CONJECTURE OPEN  
**Target:** prove or refute, for every integer `n >= 2`, the existence of positive integers `x,y,z` such that

\[
\frac4n=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z.
\]

This checkpoint does **not** claim a proof of the Erdős–Straus conjecture. The main new result is an exact prime-factor/Dirichlet-character classification for two very small Type-II divisor portals, together with a paired necessary condition for any counterexample in the hard residue class. It also extends the previous exact finite experiment from `720001` to `4,300,000` and finds that the same two fixed portals repair every `a=1` exception in that larger range.

## 3. Universal quantifiers and the standard reduction

The elementary case `n=2` is

\[
\frac42=1+\frac12+\frac12.
\]

If `d` has a solution and `n=md`, then scaling all three denominators by `m` gives a solution for `n`:

\[
\frac4d=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac4{md}=\frac1{mx}+\frac1{my}+\frac1{mz}.
\]

Thus a counterexample, if one exists, has a prime divisor that is itself a counterexample. Standard congruence identities reduce the prime problem further to primes `n≡1 (mod 24)`; Xu uses exactly this hard residue class. The work below focuses on that class without changing the universal target.

## 4. Divisor residues when the unit group has exponent two

The previous checkpoint used the portal `(a,u)=(2,1)`, whose modulus is 8. The key observation of this run is that the equally small portal `(a,u)=(6,1)` has modulus 24, and both unit groups have exponent two. That makes divisor existence exactly reducible to a support-subgroup problem.

### Lemma 4.1 — divisor residues modulo 24 are a support subgroup

Let `N` be coprime to 24 and write

\[
N=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}.
\]

Let

\[
H(N)=\left\langle p_1\bmod24,\ldots,p_r\bmod24\right\rangle
\le (\mathbb Z/24\mathbb Z)^\times.
\]

Then the set of residues modulo 24 attained by positive divisors of `N` is **exactly** `H(N)`.

#### Proof

Every element of `(Z/24Z)^×={1,5,7,11,13,17,19,23}` has square 1. Hence for each prime support residue `r_i=p_i mod24`, a divisor exponent `0<=f_i<=e_i` contributes only the parity `f_i mod2`. Since `e_i>=1`, both parities 0 and 1 are available. Therefore the divisor residues are precisely all products

\[
\prod_i r_i^{\varepsilon_i},\qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\},
\]

which is exactly the subgroup generated by the support residues. ∎

This statement fails in this simple form for moduli whose unit group has exponent greater than two; Dahan explicitly points out that this is why a subgroup argument alone breaks down in his general two-parameter setting.

## 5. Exact classification for total residue 5 modulo 24

The portal `(a,u)=(2,3)` has `4au=24` and tests divisors of `2n+3`. Although this portal is no longer needed for the two-portal sample result below, its classification is useful because it exhibits the full mechanism.

### Proposition 5.1

Let `N≡5 (mod24)`. Then `N` has a divisor `s≡23 (mod24)` iff the prime support of `N` is **not** contained entirely in either of the following two subgroups:

\[
H_{-6}=\{1,5,7,11\},
\qquad
H_{-4}=\{1,5,13,17\}.
\]

Equivalently, failure occurs iff either

\[
\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=+1\quad\text{for every prime }p\mid N,
\]

or

\[
p\equiv1\pmod4\quad\text{for every prime }p\mid N.
\]

Here the first symbol is the Kronecker/Legendre character on primes coprime to 24.

#### Proof

By Lemma 4.1, a divisor `23 mod24` exists iff `23∈H(N)`. Since `N≡5`, the total product of the prime residues (with exponent parity) equals 5, hence `5∈H(N)`.

If `23` is absent, enlarge `H(N)` to a maximal subgroup of `(Z/24Z)^×` that contains 5 but not 23. There are exactly two such index-two subgroups. Direct multiplication in the eight-element group gives precisely

\[
\{1,5,7,11\},\qquad \{1,5,13,17\}.
\]

Conversely, if all prime support residues lie in either subgroup, every divisor residue lies there, so 23 cannot occur.

The first subgroup is the kernel of the quadratic character `(-6/·)`; the second is the kernel of `(-4/·)`, equivalently the residues `1 mod4` among units modulo 24. ∎

For `n≡1 (mod24)`, `2n+3≡5 (mod24)`, so Proposition 5.1 is an exact factorization characterization of the `(2,3)` portal.

## 6. Exact classification for total residue 7 modulo 24

The more useful new portal is

\[
(a,u)=(6,1).
\]

It asks for

\[
s\mid 6n+1,\qquad s\equiv23\pmod{24}.
\]

### Proposition 6.1 — exact `(6,1)` failure criterion

Let `N≡7 (mod24)`. Then `N` has a divisor `s≡23 (mod24)` iff the prime support of `N` is not contained entirely in either

\[
H_{-6}=\{1,5,7,11\}
\]

or

\[
H_{-3}=\{1,7,13,19\}.
\]

Equivalently, failure occurs iff either

\[
\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=+1\quad\text{for every prime }p\mid N,
\]

or

\[
p\equiv1\pmod3\quad\text{for every prime }p\mid N.
\]

#### Proof

Again the divisor residues are the subgroup `H(N)`. Since `N≡7`, one has `7∈H(N)`. The maximal subgroups containing 7 but excluding 23 are exactly

\[
\{1,5,7,11\},\qquad \{1,7,13,19\}.
\]

The first is the kernel of `(-6/·)`. The second consists exactly of the units modulo 24 that are `1 mod3`, i.e. the kernel of the quadratic character `(-3/·)`. The same maximal-subgroup argument as in Proposition 5.1 proves the equivalence. ∎

For every `n≡1 (mod24)`,

\[
6n+1\equiv7\pmod{24},
\]

so this gives a complete factor-support description of the `(6,1)` portal.

When this portal fires, if

\[
s=24t-1,\qquad q=\frac{6n+1}{s},
\]

then the prior factor-portal lemma gives the explicit solution

\[
\boxed{\frac4n
=\frac1{qt}+\frac1{6nqt}+\frac1{6nt}.}
\]

## 8. A paired quadratic-splitting necessary condition for any counterexample

Combining Propositions 6.1 and 7.1 gives the main structural statement of this run.

### Theorem 8.1 — two-portal counterexample sieve

Let `n≡1 (mod24)`. If `n` is a counterexample to Erdős–Straus, then **both** of the following must hold:

1. every prime divisor `p` of `2n+1` satisfies
   \[
   p\equiv1,3\pmod8,
   \]
   equivalently every such prime splits in `Q(sqrt(-2))`;
2. for `6n+1`, one of these two global alternatives holds:
   * every prime divisor `q` satisfies `(-6/q)=+1`, i.e. all prime support lies in `H_{-6}`; or
   * every prime divisor `q` satisfies `q≡1 (mod3)`, i.e. all prime support lies in `H_{-3}`.

#### Proof

If condition 1 fails, Portal `(2,1)` supplies a divisor `s≡7 mod8` of `2n+1`, and the factor-portal identity gives a three-unit-fraction decomposition. Hence a counterexample must satisfy 1.

If condition 2 fails, Proposition 6.1 supplies a divisor `s≡23 mod24` of `6n+1`, and Portal `(6,1)` gives a decomposition. Hence a counterexample must satisfy one of the two alternatives in 2. ∎

This theorem is only a **necessary condition** for a counterexample, not a contradiction. It is compatible with Dahan's warning that fixed-shift residual sets need not be finite. Its value is that the two successful small portals are now expressed as simultaneous splitting restrictions in three explicit imaginary quadratic characters.

A possible next step is therefore to study the coupled linear forms

\[
2n+1,\qquad 6n+1=3(2n+1)-2
\]

under these splitting restrictions, rather than adding arbitrary new residue portals.

## 10. Portal coverage counts in Xu's original range

Among the 7,185 primes `n≡1 mod24`, `n<=720001`:

* Portal `(2,1)` fires for **4,307**;
* Portal `(6,1)` fires for **2,192**;
* both fire for **1,263**;
* their union fires for **5,236**;

Thus the paired portals are nowhere near a standalone finite covering of the hard residue class. Their usefulness is instead that every one of Xu's nine `a=1` exceptions lies in the paired union.

This is an important obstruction against overinterpreting the small-exception success: the paired portals miss many ordinary primes, which are instead solved by other `a=1` witnesses.

## 12. Failed routes / concrete obstructions

1. **Use `(2,1)` alone.** It fails at `83449` and `102001` among Xu's nine small `a=1` exceptions.
2. **Interpret success on the nine exceptions as a universal two-portal cover.** False even at small scale: `(2,1)` and `(6,1)` jointly miss 1,949 hard-class primes through `720001`; these primes are simply solved by other mechanisms.
3. **Push the support-subgroup proof to arbitrary `4au`.** This breaks once `(Z/4au Z)^×` has elements of order greater than two. The exponent-two identity in Lemma 4.1 is the essential special feature; Dahan's paper makes the same structural warning in its more general sieve framework.
4. **Treat quadratic-character splitting restrictions as a contradiction.** They are only sieve restrictions. There is no proof here that the coupled linear forms cannot simultaneously have those prime supports infinitely often.

## 13. Next attack

The most focused next target is no longer to add another arbitrary small portal. It is to exploit the algebraic relation

\[
6n+1=3(2n+1)-2
\]

under the exact simultaneous failure conditions from Theorem 8.1.

Two concrete branches are available:

1. **Norm-form branch.** Portal `(2,1)` failure forces every prime of `2n+1` to split in `Q(sqrt(-2))`. Since that field has class number one, `2n+1` lies in a tightly controlled norm semigroup. Combine this with either the `Q(sqrt(-3))` or `Q(sqrt(-6))` splitting restriction on `6n+1` and ask whether the linear relation forces a new divisor portal.
2. **Two-shift sieve branch.** Adapt Dahan's fixed-shift obstruction analysis to the simultaneous pair of shifts represented by `2n+1` and `6n+1`. The objective is not to prove their residual set finite by assumption, but to compute its exact sieve dimension and identify which additional algebraic condition distinguishes the actual `a=1` exceptions from the 1,949 ordinary paired misses in Xu's range.

A parallel computational task is to reproduce the larger finite exceptional lists in Dahan with the same exact fixed-`a` certificate logic and test whether `(2,1) ∪ (6,1)` continues to repair every `a=1` exception. That would remain finite evidence, but it would determine whether this two-portal phenomenon survives well beyond `4.3·10^6`.
