## 3. Universal reduction retained

The case `n=2` is elementary:

\[
\frac42=1+\frac12+\frac12.
\]

If `d` has a solution and `n=md`, scaling all three denominators by `m` gives a solution for `n`. Hence a counterexample has a prime divisor that is itself a counterexample. Classical congruence identities reduce the prime problem to the hard class `n ≡ 1 (mod 24)` (more sharply, to six square classes modulo 840, all lying in that class). Everything below is therefore a necessary-condition analysis for a prime counterexample `n ≡ 1 (mod 24)` and does not alter the full universal target.

## 4. When the support-subgroup method is exact

The key feature used in the preceding run was that every unit modulo 8 or 24 has order at most two. In that situation divisor residues are exactly the subgroup generated by prime-support residues. The first goal here is to determine **all** portals for which this elementary mechanism can occur.

### Lemma 4.1 — classification of moduli with unit exponent at most two

Let `m` be divisible by 4. Then every element of `(Z/mZ)^×` has square `1` if and only if

\[
\boxed{m\mid24.}
\]

#### Proof

Suppose the exponent of `(Z/mZ)^×` is at most two. If an odd prime `p` divides `m`, reduction modulo `p` maps onto `(Z/pZ)^×`, whose exponent is `p-1`; hence `p-1<=2`, so `p=3`. Thus `m=2^a3^b`.

For `a>=4`, the unit `3 mod 16` has order 4, so `a<=3`. For `b>=2`, `(Z/9Z)^×` has exponent 6, so `b<=1`. Since `4|m`, it follows that `m` divides 24. Conversely every divisor of 24 has unit group of exponent at most two. ∎

### Lemma 4.2 — exact divisor-support subgroup when the odd part is a unit

Let `m` have unit-group exponent at most two, and let `N=2^eN_0` with `N_0` odd and coprime to `m`. Since `-1 mod m` is odd, a divisor `s≡-1 (mod m)` must be odd and therefore divides `N_0`. If

\[
N_0=\prod p_i^{e_i},
\]

then the residues modulo `m` of divisors of `N_0` are exactly

\[
\left\langle p_i\bmod m\right\rangle.
\]

#### Proof

Every unit residue squares to one. For every prime support element `p_i`, exponent `0` and exponent `1` are both available among divisors, and higher exponents contribute only their parity. Hence the divisor residues are precisely all products of selected support residues. ∎

## 5. Maximality theorem: exactly nine exponent-two portals

### Theorem 5.1 — no other coprime portal has this exact elementary support mechanism

Let `n` be a prime with `n≡1 (mod24)` and let `a,u` be coprime positive integers. Consider the divisor portal

\[
s\mid an+u,\qquad s\equiv-1\pmod{4au}.
\]

Assume one wants the portal obstruction to be controlled exactly by Lemma 4.2, i.e. by the support subgroup in a modulus whose unit group has exponent at most two after stripping the forced power of two from `an+u`.

Then necessarily

\[
4au\in\{4,8,12,24\},
\]

so

\[
au\in\{1,2,3,6\}.
\]

With `gcd(a,u)=1`, the complete ordered list is

\[
\boxed{
(1,1),
(1,2),(2,1),
(1,3),(3,1),
(1,6),(6,1),(2,3),(3,2).}
\]

Conversely every one of these nine portals admits an exact support-subgroup classification in the hard class.

#### Proof

By Lemma 4.1, exponent at most two forces `4au|24`, hence `au∈{1,2,3,6}`. Enumerating ordered coprime factor pairs gives precisely the nine displayed pairs.

For `au=2` or `6`, `a,u` have opposite parity, so `an+u` is odd. Because `n≡1 mod24` is a prime larger than 6, `an+u` is coprime to `4au`.

For `au=1`, necessarily `(a,u)=(1,1)`. Since `n≡1 mod8`, `v_2(n+1)=1`, so the odd part `(n+1)/2` is coprime to 4.

For `au=3`, the pairs are `(1,3),(3,1)`. Since `n≡1 mod8`, both `n+3` and `3n+1` are congruent to 4 modulo 8, so each has exact 2-adic valuation two. Their odd quotients `(n+3)/4` and `(3n+1)/4` are coprime to 12. Lemma 4.2 therefore applies in all nine cases. ∎

**Interpretation.** This proves a genuine obstruction to the previous strategy: there is no tenth coprime `(a,u)` portal whose divisor obstruction can be reduced to the same exponent-two support-subgroup argument. To go further one must either use the exponent-four (or larger) divisor-residue structure, use a different branch of the Type-I/Type-II parametrization, or exploit coupling between several linear forms.

## 6. Exact failure criteria for all nine portals

Write

\[
H_{-6}=\{1,5,7,11\},\quad
H_{-4}=\{1,5,13,17\},\quad
H_{-3}=\{1,7,13,19\}
\]

inside `(Z/24Z)^×`. They are respectively the kernels of the real quadratic characters `(-6/·)`, `(-4/·)`, and `(-3/·)` on primes coprime to 24.

### Proposition 6.1 — product `au=1`

Portal `(1,1)` fails exactly when every odd prime divisor of

\[
\frac{n+1}{2}
\]

is `1 mod4`.

#### Proof

The target residue is `-1≡3 mod4`. The odd quotient `(n+1)/2` is `1 mod4`. Since `(Z/4Z)^×={1,3}`, a divisor congruent to 3 exists iff the prime support contains a residue 3. ∎

### Proposition 6.2 — product `au=2`

Each of the two portals `(1,2)` and `(2,1)` has total linear-form residue `3 mod8`. Such a portal fails exactly when every prime divisor of its linear form is

\[
\boxed{1\text{ or }3\pmod8.}
\]

Equivalently, every prime divisor has `(-2/p)=+1`.

#### Proof

The unit group modulo 8 is `{1,3,5,7}`. The support subgroup contains the total residue 3. The unique maximal subgroup containing 3 but excluding the target `-1=7` is `{1,3}`. ∎

Thus a counterexample must have this property simultaneously for

\[
n+2\quad\text{and}\quad2n+1.
\]

### Proposition 6.3 — product `au=3`

Set

\[
K_1=\frac{n+3}{4},\qquad K_2=\frac{3n+1}{4}.
\]

For `n=24k+1`, both satisfy

\[
K_1\equiv K_2\equiv6k+1\pmod{12}.
\]

Hence:

- if `n≡25 (mod48)`, then `K_1,K_2≡7 (mod12)`, and failure of either portal is equivalent to all prime-support residues lying in
  \[
  \boxed{\{1,7\}\pmod{12}},
  \]
  equivalently every prime divisor is `1 mod3`;
- if `n≡1 (mod48)`, then `K_1,K_2≡1 (mod12)`, and failure is equivalent to the support lying entirely in **one** of
  \[
  \boxed{\{1,5\}\pmod{12}\quad\text{or}\quad\{1,7\}\pmod{12}.}
  \]
  The first is the `p≡1 mod4` kernel and the second the `p≡1 mod3` kernel.

#### Proof

`(Z/12Z)^×={1,5,7,11}` is a Klein four group and the target is 11. If the generated subgroup contains 7, the only subgroup containing 7 and avoiding 11 is `{1,7}`. If the total residue is 1, the subgroups avoiding 11 are contained in either `{1,5}` or `{1,7}`. Lemma 4.2 makes this equivalent to the divisor condition. ∎

### Proposition 6.4 — product `au=6`, total residue 5

For `(a,u)=(2,3)` and `(3,2)`, the linear forms are respectively

\[
2n+3,\qquad3n+2,
\]

both `5 mod24`. Failure occurs exactly when the prime support lies entirely in one of

\[
\boxed{H_{-6}\quad\text{or}\quad H_{-4}.}
\]

This is the residue-5 classification proved in the preceding checkpoint, now placed in the complete nine-portal family.

### Proposition 6.5 — product `au=6`, total residue 7

For `(a,u)=(1,6)` and `(6,1)`, the linear forms are

\[
n+6,\qquad6n+1,
\]

both `7 mod24`. Failure occurs exactly when the prime support lies entirely in one of

\[
\boxed{H_{-6}\quad\text{or}\quad H_{-3}.}
\]

Again this follows by the exact subgroup classification from the previous checkpoint.

## 7. Nine-portal necessary condition for any counterexample

### Theorem 7.1 — maximal exponent-two splitting trap

Let `n≡1 (mod24)` be prime. If `n` is a counterexample to Erdős–Straus, then **all nine** portals in Theorem 5.1 fail. Equivalently, all of the following hold simultaneously:

1. every odd prime divisor of `(n+1)/2` is `1 mod4`;
2. every prime divisor of each of `n+2` and `2n+1` is `1` or `3 mod8`;
3. for each of `(n+3)/4` and `(3n+1)/4`, the support satisfies Proposition 6.3, with the allowed branch determined in part by `n mod48`;
4. for each of `2n+3` and `3n+2`, all prime support lies in either `H_{-6}` or `H_{-4}`;
5. for each of `n+6` and `6n+1`, all prime support lies in either `H_{-6}` or `H_{-3}`.

Every condition is exact for its individual portal. The theorem is only a **necessary** condition for a counterexample and is not a contradiction.

The striking point is that the maximal exponent-two portal family uses only four quadratic characters:

\[
\left(\frac{-2}{\cdot}\right),\quad
\left(\frac{-3}{\cdot}\right),\quad
\left(\frac{-4}{\cdot}\right),\quad
\left(\frac{-6}{\cdot}\right).
\]

Thus the elementary subgroup strategy has been compressed to a finite simultaneous splitting problem.

## 8. Reciprocal-pair coupling: exact tiny-resultant equations

The nine portals contain three reciprocal pairs. Their linear forms are not independent.

### Lemma 8.1 — general reciprocal gcd bound

For coprime `a,u`, set

\[
N_1=an+u,\qquad N_2=un+a.
\]

Then

\[
\gcd(N_1,N_2)\mid |u^2-a^2|
\]

because

\[
uN_1-aN_2=u^2-a^2.
\]

For the three nontrivial reciprocal pairs in the exponent-two family this becomes completely explicit.

### Corollary 8.2 — product 2

\[
\gcd(n+2,2n+1)=3
\]

because `n≡1 mod3`. Therefore

\[
A=\frac{n+2}{3},\qquad B=\frac{2n+1}{3}
\]

are coprime and obey

\[
\boxed{B=2A-1.}
\]

If both portals fail, every prime divisor of both `A` and `B` is `1` or `3 mod8`.

### Corollary 8.3 — product 6, residue-5 pair

Let

\[
g_5=\gcd(2n+3,3n+2).
\]

Then

\[
g_5=\begin{cases}
5,&n\equiv1\pmod5,\\
1,&n\not\equiv1\pmod5,
\end{cases}
\]

and after dividing

\[
A=\frac{2n+3}{g_5},\qquad B=\frac{3n+2}{g_5},
\]

one has `gcd(A,B)=1` and

\[
\boxed{3A-2B=\frac5{g_5}\in\{1,5\}.}
\]

The support of each original linear form, and hence of the quotients after removal of the common factor 5, lies in one of `H_{-6},H_{-4}` whenever both portals fail.

### Corollary 8.4 — product 6, residue-7 pair

Let

\[
g_{35}=\gcd(n+6,6n+1)=\gcd(n+6,35).
\]

Thus

\[
g_{35}=5^{\mathbf 1_{n\equiv4\ (5)}}7^{\mathbf 1_{n\equiv1\ (7)}}.
\]

After dividing

\[
A=\frac{n+6}{g_{35}},\qquad B=\frac{6n+1}{g_{35}},
\]

one has `gcd(A,B)=1` and

\[
\boxed{6A-B=\frac{35}{g_{35}}\in\{1,5,7,35\}.}
\]

If `n≡4 mod5`, then 5 divides **both** original linear forms. Since residue 5 is not in `H_{-3}`, simultaneous portal failure forces the `H_{-6}` branch for **both** `n+6` and `6n+1`.

There are two further one-sided branch forcings from the determinant 11 couplings:

- if `n≡5 mod11`, then 11 divides both `2n+1` and `n+6`; because `11∉H_{-3}`, failure of the `(1,6)` portal forces the `H_{-6}` branch for `n+6`;
- if `n≡9 mod11`, then 11 divides both `n+2` and `6n+1`; failure of the `(6,1)` portal forces the `H_{-6}` branch for `6n+1`.

These are exact necessary branch restrictions, not probabilistic heuristics.

## 10. Failed route / methodological conclusion

The previous checkpoint proposed adding further small quadratic-support portals. Theorem 5.1 shows exactly where that route stops: **all** coprime portals with exponent-two residue groups are already among the nine analyzed here. There is no hidden tenth portal of the same elementary subgroup type.

This is useful negative information. Future work should not spend iterations searching for more `(a,u)` with the expectation that support residues alone form a subgroup for the target modulus. For modulus 16, 20, 28, ... bounded divisor exponents matter because unit residues can have order 4 or larger.
