## 2. Source-exact P0251 statement and current literature check

The exact source is Aldous and Fill, *Reversible Markov Chains and Random Walks on Graphs*, Chapter 12, §12.1.11, Open Problem 12.5.

For even `K`, a state is a family of `K` binary words of length `L`. One step:

1. chooses a uniform random permutation and thereby a uniform perfect matching of the `K` rows;
2. for every matched pair and every column independently, keeps or swaps the two entries with probability `1/2`.

The column sums `m_l` are fixed. The proper state space is therefore

\[
I_{\mathbf m}
=\prod_{\ell=1}^L \Omega_{m_\ell},
\qquad
\Omega_m=\{S\subseteq[K]:|S|=m\},
\]

and the source notes that the transition matrix is symmetric, so the stationary law is uniform on `I_m`.

The source coupling gives

\[
\tau_1^{\rm disc}=O(K\log L),
\]

then Open Problem 12.5 asks to prove

\[
\boxed{\tau_1^{\rm disc}=O(\log K\,\log L).}
\]

The source explicitly suggests group-representation or strong-stationary-time methods by analogy with random transpositions.

## 4. B2 — attack the negation first: there is no `L`-dependent relaxation obstruction

Fix a column sum `m`. Let `H_m=L^2(\Omega_m,\pi_m)` where `pi_m` is uniform. For a perfect matching `M` of `[K]`, write `T_e` for the unitary involution induced on `Omega_m` by transposing the endpoints of an edge `e`. Define

\[
A_{M,m}
=\prod_{e\in M}\frac{I+T_e}{2}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

The transpositions in a perfect matching are disjoint, so the factors commute.

### Lemma 4.1 — fixed-matching update is an orthogonal projection

For every `M,m`, `A_{M,m}` is exactly the P0251 one-column transition operator conditional on `M`, and it is an orthogonal projection.

#### Proof

For one matched edge `e={a,b}`, independently swapping or not swapping the two entries with probability `1/2` applies the kernel `(I+T_e)/2`. Distinct matching edges are disjoint, so their updates commute and are independent, giving (4.1).

Each `T_e` is a self-adjoint unitary involution. Hence `(I+T_e)/2` is the orthogonal projection onto the `T_e`-invariant subspace. Products of commuting orthogonal projections are orthogonal projections. ∎

For the full `L`-column chain, conditional on `M` the column swap coins are independent, so the operator is

\[
A_M=\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^L A_{M,m_\ell}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The actual one-step operator is

\[
P=\mathbb E_M A_M.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Let

\[
Q_m=\mathbb E_M A_{M,m}
\tag{4.4}
\]

be the one-column operator, and let `lambda(m)` be its largest eigenvalue on the mean-zero subspace `H_m^0`. Since every `A_{M,m}` is positive semidefinite, so is `Q_m`.

### Theorem 4.2 — exact relaxation reduction to one column

For every even `K`, every `L`, and every fixed column-sum vector `m` with at least one nontrivial column,

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_2(P)=
\max_{\ell:\,0<m_\ell<K}\lambda(m_\ell).
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

Equivalently, the full multi-column shared-matching chain has exactly the same relaxation bottleneck as its slowest one-column quotient. In particular, **adding columns cannot create a new eigenvalue closer to 1**.

#### Proof

Decompose each one-column Hilbert space orthogonally as

\[
H_{m_\ell}=\mathbf 1\oplus H_{m_\ell}^0.
\]

Therefore

\[
\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^L H_{m_\ell}
=
\bigoplus_{S\subseteq[L]} \mathcal H_S,
\]

where

\[
\mathcal H_S
=
\bigotimes_{\ell\in S} H_{m_\ell}^0
\otimes
\bigotimes_{\ell\notin S}\mathbf 1.
\]

Each `A_{M,m}` fixes constants and, being self-adjoint Markov, preserves the mean-zero subspace. Hence every `A_M` and `P` preserve every `H_S`.

Fix nonempty `S` and choose `j in S`. Put

\[
B_{M,j}=\bigotimes_{\ell\ne j}A_{M,m_\ell}.
\]

This is an orthogonal projection, so `0 <= B_{M,j} <= I`. Since `A_{M,m_j}` is also positive semidefinite,

\[
0
\le
A_{M,m_j}\otimes B_{M,j}
\le
A_{M,m_j}\otimes I
\tag{4.6}
\]

in Loewner order. Averaging over `M` gives

\[
P|_{\mathcal H_S}
\le
(Q_{m_j}\otimes I)|_{\mathcal H_S}.
\]

Because the `j`th tensor factor of `H_S` is mean-zero,

\[
\langle f,Pf\rangle
\le
\lambda(m_j)\|f\|_2^2
\qquad
(f\in\mathcal H_S).
\]

This holds for every `j in S`, hence

\[
\lambda_{\max}(P|_{\mathcal H_S})
\le
\min_{j\in S}\lambda(m_j)
\le
\max_\ell\lambda(m_\ell).
\tag{4.7}
\]

Taking the maximum over all nonempty `S` gives the upper bound in (4.5).

For the reverse inequality, choose a column `j` attaining `max_l lambda(m_l)` and a one-column eigenfunction `g in H_{m_j}^0` with `Q_{m_j}g=lambda(m_j)g`. Regard `g` as a full-state function depending only on column `j`. Every other factor of (4.2) fixes constants, so

\[
Pg
=
\mathbb E_M A_{M,m_j}g
=
Q_{m_j}g
=
\lambda(m_j)g.
\]

Thus the upper bound is attained. ∎

### Consequence for the negation attack

A counterexample to Open Problem 12.5 cannot arise merely because the common matching produces an `L`-dependent spectral gap tending to zero. The full problem is therefore a **high-order total-variation/entropy/multiplicity problem**, not a relaxation-time problem.

This is mathematically useful but is not source closure: a constant relaxation time plus the crude `pi_min` bound is far too weak on the exponentially large product state space.

## 5. B14 — find the group acting: exact Bernstein involution walk in each column

For one column, selecting swaps on a perfect matching produces a random permutation

\[
\tau=\prod_{e\in F}(e),
\]

where `F` is the random set of active matching edges.

### Proposition 5.1 — exact active-edge law

Let `B=|F|`. Then

\[
B\sim\operatorname{Bin}(K/2,1/2),
\]

and conditional on `B=b`, `F` is uniform over all `b`-edge matchings of `[K]`.

#### Proof

Conditional on the chosen perfect matching `M`, each of its `K/2` edges is selected independently with probability `1/2`, so the count is binomial.

Fix a particular `b`-matching `F`. The number of perfect matchings extending it is

\[
(K-2b-1)!!,
\]

independent of `F`. Every extension has the same probability, and within an extension the coin pattern selecting exactly the edges of `F` has probability `2^{-K/2}`. Hence every `b`-matching has the same unconditional probability. ∎

Thus at `p=1/2` the one-column group increment is exactly Bernstein's random involution generator. A column with sum `m` is simply the quotient action of this `S_K` walk on the `m`-subsets.

### Corollary 5.2 — every one-column quotient mixes in `O(log K)`

Bernstein proves `O(log K)` total-variation mixing for the `S_K` involution walk at fixed `p=1/2`. Total variation cannot increase under the quotient map from a permutation to the image of a fixed `m`-subset. Therefore every P0251 one-column chain has `O(log K)` total-variation mixing, uniformly in its column sum.

This confirms, with later primary literature, the single-column analogy that Aldous–Fill only described heuristically.

### Why this still does not solve Open Problem 12.5

The full P0251 increment is **not** a product of `L` independent Bernstein involutions. The perfect matching `M_t` is shared by all columns at time `t`; only the keep/swap bits are independent across columns.

Equivalently, conditional on the whole matching history `(M_1,...,M_t)`, the column permutation histories are independent, but after averaging over that common environment they are correlated. A union bound over `L` independent stationary times is therefore not justified.

The missing source-level ingredient is a quenched or high-order estimate strong enough to control this shared matching environment simultaneously across `L` columns.

## 6. B25 — count the same shared restriction two ways: an exact cross-column eigenmode

The shared environment does create genuine column correlations, so it is useful to compute one exactly.

Take two columns `a,b` with sums `m_a,m_b`, and write

\[
u_i=x_{i,a}-\frac{m_a}{K},
\qquad
v_i=x_{i,b}-\frac{m_b}{K}.
\]

Then `sum_i u_i=sum_i v_i=0`. Define

\[
F_{ab}(x)
=
\sum_{i=1}^K u_i v_i.
\tag{6.1}
\]

### Theorem 6.1 — exact pair-correlation eigenvalue

For the full P0251 chain,

\[
\boxed{
PF_{ab}
=
\frac{K-2}{2(K-1)}F_{ab}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Thus the most obvious common-matching pair-correlation mode contracts by at least a factor `1/2` per generation asymptotically; it is not an order-`K` obstruction.

#### Proof

Condition on a perfect matching `M`. For one matched pair `{r,s}`, the swap choices in columns `a` and `b` are independent fair coins. Therefore, after the update,

\[
\mathbb E[
u'_rv'_r+u'_sv'_s
\mid M,x
]
=
\frac12(u_r+u_s)(v_r+v_s).
\]

Summing over all matched pairs,

\[
\mathbb E[F'_{ab}\mid M,x]
=
\frac12 F_{ab}
+
\frac12
\sum_{\{r,s\}\in M}(u_rv_s+u_sv_r).
\tag{6.3}
\]

Now average over the uniform perfect matching. For every fixed `r`, its partner is uniform over the other `K-1` vertices, so

\[
\mathbb E_M
\sum_{\{r,s\}\in M}(u_rv_s+u_sv_r)
=
\frac1{K-1}\sum_{r\ne s}u_rv_s.
\]

Using the zero-sum identities,

\[
\sum_{r\ne s}u_rv_s
=
\left(\sum_r u_r\right)\left(\sum_s v_s\right)
-\sum_r u_rv_r
=
-F_{ab}.
\]

Substituting into (6.3),

\[
PF_{ab}
=
\frac12\left(1-\frac1{K-1}\right)F_{ab}
=
\frac{K-2}{2(K-1)}F_{ab}.
\]

∎

The same two-way matching count is the mechanism behind Proposition 5.1: one may first choose a perfect matching and then its active edges, or first specify the active `b`-matching and count its perfect-matching extensions. Both views are exact.
