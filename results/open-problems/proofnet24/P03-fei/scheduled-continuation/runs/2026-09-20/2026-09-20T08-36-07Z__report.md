# 4. B12 — the conserved Boolean Fourier lattice gives a constant-factor Shannon proxy

The first new theorem is all-dimensional and uses a property absent for an arbitrary probability distribution: normalized Boolean Walsh coefficients lie on a dyadic lattice.

## Theorem 4.1 — dimension-adaptive Renyi proxy is uniformly equivalent to Fourier entropy

For every Boolean function on `n>=2` variables,

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\ln2}{2}H(f)
\le \Phi_n(f)
\le c_n H(f)
<\frac12 H(f),
}
\tag{4.1}
\]
where

\[
c_n=
\frac{2^{1-1/n}-1}{2(1-1/n)}.
\tag{4.2}
\]
For `n=1`, both `H` and `Phi_1` vanish. Consequently

\[
\boxed{2\Phi_n(f)\le H(f)\le\frac2{\ln2}\Phi_n(f).}
\tag{4.3}
\]
Thus a dimension-free inequality `Phi_n(f)<=K I(f)` is equivalent, at the level of existence of a finite universal constant, to FEI.

### Proof

For `n>=1`,

\[
\widehat f(S)=2^{-n}\sum_x f(x)(-1)^{S\cdot x}.
\]

The numerator is a sum of `2^n` signs. Since `2^n` is even, every nonzero numerator is an even integer. Hence every nonzero Fourier coefficient satisfies

\[
|\widehat f(S)|\ge2^{1-n},
\qquad
p_S\ge2^{2-2n}.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Let `X=-ln p_S` when `S` is sampled from the squared Fourier law. Then

\[
0\le X\le2(n-1)\ln2,
\qquad
H_{\rm nat}(f)=\mathbb E X=(\ln2)H(f).
\tag{4.5}
\]

Put `delta=1/(2n)`. Since

\[
M_{s_n}(f)
=\sum_S p_S^{1-1/(2n)}
=\mathbb E e^{\delta X},
\]
we have

\[
\Phi_n(f)
=n\mathbb E(e^{\delta X}-1)
=\frac12\mathbb E\left[X\,r(\delta X)\right],
\tag{4.6}
\]
where `r(y)=(e^y-1)/y` and `r(0)=1`. The function `r` is increasing on `[0,infinity)`. From (4.5),

\[
0\le\delta X\le(1-1/n)\ln2=:a_n.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac12H_{\rm nat}(f)
\le\Phi_n(f)
\le\frac12r(a_n)H_{\rm nat}(f).
\tag{4.7}
\]

Converting natural entropy to bits gives the lower bound in (4.1). Moreover

\[
\frac{\ln2}{2}r(a_n)
=\frac{2^{1-1/n}-1}{2(1-1/n)}=c_n.
\]

For `0<t<1`, convexity of `2^t` places its graph strictly below the chord joining `(0,1)` and `(1,2)`, so `2^t<1+t`. Taking `t=1-1/n` gives `c_n<1/2`, proving (4.1)--(4.3). For one variable every Boolean function is constant or a character, hence its squared spectrum is a point mass and both quantities vanish. QED.

\boxed{

\tag{5.2}

## Theorem 6.1 — choosing `s_n=2-1/n` but keeping that same exponent on the children still fails for every finite constant

\[
\operatorname{Inf}_t(F_d)=\delta:=N^{-1}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Set `n=d+1`, `s=s_n=2-1/n`, and use the **same** exponent `s` for parent and both children. Then

\[
\boxed{
\frac{D_{s,t}(F_d)}{\delta}\longrightarrow2-\sqrt2,
}
\tag{6.2}
\]
so

\[
\boxed{
\frac{nD_{s,t}(F_d)}{\operatorname{Inf}_t(F_d)}
\sim(2-\sqrt2)n\longrightarrow\infty.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Thus even a dimension-dependent exponent approaching two does **not** repair the fixed-exponent local payment if the child parameter is frozen at the parent value.

### Proof

Every Fourier coefficient of `g` has magnitude `rho`, and the fractions of positive/negative Fourier signs are `(1+rho)/2` and `(1-rho)/2`. For `k=1,2`, define

\[
B_k(\rho,s)
=\frac{1+\rho}{2}(1-k\rho)^s
+\frac{1-\rho}{2}(1+k\rho)^s.
\tag{6.4}
\]

The child and parent `s`-masses are exactly

\[
M_s(g)=N^{1-s/2},
\qquad
M_s(h)=N^{1-s/2}B_2(\rho,s),
\tag{6.5}
\]

\[
M_s(F_d)=N^{1-s/2}B_1(\rho,s)+N^{1-s}.
\tag{6.6}
\]

Indeed the control-paired parent Fourier coefficients are `rho(epsilon_S-rho)` and `rho^2`, while the flipped child coefficients are `rho(epsilon_S-2rho)`.

Uniformly for `s` near two,

\[
B_k(\rho,s)
=1+\left[-sk+\frac{s(s-1)}2k^2\right]\rho^2+O(\rho^3).
\tag{6.7}
\]

Hence, writing `A=N^{1-s/2}`,

\[
\frac{D_{s,t}(F_d)}{\rho^2}
=A\frac{s(1-s)}2+N^{1/n}+O(A\rho).
\tag{6.8}
\]

As `d->infinity`, `s->2`, `A=N^{1/(2n)}->sqrt2`, and `N^{1/n}->2`. Equation (6.2) follows, and (6.3) follows from `delta=rho^2`. QED.
