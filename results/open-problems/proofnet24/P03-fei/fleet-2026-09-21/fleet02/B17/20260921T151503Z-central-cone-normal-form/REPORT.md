## 2. Exact central-cone spectrum and influence

Let f:F_2^n->{+1,-1} be balanced, so E f=0, and set
    A(z)=(1-f(z))/2.
Then E A=1/2. Add y in F_2 and put
    C_f(z,y)=(-1)^{y A(z)}.

Using chi_y=(-1)^y,
    C_f(z,y)=(1-A(z)) + chi_y A(z).                       (2.1)
Indeed the y=0 branch is the constant +1 and the y=1 branch is f.

Since
    1-A=(1+f)/2,
    A=(1-f)/2,
and fhat(empty)=0, the Walsh coefficients are:

- no-y layer:
      Chat_f(empty)=1/2,
      Chat_f(S)=fhat(S)/2 for S nonempty;
- y layer:
      Chat_f({y})=1/2,
      Chat_f(S union {y})=-fhat(S)/2 for S nonempty.

Therefore the squared spectral law consists of two atoms of mass 1/4 and, for every nonempty S, two atoms of mass p_f(S)/4. Because f is balanced, its entire spectral mass is on nonempty S.

### Proposition 2.1 — entropy identity

The two 1/4 atoms contribute one bit total. For one old spectral atom of mass p,
    -2 (p/4) log2(p/4)
      = -(p/2) log2 p + p.
Summing p over the old spectrum gives another one bit plus H(f)/2. Hence
    boxed: H(C_f)=2+H(f)/2.                               (2.2)

### Proposition 2.2 — influence identity

For an old coordinate i, the y=0 branch is constant and the y=1 branch is f, so
    Inf_i(C_f)=Inf_i(f)/2.                                (2.3)
Flipping y changes C_f exactly when A=1, which has probability 1/2:
    Inf_y(C_f)=1/2.                                       (2.4)
Thus
    boxed: I(C_f)=1/2+I(f)/2.                             (2.5)

Every influence of C_f is <=1/2. In the folded notation
    J(g)=sum_i min(Inf_i(g),1-Inf_i(g)),
we therefore have
    J(C_f)=I(C_f).                                        (2.6)

The phase polynomial of C_f is exactly y A(z). Hence **every nonlinear monomial contains the same central variable y**, and there is no base phase avoiding y.

## 3. PROVED: central-cone normal form is FEI-complete

### Theorem 3.1

Fix a finite C. The following are equivalent.

(A) Every uniform-cube Boolean h satisfies H(h)<=C I(h).

(B) Every central cone C_f generated from a balanced Boolean f satisfies
    H(C_f)<=C I(C_f).

Consequently, if
    C_FEI=sup_{nonconstant h} H(h)/I(h),
and
    C_cone=sup_{balanced f} H(C_f)/I(C_f),
then
    C_cone=C_FEI
(with the same statement in the extended-real sense).

### Proof

(A)=>(B) is immediate because central cones are Boolean functions.

For (B)=>(A), let h be an arbitrary Boolean function. Fix an integer m>=1 and take m disjoint copies:
    T_m(x^(1),...,x^(m))=product_{r=1}^m h(x^(r)).
The Fourier law of a disjoint product is the product of the component Fourier laws, hence exactly
    H(T_m)=m H(h),                                        (3.1)
    I(T_m)=m I(h).                                        (3.2)

Now add one fresh suspension bit s:
    f_m(s,x)=(-1)^s T_m(x).
Then f_m is balanced. Multiplication by the new Walsh character simply shifts every spectral set by s, while flipping s is always pivotal, so
    H(f_m)=m H(h),                                        (3.3)
    I(f_m)=1+m I(h).                                      (3.4)

Apply the central-cone construction to f_m. By (2.2)-(2.5),
    H(C_{f_m})=2+(m/2)H(h),                               (3.5)
    I(C_{f_m})=1+(m/2)I(h).                               (3.6)

Assumption (B) gives
    2+(m/2)H(h) <= C[1+(m/2)I(h)].
Rearranging,
    H(h) <= C I(h) + (2C-4)/m.                           (3.7)
Let m tend to infinity through finite integers. Then
    H(h)<=C I(h).
Since h was arbitrary, (A) holds. QED.

### Why this is a useful obstruction

The reduction does not need a complicated shared-variable graph, many selectors, or a broad Fourier support. It pushes arbitrary FEI hardness into an **already folded** function with:

- one distinguished coordinate of influence exactly 1/2;
- every other influence at most 1/2;
- every nonlinear ANF monomial passing through that distinguished coordinate;
- one branch identically constant.

Therefore “central star geometry”, “all nonlinear terms share one control”, or “one branch is constant” is not by itself a simplifying universal hypothesis. Any proof using those properties must additionally exploit a gate invariant that excludes arbitrary balanced gates.

## 5. PROVED: balanced quadratic gates are easy

The normal-form theorem says arbitrary gates are full-hard. The next question is where gate complexity first matters. Degree <=2 is completely controlled.

### Theorem 5.1 — quadratic-gate cone bound

Let f=(-1)^A be balanced, where A:F_2^n->F_2 has algebraic degree at most 2. Then
    H(C_f)/I(C_f) <= 12/5.                                (5.1)
If A is affine, the ratio is at most 2.

### Proof

The affine case first. A balanced affine phase is a nonconstant Walsh character, so H(f)=0 and I(f)>=1. By (2.2) and (2.5),
    H(C_f)/I(C_f)
      =2 / [(1+I(f))/2]
      =4/(1+I(f))
      <=2.                                                (5.2)

Now suppose the quadratic part is nonzero. Remove the irrelevant constant from A and let B be the polar bilinear matrix of the quadratic part:
    B(u,v)=A(u+v)+A(u)+A(v)+A(0).
Over F_2, B is alternating and has even rank r>=2.

#### Standard quadratic Walsh fact

The Walsh transform of a quadratic phase is flat on an affine support of size 2^r. Equivalently, its autocorrelation is supported on the radical of B. Thus
    H(f)=r.                                               (5.3)

For completeness, the only part of the standard calculation needed below is also visible from derivatives. The derivative in coordinate i is an affine phase whose linear part is the i-th row of B.

- If row i is nonzero, that affine derivative is balanced, so
      Inf_i(f)=1/2.
- If row i is zero, the derivative is constant, so
      Inf_i(f) is 0 or 1.                                (5.4)

Let s be the number of nonzero rows of B. Since rank(B)=r, s>=r.

If s>=r+1, the nonzero-row coordinates alone give
    I(f)>=s/2 >= (r+1)/2.                                 (5.5)

It remains to handle s=r. Then exactly n-r coordinate rows are zero. Their coordinate basis vectors lie in rad(B), and because dim rad(B)=n-r they span the radical.

A quadratic exponential sum is balanced precisely when the affine-linear part of A restricted to rad(B) is nonzero: if some radical vector u has A(x+u)+A(x)=1 identically, the sum cancels in u-pairs; if the restriction is zero, the phase descends to a nondegenerate quadratic form on the quotient and its Gauss sum has nonzero magnitude. Since f is balanced, at least one zero-row coordinate therefore has constant derivative 1 and contributes influence 1. Hence in the s=r case
    I(f)>=r/2+1 >= (r+1)/2.                               (5.6)

Combining (5.5)-(5.6),
    I(f)>=(r+1)/2.                                        (5.7)

Use the exact cone identities:
    H(C_f)=2+r/2=(r+4)/2,
    I(C_f)>=1/2+(r+1)/4=(r+3)/4.
Therefore
    H(C_f)/I(C_f)
      <= 2(r+4)/(r+3)
      = 2+2/(r+3)
      <= 12/5,                                            (5.8)
because r>=2. QED.

### Sharpness inside this theorem

The exact finite checker finds equality 12/5 already at n=3, for a balanced rank-2 quadratic phase with I(f)=3/2. Thus the coefficient 12/5 is sharp for the proof statement as written.

This theorem is not subsumed merely by saying “quadratic functions are partially bent”: the object being bounded is the **central cone C_f**, whose ANF degree can be 3 even when A has degree 2. Potapov's sharp partially-bent coefficient 2 is relevant background for the gate itself, but does not directly give (5.1).

## 8. Better proof route forced by the obstruction

The false route was:

    “peel or bound every pure high-degree gate leaf by a universal small constant
     because all its nonlinear monomials share one central variable.”

The normal-form theorem kills that route at full generality.

A viable replacement must expose a **gate complexity invariant** Q(A) with all three properties:

1. Q is small on an analytically controllable gate class and gives an FEI payment for C_f.
2. Q is stable or summable under the restriction/composition operations needed to reduce a general Boolean function.
3. Q cannot be trivially bounded for arbitrary balanced gates, because arbitrary gates are FEI-complete under Theorem 3.1.

The quadratic theorem gives the first nontrivial anchor:
    degree(A)<=2  ==>  H(C_f)/I(C_f)<=12/5.
The next genuinely informative target is therefore not “another leaf” but one of:

- bounded algebraic-degree gates d>=3, seeking a coefficient depending only on d;
- low polar/derivative-rank gates, where the quadratic proof suggests rank rather than arity as the right state;
- a decomposition that pays the genuinely high-degree gate core while peeling the already-cheap quadratic fringe.

Any bounded-degree theorem must be proved without assuming automatic integrality of Fourier magnitude packets, and any recursive version must retain the known cancellation/activation terms rather than imposing a false prescribed-coordinate charge.
