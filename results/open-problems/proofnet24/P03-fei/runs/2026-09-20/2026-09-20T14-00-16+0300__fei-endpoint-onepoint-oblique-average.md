## 4. Main theorem — arbitrary-background one-point sibling payment

### Theorem 4.1

Let `f` be Boolean on `n>=2` physical variables and query coordinate `i`. Suppose, after an optional sign change of one child, the two children differ at exactly one point of the residual `(n-1)`-cube. Equivalently, their relative sign is constant except at one residual input. Then

\[
\boxed{
A_i^{(n)}(f)\le4j_i(f)\le4\operatorname{Inf}_i(f).
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The background child is completely arbitrary. No quadratic, symmetry, low-degree, spectral-flatness, fresh-control, or independence hypothesis is imposed.

If the children agree except at one point, then

\[
j_i(f)=\operatorname{Inf}_i(f)=2^{1-n}.
\]

If they are opposite except at one point, multiplication of the parent by the queried character changes `Inf_i` to its complement but preserves all Fourier magnitudes and all `Phi` values, reducing to the first case. Thus it suffices to prove the agreeing case.

### Proof: normalization and exact dyadic moment reduction

Let

\[
m=n-1,\qquad N=2^m,\qquad \delta=N^{-1}.
\]

Translate the residual input so that the exceptional point is zero, multiply the whole function by a sign so that `g(0)=1`, and write

\[
h=g(1-2\mathbf1_{\{0\}}).
\]

If `a_T=\widehat g(T)`, then

\[
\widehat h(T)=a_T-2\delta.
\]

The two parent coefficients in the Fourier fibre over `T` are therefore

\[
\widehat f(T)=a_T-\delta,
\qquad
\widehat f(T\cup\{i\})=\delta.
\tag{4.2}
\]

For `d>=1` put

\[
\psi_d(x)=d\left(|x|^{2-1/d}-x^2\right).
\]

Parseval gives `Phi_d=\sum\psi_d(\widehat f)`. Since `a_T` is a Boolean Walsh coefficient on `m` bits,

\[
a_T=\delta W_T
\]

with even integer `W_T`. Put

\[
t_T=W_T-1.
\]

Every `t_T` is odd. The normalization `g(0)=1` excludes `W_T=-N`, hence

\[
|t_T|\le N-1.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Fourier inversion at zero and Parseval give

\[
\sum_T W_T=N,
\qquad
\sum_T W_T^2=N^2.
\]

Since there are exactly `N` Fourier labels,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_Tt_T=0,
\qquad
\sum_Tt_T^2=N(N-1).
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

This pair of global moment identities is the shared spectral budget that a coefficientwise argument discards.

Let

\[
r=2-\frac1n,\qquad
s=2-\frac1{n-1},\qquad
c=2^{1-1/n}=N^{1/n}.
\]

For `n=2`, interpret `s=1`. A direct substitution using

\[
\delta^r=c\delta^2,
\qquad
\delta^s=2\delta^2
\]

gives

\[
A_i^{(n)}(f)=\delta^2\sum_T g_n(t_T),
\tag{4.5}
\]

where the even scalar function

\[
\boxed{
 g_n(t)=
 n c\left(|t|^r+1\right)
 -(n-1)\left(|t+1|^s+|t-1|^s\right)
 -(t^2+1).
}
\tag{4.6}
\]

The crucial scalar statement is not a constant pointwise bound on `g_n`; such a bound is false near the largest lattice amplitudes. Instead we use a quadratic majorant whose extra term is paid exactly by (4.4).

### Lemma 4.2 — dyadic scalar majorant

For every `n>=2`, `N=2^{n-1}`, and every odd integer `t` with `|t|<=N-1`,

\[
\boxed{
 g_n(t)\le3+\frac{1+t^2}{N}.
}
\tag{4.7}
\]

The proof consists of an analytic infinite-dimensional range and a finite exact rational certificate. Both parts are given below.

### Deduction of Theorem 4.1 from Lemma 4.2

Sum (4.7) over the `N` Fourier labels and use (4.4):

\[
\begin{aligned}
A_i^{(n)}(f)
&\le\delta^2\left[
3N+\frac{N+\sum_Tt_T^2}{N}
\right]\\
&=\delta^2\left[3N+N\right]
=4\delta.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since `delta=j_i=Inf_i` in the chosen orientation, (4.1) follows. QED modulo Lemma 4.2.

## 5. Complete proof of the scalar majorant

By evenness take `t>=1`. Write

\[
L=\ln(N/t).
\]

### 5.1 A coarse bound that handles the entire middle range

Convexity of `x\mapsto x^s`, `s>1`, gives

\[
(t+1)^s+(t-1)^s\ge2t^s.
\]

Using `c=N^{1/n}` and `N^{1/(n-1)}=2`, we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
g_n(t)
&\le nc-1
+t^2\left[n e^{L/n}-(n-1)e^{L/(n-1)}-1\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Define

\[
q_n(L)=(n-1)e^{L/(n-1)}+1-ne^{L/n}.
\]

Then `q_n(0)=q_n'(0)=0`, while

\[
q_n''(L)
=\frac{e^{L/(n-1)}}{n-1}-\frac{e^{L/n}}n
\ge\frac1{n(n-1)}.
\]

Therefore

\[
q_n(L)\ge\frac{L^2}{2n(n-1)}
\]

and hence

\[
\boxed{
 g_n(t)\le nc-1-\frac{t^2L^2}{2n(n-1)}.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

In particular, if

\[
tL\ge2n\sqrt{n-1},
\tag{5.3}
\]

then `g_n(t)<-1`, since `c<2`.

For `n>=17`, condition (5.3) holds throughout

\[
4\sqrt n\le t\le N/2.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Indeed `t\ln(N/t)` is concave in `t`, so its minimum on (5.4) is at an endpoint. At the lower endpoint, (5.3) is equivalent to

\[
2\left[(n-1)\ln2-\ln4-\tfrac12\ln n\right]
\ge\sqrt{n(n-1)}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

The left minus right is positive at `n=17` (`0.0824852...`) and has positive derivative thereafter; for example `ln2>0.693` and

\[
\frac{2n-1}{2\sqrt{n(n-1)}}<\frac{17}{16}
\]

make the derivative positive for every `n>=17`. At the upper endpoint `(N/2)ln2` already exceeds `2n sqrt(n-1)` at `n=17`, and the former doubles when `n` increases while the latter grows by a factor less than two. Thus (5.4) is proved.

### 5.2 The large-amplitude tail

The same convexity step before (5.2) also gives

\[
g_n(t)\le nc-1<2n-1
\tag{5.6}
\]

for every `1<=t<=N`. If `t>=N/2` and `n>=17`, then

\[
3+\frac{1+t^2}{N}
\ge3+\frac N4
>2n-1.
\]

So (4.7) holds on the tail.

### 5.3 Small amplitudes in all sufficiently large dimensions

It remains to handle `t^2<16n`. For `n>=73` this is analytic.

Set `b=ln2`. The identities

\[
ct^r=2t^2e^{-\ln(2t)/n},
\qquad
c=2e^{-b/n},
\]

and

\[
u^s=u^2e^{-\ln u/(n-1)}
\]

hold for positive `u`. Using

\[
e^{-x}\le1-x+\frac{x^2}{2},
\qquad
e^{-x}\ge1-x
\qquad(x\ge0)
\]

in the parent and child terms respectively yields

\[
g_n(t)\le G_\infty(t)
+\frac{t^2\ln^2(2t)+\ln^22}{n},
\tag{5.7}
\]

where, with `0^2 ln0=0`,

\[
G_\infty(t)
=t^2+1-2t^2\ln(2t)-2\ln2
 +(t+1)^2\ln(t+1)+(t-1)^2\ln(t-1).
\tag{5.8}
\]

At `t=1`, `G_infty(1)=2`, hence the right side of (5.7) is less than three for `n>=73`.

For `t>=3`, let `F(x)=x^2\ln x`. Since `F''(x)=2\ln x+3` is increasing,

\[
F(t+1)+F(t-1)-2F(t)
=\int_{-1}^1(1-|u|)F''(t+u)\,du
\le2\ln(t+1)+3.
\]

Writing

\[
a=2\ln2-1,
\]

we get

\[
G_\infty(t)
\le-at^2+4-2\ln2+2\ln(t+1).
\tag{5.9}
\]

Call the right side of (5.9) plus the error term in (5.7) `B_n(t)`. Then

\[
\frac{B_n'(t)}{2t}
=-a+\frac1{t(t+1)}
 +\frac{\ell^2+\ell}{n},
\qquad \ell=\ln(2t).
\tag{5.10}
\]

For `3<=t<4 sqrt(n)`,

\[
\frac1{t(t+1)}\le\frac1{12},
\qquad
\ell\le\ln(8\sqrt n).
\]

The function

\[
R(n)=\frac{\ln^2(8\sqrt n)+\ln(8\sqrt n)}n
\]

is decreasing for `n>=73`, because its derivative has the sign of `1/2-ln^2(8 sqrt(n))`. Elementary enclosures give

\[
R(73)+\frac1{12}<0.38574
<2\ln2-1.
\]

Thus `B_n` is decreasing on this interval. At `t=3`,

\[
B_n(3)
\le13-16\ln2+
\frac{9\ln^26+\ln^22}{73}
<2.313<3.
\tag{5.11}
\]

Hence `g_n(t)<3` for every odd `t` with `t^2<16n` once `n>=73`. Since the right side of (4.7) is greater than three, the scalar majorant follows in this range.

## 6. Endpoint max-splicing consequence

Theorem 4.1 plugs directly into the exact endpoint telescope, with no recombination term to estimate separately.

### Corollary 6.1

Let `T` be a partial physical restriction tree such that at every internal node of remaining dimension at least two, the two queried children agree or are opposite except at one residual input. Suppose every stopping leaf `ell` satisfies

\[
\Phi_{m(\ell)}(f_\ell)\le K I(f_\ell).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_n(f)\le\max\{4,K\}I(f).
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
H(f)\le\frac2{\ln2}\max\{4,K\}I(f).
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

In particular, if the tree is full, then

\[
\boxed{H(f)\le\frac8{\ln2}I(f)=11.5415603271117\ldots I(f).}
\tag{6.4}
\]

#### Proof

The exact partial-tree endpoint telescope is

\[
\Phi_n(f)
=\sum_{v\text{ internal}}w_vA_{i(v)}^{(m(v))}(f_v)
 +\sum_{\ell\text{ leaf}}w_\ell\Phi_{m(\ell)}(f_\ell).
\]

Theorem 4.1 pays every internal term by at most four times its queried influence. The exact physical influence budget is

\[
Q_T+L_T=I(f).
\]

Thus

\[
\Phi_n(f)\le4Q_T+K L_T\le\max\{4,K\}I(f).
\]

Equation (3.2) gives (6.3). A full tree has zero-dimensional leaves, so `K=0`. QED.

This is a restricted closure theorem, not full FEI: arbitrary Boolean functions have not been shown to admit such a sibling tree or certified stopping leaves.

## 7. New translation-averaged oblique derivative identity

The preceding theorem uses the endpoint potential and shared dyadic moments. A different calculation sharpens the companion oblique-alignment mechanism and identifies exactly where that route still loses on rare siblings.

Fix an arbitrary physical split `i`, with residual children `g,h` on `m=n-1` variables. For a residual translation `w` define

\[
d_w(y)=g(y)h(y+w).
\tag{7.1}
\]

A global sign on `d_w` is irrelevant to its influence. For each residual physical coordinate `k`, put

\[
a_k=\operatorname{Inf}_k(g),
\qquad
b_k=\operatorname{Inf}_k(h).
\]

### Theorem 7.1 — exact translation average

\[
\boxed{
2^{-m}\sum_w I(d_w)
=\sum_{k\ne i}\left(a_k+b_k-2a_kb_k\right)
\le2\sum_{k\ne i}j_k(f).
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Therefore there is a residual translation `w` such that

\[
\boxed{
I(d_w)\le2\bigl(J(f)-j_i(f)\bigr).
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Combining this with the companion oblique-derivative theorem (1.2) gives the fresh-variable-free local bounds

\[
\boxed{
B_i(f)\le2\bigl(J(f)-j_i(f)\bigr),
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_iH(f)\le4\bigl(J(f)-j_i(f)\bigr).
}
\tag{7.5}
\]

#### Proof

For a residual coordinate `k`, the multiplicative derivative of (7.1) is

\[
D_kd_w(y)=D_kg(y)D_kh(y+w).
\]

Average over uniform independent `y,w`. Then `y` and `y+w` are independent uniform residual inputs. The two derivative signs are therefore independent signs whose `-1` probabilities are `a_k,b_k`. Their product is `-1` with probability

\[
a_k(1-b_k)+(1-a_k)b_k=a_k+b_k-2a_kb_k.
\]

This proves the equality in (7.2) after summing over `k`.

The parent residual influence is

\[
\beta_k=\frac{a_k+b_k}{2}.
\]

If `beta_k<=1/2`, then

\[
a_k+b_k-2a_kb_k\le a_k+b_k=2\beta_k.
\]

If `beta_k>=1/2`, then `(1-a_k)(1-b_k)>=0` gives

\[
a_k+b_k-2a_kb_k\le2-a_k-b_k=2(1-\beta_k).
\]

Thus the summand is at most `2j_k(f)`, proving (7.2), and averaging gives (7.3).

For completeness, the mechanism behind (1.2) is as follows. After choosing the better global sign of the aligned child, let `delta` be the minority density of `d_w`. Parseval/Hellinger comparison gives `M_i<=delta`; the exact P03 child-spectrum identities give `B_i<=h_2(M_i)` and the same entropy scale for the Jensen--Shannon recombination term. Cube edge-isoperimetry gives `h_2(delta)<=I(d_w)`. Hence `B_i<=I(d_w)` and `Delta_iH<=2I(d_w)`. Substituting (7.3) yields (7.4)--(7.5). QED.

### Corollary 7.2 — dominant folded-coordinate hereditary class

Fix `0<theta<=1`. Suppose a restriction-closed class has the property that every non-character descendant with `J>0` has a physical coordinate `i` satisfying

\[
j_i(f)\ge\theta J(f).
\tag{7.6}
\]

Then there is a full adaptive physical restriction tree with

\[
\boxed{
R_T(f)\le\frac{2(1-\theta)}{\theta}J(f)
\le\frac{2(1-\theta)}{\theta}I(f),
}
\tag{7.7}
\]

and directly

\[
\boxed{
H(f)\le\frac{4(1-\theta)}{\theta}J(f)
\le\frac{4(1-\theta)}{\theta}I(f).
}
\tag{7.8}
\]

#### Proof

For every residual coordinate `k`, concavity of `j(x)=min{x,1-x}` and the exact child-average influence identity give

\[
\frac{j_k(g)+j_k(h)}2\le j_k(f).
\]

The queried coordinate disappears, so

\[
\frac{J(g)+J(h)}2\le J(f)-j_i(f)\le(1-\theta)J(f).
\tag{7.9}
\]

At a `J=0` node the function is a character on its live coordinates, hence has zero Fourier entropy and zero allocation and can stop.

Induct on remaining dimension. If the hereditary constant is `C`, then (7.4) and (7.9) give

\[
R(f)\le[2+C](1-\theta)J(f).
\]

Choosing

\[
C=\frac{2(1-\theta)}\theta
\]

makes the right side `CJ(f)`. The direct argument uses (7.5) instead and closes with `D=4(1-theta)/theta`. QED.

This corollary is all-dimensional and hereditary but restricted by (7.6). It is not a universal FEI theorem.

## 8. Decisive limitation of the oblique-boundary route and why the endpoint theorem matters

The one-point defect

\[
w_n=1-2\mathbf1_{\{0\}}
\]

shows that Theorem 7.1 cannot by itself yield a universal coordinatewise payment. For every coordinate `i`, after restriction one child is constant and the other is a one-point defect on `n-1` variables. For **every** residual translation `w`, `d_w` is a translate/sign of that one-point defect. Therefore

\[
I(d_w)=(n-1)2^{2-n},
\qquad
j_i(w_n)=2^{1-n},
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
\frac{I(d_w)}{j_i(w_n)}=2(n-1)\to\infty.
}
\tag{8.1}
\]

In fact the average bound (7.3) is exact on this family. Thus a strategy that pays each queried endpoint charge solely through a chosen oblique derivative boundary still has an unavoidable linear loss on the canonical rare defect.
