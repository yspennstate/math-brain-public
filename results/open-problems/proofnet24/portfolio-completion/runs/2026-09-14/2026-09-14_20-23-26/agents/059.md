## 1. Process, assumptions, and rate conventions

Let `d >= 3`. Let `S` be a finite symmetric subset of `Z^d \ {0}` containing every nearest-neighbor step `+/-e_i`. Give each signed displacement `a in S` a positive rate `c_a=c_{-a}`. Every **unoriented** edge `{x,x+a}` rings at rate `c_a`; its two orientations do not create two independent clocks. Let
\[
C=\sum_{a\in S}c_a.
\]
Thus one tagged label has total jump rate `C`. In the nearest-neighbor case with coordinate rates `c_i`, `C=2 sum_i c_i`.

For two distinct initial labels, write their chronological positions as `X_t,Y_t`, and put
\[
R_t=Y_t-X_t\in E:=\mathbb Z^d\setminus\{0\},\qquad A:=S.
\]
The contact states are exactly the displacements at which there is a direct edge between the two current positions. Set
\[
H_t=\int_0^t 1_A(R_u)\,du,\qquad H=H_\infty.
\]

### The actual relative generator

For `z != 0`,
\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
Lf(z)=2\sum_{\substack{a\in S\\z+a\ne0}}c_a
       [f(z+a)-f(z)]
   +1_{\{z\in A\}}c_z[f(-z)-f(z)].}
\tag{1.1}
\]

Proof of the factors. Away from a common edge, a relative increment `a` is caused either by the second label moving by `a`, or the first label moving by `-a`; these are two distinct rate-`c_a` clocks. At contact, the two apparent moves into displacement zero refer to the **same common physical edge**. Its one rate-`c_z` ringing swaps both labels and sends `z` to `-z`. Thus the rate-`2c_z` collision term is deleted and one rate-`c_z` swap term is added. This proves (1.1).

The total rate away from a state is `2C` off contact and `2C-c_z` at contact. With long-range steps, the ordinary relative move and the swap can lead to the same target; their rates must then be added. Formula (1.1) already does so.

For nearest neighbors, any ordinary allowed move from a contact state leaves `A`, so
\[
\text{contact departure rate at }\pm e_i=2C-2c_i,
\quad
\text{internal swap rate}=c_i,
\quad
\text{total rate}=2C-c_i.
\tag{1.2}
\]
For `c_i=1`, these are `4d-2`, `1`, and `4d-1`, respectively. Rates of clocks on edges incident to neither label are irrelevant to this two-tag chain.

The mutual-swap count `N_t` has compensator
\[
\int_0^t c_{R_u}1_A(R_u)\,du.
\tag{1.3}
\]
Consequently, in an anisotropic model the mean unweighted contact time and the mean swap count need not agree.

### Transience needed below

The assumptions that `S` contains the nearest-neighbor steps, their rates are positive, and `d>=3` are convenient sufficient hypotheses, not a claim of maximal generality. The ordinary rate-`2c_a` walk is transient, as also follows from the Fourier integral below. Its Green function tends to zero at spatial infinity. The relative chain is identical to that walk outside the finite set `A`, and its punctured nearest-neighbor subgraph is connected.

Here is a direct justification that the finite perturbation remains transient and has finite expected contact time. For the ordinary walk, the probability from `z` of hitting `A` is at most
\[
\sum_{a\in A}\frac{r_0(z-a)}{r_0(0)},
\]
which tends to zero as `|z|` tends to infinity. Choose an exterior state from which this hitting probability is below `1/2`. From each of the finitely many states in `A`, choose a finite positive-probability path in the punctured nearest-neighbor graph to that exterior state. The path followed by permanent avoidance of `A` has a positive probability bounded below over the finite starting set. There is a finite common bound on the number of contact visits in these chosen paths. Applying the strong Markov property in blocks of that many contact visits bounds their expected number by a geometric tail. The holding-time means on finite `A` are bounded, so `E_a H<infinity`. The same argument applies to any enlarged finite set, giving transience of the relative chain. All jump rates are bounded, so neither chain explodes.

The formulas below also apply with a different symmetric finite support whenever its relative chain is transient; that broader case requires checking transience/connectivity rather than silently using the sufficient hypotheses just given.

## 2. Exact resolvent reduction

Let `L^0` be the ordinary translation-invariant walk on all of `Z^d` with
\[
L^0f(z)=2\sum_{a\in S}c_a[f(z+a)-f(z)].
\]
For `lambda>=0`, define its continuous-time resolvent kernel
\[
r_\lambda(x)=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}
                    \mathbb P_0(Z_t=x)\,dt.
\]
At `lambda=0`, the integral is finite under our assumptions. The embedded discrete walk has probabilities `p(a)=c_a/C` and total holding rate `2C`, so
\[
\boxed{r_0(x)=G_p(x)/(2C).}
\tag{2.1}
\]
In particular, the isotropic nearest-neighbor case has `r_0=G_d/(4d)` when each physical edge has rate one.

An exact integral specification of every needed entry is
\[
r_\lambda(x)=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}
\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^d}
 \frac{e^{-i\theta\cdot x}}
 {\lambda+\psi(\theta)}\,d\theta,
\quad
\psi(\theta)=2\sum_{a\in S}c_a(1-\cos(\theta\cdot a)).
\tag{2.2}
\]
The signed sum in (2.2) matters: for nearest-neighbor coordinate rates, `psi(theta)=4 sum_i c_i(1-cos theta_i)`. The nearest-neighbor lower bound makes `1/psi` integrable for `d>=3`; Fourier inversion then also gives decay at infinity.

Kill this ordinary walk on first hitting zero. Its resolvent on `E` is
\[
D_\lambda(x,y)
=r_\lambda(y-x)-\frac{r_\lambda(x)r_\lambda(y)}{r_\lambda(0)}.
\tag{2.3}
\]
Indeed, the strong Markov property at first hitting zero gives
`E_x[e^{-lambda tau_0};tau_0<infinity]=r_lambda(x)/r_lambda(0)`, and subtracting the expected post-hit resolvent gives (2.3). Symmetry is used here.

Let `M_lambda` be the `|A| by |A|` matrix
\[
(M_\lambda)_{ab}=D_\lambda(a,b),\qquad a,b\in A.
\]
Define the symmetric contact correction matrix `B` by
\[
B_{aa}=c_a,\qquad B_{a,-a}=c_a,
\qquad B_{ab}=0\text{ otherwise}.
\tag{2.4}
\]
The block corresponding to `a,-a` is `c_a [[1,1],[1,1]]`. Thus `B` vanishes on odd functions of the displacement; it is not the swap generator alone.

Let
\[
R_\lambda(x,y)=\mathbb E_x\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}
                      1_{\{R_t=y\}}\,dt,
\quad
K_\lambda=(R_\lambda(a,b))_{a,b\in A}.
\]

### Theorem 059-RESOLVENT

All the finite matrices inverted below are invertible, and
\[
\boxed{K_\lambda=(M_\lambda^{-1}-B)^{-1}.}
\tag{2.5}
\]
For every `z in E`,
\[
\boxed{R_\lambda(z,A)=D_\lambda(z,A)M_\lambda^{-1}K_\lambda.}
\tag{2.6}
\]

Proof. The ordinary walk killed at zero has the restriction of `L^0` to `E` as its subgenerator: the diagonal still includes its rate of attempted jumps to zero. To obtain (1.1), add `2c_a` to that diagonal at contact, then add the swap generator with diagonal `-c_a` and off-diagonal `c_a` at `-a`. Their sum is exactly `B`, embedded on `A`.

For `lambda>0`, the resolvent identity gives
\[
K_\lambda=M_\lambda+M_\lambda B K_\lambda,
\]
and hence (2.5). Similarly
\[
R_\lambda(z,A)=D_\lambda(z,A)[I+B K_\lambda]
              =D_\lambda(z,A)M_\lambda^{-1}K_\lambda,
\]
which proves (2.6). Equivalently one may derive both identities by finite-volume matrix inversion and then increase the volume.

Both contact Green matrices are symmetric positive definite. This follows by integrating the positive definite self-adjoint transition semigroups against finite vectors supported on `A`; strict positivity is already present near time zero. At zero discount their entries are finite by transience. Passing `lambda` down to zero in the finite matrices proves the formulas there, and also verifies that `M_0^{-1}-B=K_0^{-1}` is positive definite. This avoids assuming invertibility of a zero-discount infinite operator on all of l2.

### First-contact distribution

Put `tau_A=inf{t>=0:R_t in A}` and let the row vector
\[
\alpha_z^{(\lambda)}(a)
=\mathbb E_z[e^{-\lambda\tau_A};\tau_A<\infty,R_{\tau_A}=a].
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{\alpha_z^{(\lambda)}=D_\lambda(z,A)M_\lambda^{-1}.}
\tag{2.7}
\]
For `z in A`, this is its unit row vector. To prove (2.7), apply the strong Markov property at `tau_A` in (2.6): `R_lambda(z,A)=alpha_z^(lambda)K_lambda`. Before this stopping time the relative chain is the ordinary rate-`2c` walk; zero cannot be reached without first visiting `A`. Thus the first-contact distribution does not depend on the modification at contact, as the formula confirms.

## 3. The finite contact-clock chain and Laplace laws

Write `K=K_0`, `M=M_0`, and
\[
\boxed{Q=-K^{-1}=B-M^{-1},\qquad k=-Q\mathbf1.}
\tag{3.1}
\]
The matrix `Q` is a transient continuous-time **subgenerator**: its off-diagonal entries are nonnegative, its row sums are nonpositive, and `k` is the vector of rates of killing at infinity in contact-clock time. It is symmetric negative definite.

For completeness, obtain the contact-clock chain by deleting the time the relative chain spends outside `A`. A final excursion that never returns becomes absorption at a cemetery state. The strong Markov property at departures and returns makes this a finite-state Markov chain. Its Green matrix is exactly `K`, because occupation of each contact state is unchanged when outside time is deleted. Therefore its generator is `-K^{-1}`. Equivalently, with `U=E\A`, its excursion description is
\[
Q_{ab}=L_{ab}+\sum_{x\in U}L_{ax}
             \mathbb P_x(R_{\tau_A}=b,\tau_A<\infty),
\tag{3.2}
\]
including the diagonal case; an excursion returning to its departure state adds to the diagonal in the compressed clock. In a killed finite model, add its direct physical killing to the last-excursion killing rates. Formula (3.2) identifies the signs and row sums without treating infinite-volume inverses formally.

For an initial probability row vector `alpha` on `A`, the lifetime of this trace chain is exactly `H`. Thus
\[
\boxed{\mathbb P_\alpha(H>u)=\alpha e^{uQ}\mathbf1,}
\tag{3.3}
\]
\[
\boxed{\mathbb E_\alpha e^{-sH}
=\alpha(sI-Q)^{-1}k
=\alpha(I+sK)^{-1}\mathbf1,\qquad s\ge0.}
\tag{3.4}
\]
This is the finite-state phase-type representation, including all orientations and their excursions. The mean and higher moments are
\[
\mathbb E_\alpha H=\alpha K\mathbf1,
\qquad
\mathbb E_\alpha H^n=n!\,\alpha K^n\mathbf1.
\tag{3.5}
\]

For arbitrary `z != 0`, let `alpha_z=alpha_z^(0)` and `p_z=alpha_z 1`. There is an atom `1-p_z` at `H=0`, and
\[
\boxed{\mathbb E_z e^{-sH}
=1-p_z+\alpha_z(I+sK)^{-1}\mathbf1
=1-sR_0(z,A)(I+sK)^{-1}\mathbf1.}
\tag{3.6}
\]
Conditional on first contact, replace `alpha` in (3.3)–(3.4) by `alpha_z/p_z`. In the anisotropic case, this entrance distribution is relevant; it must not be discarded by an isotropic symmetry argument.

There is also the exact mixed transform
\[
\boxed{\mathbb E_z[e^{-\lambda\tau_A-sH};\tau_A<\infty]
=\alpha_z^{(\lambda)}(I+sK_0)^{-1}\mathbf1.}
\tag{3.7}
\]

### Finite physical-time transform

For `lambda>0` and `s>=0`, Feynman–Kac and the resolvent identity give
\[
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb E_z[e^{-sH_t}]\,dt
=\frac1\lambda\left[
1-sR_\lambda(z,A)(I+sK_\lambda)^{-1}\mathbf1\right].}
\tag{3.8}
\]
To see this directly, call the integral `u(z)`. Then
`u=1/lambda-s R_lambda(.,A)u_A`, so
`u_A=(1/lambda)(I+sK_lambda)^(-1)1`, proving (3.8).

The matrix `-K_lambda^{-1}` is the contact-clock generator when the original chain is additionally killed by an independent rate-`lambda` **physical-time** clock. Excursions have physical duration, so this is generally not simply `Q-lambda I`. More explicitly,
\[
-K_\lambda^{-1}=L_{AA}-\lambda I
 +L_{AU}(\lambda I-L_{UU})^{-1}L_{UA},
\tag{3.9}
\]
where the last term is interpreted through the killed excursion kernel. Formula (2.5) is a finite-matrix way of evaluating it.

### Weighted contact rewards and swaps

For a nonnegative vector `w` on `A`, write `W=diag(w)` and
`H_w=int_0^infinity w(R_t)1_A(R_t)dt`. A first-step calculation in the trace chain gives
\[
\mathbb E_\alpha e^{-sH_w}=\alpha(sW-Q)^{-1}k,
\qquad
\mathbb E_\alpha H_w=\alpha K w.
\tag{3.10}
\]
For mutual swaps, (1.3) and monotone convergence give
\[
\mathbb E_z N=\alpha_z K(c_a)_{a\in A},
\tag{3.11}
\]
whereas `E_z H=alpha_z K1`. Formula (3.11) is a compensator identity, not an assertion that the count is conditionally Poisson given the unweighted total contact time.

## 4. Exactly when an exponential law follows

### A common exponential law from every contact state

For any such contact matrix, the following are equivalent:

1. There is `kappa>0` such that `P_a(H>u)=exp(-kappa u)` for every `a in A` and every `u>=0`.
2. `Q1=-kappa 1`, equivalently `k=kappa 1`.
3. `K1=(1/kappa)1`.

Proof. Differentiating the first assertion at zero gives the second. The second implies `exp(uQ)1=exp(-kappa u)1` by the matrix exponential series. Inverting `Q` proves equivalence with the third.

If a symmetry group preserving the weighted relative dynamics acts transitively on `A`, then `Q1` is invariant under that transitive action and hence constant. Transience makes its common negative row sum strictly negative. This explains the orientation-independent exponential law in 004. Transitivity is sufficient, not necessary: the exact condition is the row-sum identity.

For the isotropic nearest-neighbor model with each physical edge rate `c`, put `g=G_d(0)` for the discrete-time simple walk. Then `r_0(0)=g/(4dc)`, `r_0(e)=(g-1)/(4dc)`. Harmonicity at a contact point gives
\[
M\mathbf1=\frac{r_0(e)}{2c\,r_0(0)}\mathbf1,
\qquad B\mathbf1=2c\mathbf1,
\]
and therefore
\[
k=\frac{2c}{g-1}\mathbf1.
\tag{4.1}
\]
This recovers the already reported rate `2/(G_d(0)-1)` at `c=1`, with the expected scaling under a common change of clock rate.

### A spectral criterion for a specified initial law

Let the distinct eigenvalues of `-Q` be `kappa_j>0` and their orthogonal projections be `P_j`. Since `Q` is symmetric,
\[
\mathbb P_\alpha(H>u)=\sum_j
       (\alpha P_j\mathbf1)e^{-\kappa_j u}.
\tag{4.2}
\]
For a fixed initial law, a single exponential occurs exactly when all but one of these coefficients vanish. Without full transitivity, a specially chosen initial law can still give an exponential, for example a quasi-stationary initial law. Unequal row sums therefore do not by themselves prove nonexponentiality from **every** deterministic state.

For a uniform initial contact state, all coefficients in (4.2) are nonnegative:
\[
\mathbb P_{\rm unif}(H>u)=\frac1{|A|}\sum_j
             \|P_j\mathbf1\|^2e^{-\kappa_j u}.
\tag{4.3}
\]
Its survival second-derivative defect is exactly
\[
S''(0)-S'(0)^2
=\frac1{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}k_a^2
 -\left(\frac1{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}k_a\right)^2.
\tag{4.4}
\]
Thus unequal killing rates imply a nontrivial positive exponential mixture for uniform contact initialization. The parent independently suggested using this criterion while the two-type example below was being developed.

For an arbitrary deterministic or nonuniform starting state, the coefficients in (4.2) can be negative. The valid general statement is a phase-type/matrix-exponential law, not necessarily a nonnegative mixture of scalar exponentials. The examples below make this distinction explicit.

### Sign symmetry reduces nearest-neighbor contact matrices

For coordinate edge rates, reflection `a -> -a` leaves every contact law unchanged. On even functions of contact displacement, the `2d` contact states reduce to `d` unoriented coordinates. The explicit matrix entering this reduction is
\[
M^+_{ij}=r_0(e_j-e_i)+r_0(e_j+e_i)
 -\frac{2r_0(e_i)r_0(e_j)}{r_0(0)},
\tag{4.5}
\]
and the reduced generator is
\[
\boxed{Q^+=2\,\operatorname{diag}(c_1,\ldots,c_d)-(M^+)^{-1}.}
\tag{4.6}
\]
All pair orbits have size two, so this quotient is symmetric. If several coordinate rates coincide, further lumping may reduce the matrix again; such a further quotient is reversible with orbit-size weights and need not be symmetric in its ordinary coordinates.

## 5. An exact infinite anisotropic nonexponential family

### Theorem 059-ANISO-NONEXP

Consider fresh chronological nearest-neighbor interchange on Z^4 with physical edge rates
\[
c_1=\varepsilon,\qquad c_2=c_3=c_4=1.
\]
There is `epsilon_0>0` such that, for every `0<epsilon<epsilon_0`, the total contact time `H` is nonexponential from **each** initial displacement in `{+/-e_1,...,+/-e_4}`. The contact law reduces exactly to a two-state phase-type law, with vertical type `V={+/-e_1}` and horizontal type `T={+/-e_2,+/-e_3,+/-e_4}`.

The theorem provides an existence interval, not a numerical value of `epsilon_0` and not a claim for every unequal set of rates.

### Proof: a transient degenerate limit

For every positive epsilon the preceding hypotheses hold. The free relative Fourier denominator is
\[
4\varepsilon(1-\cos\theta_1)
 +4\sum_{j=2}^4(1-\cos\theta_j).
\]
Its reciprocal is bounded by the reciprocal of the horizontal three-dimensional denominator, which is integrable on the four-dimensional torus. Dominated convergence gives, for every lattice point `x`,
\[
r^{(\varepsilon)}_0(x)\longrightarrow
 1_{\{x_1=0\}}r^{(3)}_0(x_2,x_3,x_4),
\tag{5.1}
\]
where `r^(3)` is the continuous-time Green kernel of the three-dimensional rate-2-per-neighbor walk.

Keep the eight observation/contact states fixed in this limit. At epsilon zero, the vertical states are auxiliary observation states, even though their physical edge rate has become zero; they are used only to compute a matrix limit of the positive-rate models. The relative process splits into three-dimensional layers, all transient. Hence the limiting `M` and `K` contact matrices remain finite and positive definite. By finite-dimensional continuity in (2.5), `Q^(epsilon)` and its killing vector converge to their values at zero.

Write `g=G_3(0)` for discrete-time three-dimensional simple random walk. At zero epsilon, each vertical observation state is the only observed site in its layer, and
\[
k_V(0)=\frac1{r^{(3)}_0(0)}=\frac{12}{g}.
\tag{5.2}
\]
The six horizontal contact states lie in the zero layer and have the isotropic three-dimensional two-tag trace. Equation (4.1), already normalized independently above, gives
\[
k_T(0)=\frac2{g-1}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

These constants are unequal, with an exact sign. Counting the zero-, two-, and four-step returns gives
\[
g\ge1+\frac16+\frac{90}{6^4}
  =\frac{89}{72}>\frac65.
\tag{5.4}
\]
There are 18 four-step returns using one coordinate and 72 using two coordinates. Consequently
\[
k_V(0)-k_T(0)=\frac{10g-12}{g(g-1)}>0.
\tag{5.5}
\]
No decimal approximation to the lattice Green constant is needed. Continuity yields `k_V(epsilon)>k_T(epsilon)` throughout some positive interval `(0,epsilon_0)`.

The sign/reflection symmetries and permutations of the three equal-rate horizontal coordinates make the trace chain exactly lumpable into `V,T`. Write its quotient as
\[
\overline Q_\varepsilon=
\begin{pmatrix}
-p_\varepsilon-k_V(\varepsilon)&p_\varepsilon\\
q_\varepsilon&-q_\varepsilon-k_T(\varepsilon)
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{5.6}
\]
Both off-diagonal rates are positive for every epsilon greater than zero. For example, the excursion
`e_1 -> e_1+e_2 -> e_2` has positive rates `2` and `2 epsilon`, and its intermediate state is outside contact. Its reversal also has positive rates. Symmetry of the full eight-state matrix gives `2p_epsilon=6q_epsilon`, though only positivity is needed.

For any two-state subgenerator of the form (5.6), with survival functions started in the respective states,
\[
S_V'(0)=-k_V,\quad S_T'(0)=-k_T,
\]
\[
S_V''(0)-S_V'(0)^2=p_\varepsilon(k_V-k_T),
\qquad
S_T''(0)-S_T'(0)^2=q_\varepsilon(k_T-k_V).
\tag{5.7}
\]
These identities follow by multiplying `Qbar 1=-k` once more by `Qbar`. Both quantities are nonzero in the chosen epsilon interval. Every exponential survival function has zero defect, so neither type has an exponential law. Within each type all deterministic orientations are equivalent, proving the claimed statement for all eight initial contact states.

The second defect is negative. A nonnegative mixture of exponential survival functions would have a nonnegative defect, equal to the variance of its mixing rate. Thus the horizontal contact law in this family requires a genuine phase-type description, or a signed spectral expansion, rather than a nonnegative mixture of exponentials. Uniform initialization on the eight contact states has type weights `(1/4,3/4)` and, by (4.4), a positive defect `3(k_V-k_T)^2/16`.

This completes an infinite-volume proof. It does not use finite-box numerical nonfailure or a presumed novelty of the phase-type formalism.

## 6. Exact finite example and executable certificate

Consider the same chronological model on Z^3 with coordinate rates `(1,2,2)`, but stop the relative process when it exits
\[
D=\{z\in\mathbb Z^3: \|z\|_1\le2\}.
\]
The observable is `H_D=int_0^{tau_D}1_A(R_t)dt`. This is killing of the relative displacement; it is not interchange on a finite graph with suppressed boundary clocks.

There are 25 free states, 24 nonzero relative states, and 6 contact states. The free relative total rate is 20. At a rate-one contact the actual total is 19; at a rate-two contact it is 18.

For this finite check, the free kernel is the two-variable resolvent `F_lambda(x,y)` of the walk killed at the domain boundary. The kill-at-zero subtraction is `F_lambda(x,y)-F_lambda(x,0)F_lambda(0,y)/F_lambda(0,0)`; a translation-invariant replacement `r_lambda(y-x)` is not used across this boundary.

### Hand calculation of the trace

All states of l1 norm two are outside contact, and in this domain there are no transitions between two such states. Their diagonal holding rate is 20. Eliminating these excursions gives the contact Schur complement
\[
Q=L_{AA}+L_{AU}(-L_{UU})^{-1}L_{UA}.
\tag{6.1}
\]
For arbitrary coordinate rates in this radius-two domain, put `r=4 sum_i c_i`. On the even coordinate quotient,
\[
Q^+_{ii}=-r+2c_i+
 \frac{4c_i^2+8\sum_{j\ne i}c_j^2}{r},
\qquad
Q^+_{ij}=\frac{8c_i c_j}{r}\quad(i\ne j).
\tag{6.2}
\]
For the diagonal excursion contribution, the state `2e_i` contributes `4c_i^2/r`; the two states `e_i+e_j,e_i-e_j` contribute `8c_j^2/r`. Those same two states give the displayed off-diagonal term when the excursion returns to the `j`th contact type. Internal flips cancel on even functions, leaving the term `-r+2c_i` before excursions.

For `(1,2,2)`, lump the two rate-one contact states into `V` and the four rate-two states into `T`. This gives
\[
\overline Q=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}-73&8\\4&-58\end{pmatrix},
\qquad k=\begin{pmatrix}13\\54/5\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{6.3}
\]
Consequently, Cramer's rule in `(sI-Qbar)^{-1}k` gives
\[
\boxed{\mathbb E_Ve^{-sH_D}
=\frac{325s+4202}{25s^2+655s+4202},}
\quad
\boxed{\mathbb E_Te^{-sH_D}
=\frac{270s+4202}{25s^2+655s+4202}.}
\tag{6.4}
\]
The survival defects are respectively `88/25` and `-44/25`, so neither law is exponential. The two exponential decay rates are
\[
\gamma_\pm=\frac{131\pm\sqrt{353}}{10}.
\]
In particular, the second survival function is
\[
S_T(u)=\frac{\sqrt{353}+23}{2\sqrt{353}}e^{-\gamma_-u}

## 7. Failures, rejected shortcuts, and limitations

1. The first implementation used SymPy. Both the default `python` and the advertised primary-runtime Python lacked that module, each failing immediately with `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'sympy'`. No mathematical check executed in either failed attempt. That complete attempted implementation is retained as `computations/059_contact_resolvent_sympy_attempt.py`. The successful version was rewritten with explicit rational Gaussian elimination rather than claiming the unavailable dependency had run.
2. Lack of contact transitivity is not by itself a proof of nonexponentiality. Equal trace killing rates can occur for other reasons, and a special entrance law can be quasi-stationary. The exact row-sum/spectral criteria and the two-type derivative calculation replace that invalid shortcut.
3. Starting a small-rate degeneration in Z^3 would leave only two fast directions. The resulting recurrent limit would make this particular finite-Green continuity argument fail. The proved example is deliberately Z^4, where the three fast directions remain transient. No Z^3 anisotropic nonexponential theorem is claimed from this degeneration.
4. A finite killed lattice certificate does not automatically pass its strict nonexponentiality to the infinite lattice. The infinite theorem instead uses its own dominated-convergence proof and a strict exact limiting inequality.
5. The interval endpoint `epsilon_0` is not quantified, and no classification of all anisotropic rate vectors is proved. The resolvent formulas determine any specified case exactly through lattice Green integrals, but a universal anisotropic classification would require further analysis.
6. A phase-type law need not be a nonnegative scalar exponential mixture from every starting state. The horizontal laws in Sections 5 and 6 explicitly disprove that stronger assertion.
7. All stopping-time/strong-Markov arguments here use fresh chronological clocks. None applies to arbitrary cyclic reuse of a previously revealed slab. The positive-probability cyclic trap in 004 remains a direct obstruction to such a transfer. No conditioning/restart theorem for the cyclic problem is proved.
