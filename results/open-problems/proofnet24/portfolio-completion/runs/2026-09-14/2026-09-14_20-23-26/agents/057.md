## Results and scope

For a rational four-type strictly non-Volterra QSO, write its unique simplex
fixed point as \(p=(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)\). Then

\[
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4):\mathbb Q]\le8.}
\tag{1}
\]

If \(p\) is on the boundary, the stronger sharp bound is

\[
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4):\mathbb Q]\le4.}
\tag{2}
\]

Both bounds are attained by complete rational coefficient tables below.
The degree-eight example has **every allowed coefficient strictly positive**,
so neither stochastic nonnegativity nor interiority improves (1) to four.
The report also supplies degree-one and degree-two interior examples.

The signed, normalized coefficient system has an irreducible incidence
variety and generic degree eight. Its analytic simplex branch also has
generic algebraic degree eight over the rational parameter-function field.
Nevertheless, rational coefficient arrays whose fixed point is rational
are Euclidean dense in the coefficient polytope. These statements use
different notions of degree and genericity and are compatible.

No Galois group, impossibility of formulas by radicals, classification of
all attained intermediate degrees, worldwide novelty, or proof-assistant
verification is claimed. The existing four-type uniqueness and tangent
nonsingularity theorems are explicit dependencies.

## 1. Target 1: sharp universal bounds and an exact elimination that handles degeneracies

### 1.1. Setup and the nonsingularity input

The coefficients obey

\[
P_{ij,k}=P_{ji,k}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0},\qquad
\sum_kP_{ij,k}=1,\qquad
P_{ij,k}=0\quad(k\in\{i,j\}),\qquad 1\le i,j,k\le4.
\tag{3}
\]

Let \(V_k(x)=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij,k}x_ix_j\). In normalized affine coordinates
\(y=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\), put \(x_4=1-x_1-x_2-x_3\) and define

\[
f_i(y)=x_i-V_i(x),\quad 1\le i\le3,
\qquad I=(f_1,f_2,f_3)\subset\mathbb Q[y_1,y_2,y_3].
\tag{4}
\]

Each \(f_i\) has degree at most two. The fourth fixed-point equation follows
from the first three, because \(\sum_iV_i(x)=(\sum_i x_i)^2=1\).
Write

\[
D(y)=\det\left(\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial y_j}\right)_{i,j=1}^3.
\tag{5}
\]

Theorem 1 and Corollary 5 of `FOUR_TYPE_UNIQUENESS.md`, equivalently the
full-tangent inverse in `agents/044.md`, establish a unique simplex fixed
point \(p\) and

\[
D(p_1,p_2,p_3)\ne0.
\tag{6}
\]

This determinant is the matrix of \(I-DV(p)\) on
\(E=\{z:\sum_i z_i=0\}\), in the chart basis
\(e_1-e_4,e_2-e_4,e_3-e_4\). In particular, (6) applies at coefficient
and state boundaries. It does not rely on pretending that boundary points
have all two-sided feasible simplex directions.

### 1.2. Proof of the field-degree bound

The isolated-point Bézout inequality bounds the number of nonsingular common
zeros of three quadrics over \(\mathbb C\) by eight. It applies even if
there are other positive-dimensional components or points at infinity.
For precisely the nonsingular version needed here, one can also see this
by choosing any finite collection of nonsingular zeros, perturbing the
three equations generically and sufficiently little so each chosen zero
persists by the complex implicit function theorem, and applying the usual
degree-product bound to the perturbed quadrics. More than eight chosen
zeros would contradict that bound.

For a primary reference that explicitly counts isolated points, including
fibers with other components, see Pinaki Mondal, *General Bezout-type
theorems*, Theorem 3.1 and Example 3.4, with all variable weights one:
[author's arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.0614). The polynomial map
\(f:\mathbb A^3\to\mathbb A^3\) here is generically finite because its
Jacobian is nonzero at (6), meeting the hypothesis of that theorem.

Consider the saturation

\[
I^{\rm ns}=I:D^\infty
=\bigl(f_1,f_2,f_3,uD-1\bigr)\cap\mathbb Q[y_1,y_2,y_3].
\tag{7}
\]

Its complex points are exactly the finitely many common zeros with
\(D\ne0\): every such point is isolated, there are at most eight of them,
and a finite set is already closed. At every retained point the Jacobian
criterion gives a reduced local algebra of dimension one over
\(\mathbb C\). Thus

\[
B=\mathbb Q[y_1,y_2,y_3]/I^{\rm ns}
\tag{8}
\]

is a nonzero finite reduced \(\mathbb Q\)-algebra, with

\[
N:=\dim_{\mathbb Q}B\le8.
\tag{9}
\]

Nonzeroness follows from (6). Equivalently, the algebra obtained by adding
\(uD-1\) is finite and reduced, and eliminating \(u\) gives (8).
Evaluation at the simplex point maps \(B\) to a finite-dimensional
\(\mathbb Q\)-subalgebra of \(\mathbb R\). A finite-dimensional domain
over a field is a field, so its image is precisely
\(\mathbb Q(p_1,p_2,p_3)\), and its dimension is at most \(N\).
Since \(p_4=1-p_1-p_2-p_3\), this proves (1).

In particular, this proves algebraicity first and bounds the degree of the
**whole coordinate field**, not merely each coordinate separately. It
does not multiply three separate degree-eight coordinate bounds together.
Every embedding of this field into \(\mathbb C\) produces a distinct
nonsingular normalized fixed point. The conjugates need not be real or
nonnegative; the uniqueness theorem cannot identify them with \(p\).

### 1.3. Boundary reduction and all support cases

The coordinate cap \(p_i\le(3-\sqrt5)/2<1/2\) rules out supports of
size one or two. A boundary fixed point therefore has exactly three
positive coordinates. Relabel so that \(p_4=0\). Then

\[
0=V_4(p)=\sum_{i,j\le3}P_{ij,4}p_ip_j
\]

forces \(P_{ij,4}=0\) for every \(i,j\le3\), by nonnegativity and the
strict positivity of those parental products. Parent exclusion already
gives \(P_{i4,4}=P_{44,4}=0\). Hence

\[
V_4\equiv0.
\tag{10}
\]

Conversely, (10) forces every fixed point to have fourth coordinate zero.
The remaining three-type system has two free normalized variables and two
quadratic equations. Their Jacobian at \(p\) is nonsingular: in the
chart \((x_1,x_2,x_4)\), with \(x_3=1-x_1-x_2-x_4\), the last
equation is exactly \(x_4=0\), so the full three-dimensional determinant
is the two-dimensional active determinant. The preceding argument with
two quadrics now gives (2). This also handles all possible zero allowed
coefficients and degree drops. The arbitrary parental rows involving type
4 have no effect on the active fixed-point equations.

### 1.4. A finite, exact selection procedure

Equations (7)–(8) give an exact algorithm for every rational admissible
array, including arrays for which an unsaturated resultant might vanish
identically or contain extra factors. The following is a constructive
description, not a claimed bit-complexity bound or an implementation of
all possible saturated Gröbner computations in the companion script.

1. Form the three quadrics (4), their determinant (5), and the ideal
   \((f_1,f_2,f_3,uD-1)\). Exact Gröbner elimination computes (7), a
   basis of \(B\), and its coordinate multiplication matrices. Its
   dimension \(N\) is in \(\{1,\ldots,8\}\).
2. Try the integer linear forms
   \[
   \ell_m=y_1+m y_2+m^2 y_3,
   \qquad 0\le m\le N(N-1).
   \tag{11}
   \]
   At least one separates all \(N\) geometric points. For any pair of
   distinct points, equality of their \(\ell_m\)-values is a nonzero
   polynomial in \(m\) of degree at most two, excluding at most two
   integers. There are at most \(N(N-1)/2\) pairs. Thus at most 56 bad
   integers exist, and **57 trials suffice** uniformly. Test a trial by
   checking whether the characteristic polynomial
   \(\chi_m(t)=\det(tI-M_{\ell_m})\) is squarefree.
3. For a separating form, \(1,\ell_m,\ldots,\ell_m^{N-1}\) is a basis
   of \(B\), by the Vandermonde matrix over the distinct values. Exact
   linear algebra therefore gives
   \[
   \chi_m(t)=0,\qquad y_i=g_i(t),\qquad
   \deg\chi_m=N\le8,\quad \deg g_i<N.
   \tag{12}
   \]
4. Isolate the real roots of the squarefree rational polynomial
   \(\chi_m\), for example by a Sturm sequence. Decide the exact signs
   of \(g_1,g_2,g_3,1-g_1-g_2-g_3\) at each root. Exactly one root
   gives four nonnegative coordinates, by existence, (6), and uniqueness.
   The irreducible factor containing that root is its primitive-element
   minimal polynomial. Its degree is the coordinate-field degree in (1).

Zero coordinate values are handled by exact algebraic sign determination,
not by floating tolerances. No singular point discarded by saturation can
be the simplex point, by (6). If the missing-output face is known, one
may instead use the active two-variable ideal and \(\ell_m=y_1+m y_2\);
\(N\le4\), and \(m=0,\ldots,6\) suffices. The procedure never treats
uniqueness in the simplex as uniqueness among all complex roots.

For a favorable nonsingular eight-point system, the companion computation
uses a simpler degree-four Macaulay reduction in place of general
saturation. Its exact certificate and why that reduction suffices in the
displayed example are given in Section 3.1.

## 2. Target 2: generic signed degree, the simplex branch, and dense rational specializations

### 2.1. Irreducibility of the full incidence variety

Let \(\mathscr A\cong\mathbb A^{14}_{\mathbb Q}\) retain parent
symmetry, exclusion and normalization, but allow signed or complex
coefficients. The dimension is
\(4(3-1)+6(2-1)=14\). Let

\[
X=\{x\in\mathbb A^4:\sum_i x_i=1\},\qquad
\mathcal I=\{(P,x)\in\mathscr A\times X:V_P(x)=x\}.
\tag{13}
\]

There is no solution at a vertex \(e_i\), since its \(i\)-th output is
zero. At every other normalized state, at least two coordinates, say
\(x_i,x_j\), are nonzero. Directions in the diagonal parental row \(ii\)
give every forcing vector in

\[
x_i^2 U_i,\qquad
U_i=\{v:\sum_kv_k=0,\ v_i=0\}.
\tag{14}
\]

These are two-dimensional subspaces of \(E\). Since
\(U_i+U_j=E\) for \(i\ne j\), the derivative of
\(P\mapsto V_P(x)\) has rank three. This argument works for arbitrary
complex state coordinates: a nonzero coordinate has nonzero square.

For each \(x\in X\setminus\{e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4\}\), its fixed-point
coefficient fiber is consequently a nonempty affine space of dimension
11. Nonemptiness follows because the rank-three affine map takes values
in the normalized affine hyperplane and is onto it. On the open sets
where a chosen rank-three coefficient minor is nonzero, three coefficient
variables can be solved rationally in terms of the other eleven.
Accordingly, projection to \(x\) makes \(\mathcal I\) a locally trivial
affine-space bundle of relative dimension 11 over an irreducible
three-dimensional base. It follows that

\[
\boxed{\mathcal I\text{ is geometrically irreducible and has dimension }14.}
\tag{15}
\]

This proof covers all signed normalized states: no extra incidence
components were discarded at states with one or more zero coordinates.
Only the four impossible vertices were removed.

### 2.2. The generic degree is exactly eight

The degree-eight example in Section 3.1 has eight distinct nonsingular
complex affine solutions. The implicit function theorem continues all
eight in a complex neighborhood of its coefficient point. Thus the
projection \(\mathcal I\to\mathscr A\) is dominant and generically
finite; its generic degree is at least eight. More explicitly, a
nonempty algebraic open set on which a generically finite map has its
generic number of reduced points intersects that neighborhood. Bézout
bounds the generic degree by eight. Therefore

\[
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\mathcal I):\mathbb Q(\mathscr A)]=8.}
\tag{16}
\]

In the same example, all eight values of \(x_3\) are distinct, because
they are the roots of its separable irreducible octic. The eight distinct
values persist near that coefficient point. Any generic polynomial
relation in \(x_3\) of degree less than eight would, after avoiding its
coefficient poles and leading-coefficient zeros in that neighborhood,
vanish at eight distinct values. This is impossible. Consequently
\(x_3\) is a generic primitive element and has minimal degree eight.
Permuting the four type labels proves the analogous generic degree for
each coordinate.

The real analytic simplex branch at a strictly positive coefficient point
is an open real graph in the smooth incidence variety. A polynomial in
parameters and a state coordinate vanishing on this graph vanishes on
its local complex analytic extension, and hence on the irreducible
incidence variety. Thus the simplex branch cannot satisfy a nonzero
generic relation of smaller degree in that coordinate. Its **generic
algebraic-function degree** is also eight.

This does not assert that there are eight simplex points, eight real
points, or degree eight at every rational input. Nor does it assert a
single degree-eight polynomial in one fixed coordinate is a safe
eliminant at every degenerate specialization. The saturation and
separating-form algorithm in Section 1.4 addresses individual inputs.

### 2.3. Rational fixed-point specializations are nevertheless dense

**Proposition.** Rational admissible arrays with rational simplex fixed
points are Euclidean dense in the full closed coefficient polytope.

**Proof.** First fix an array \(P\) with every allowed coefficient
strictly positive. Its simplex point \(p\) has all coordinates positive.
Use the following three normalized coefficient directions:

\[
H_1:11\text{ transfers between outputs }2,3;\quad
H_2:11\text{ transfers between outputs }4,3;\quad
H_3:22\text{ transfers between outputs }1,3.
\]

Their state forcing vectors are respectively
\(x_1^2(e_2-e_3),x_1^2(e_4-e_3),x_2^2(e_1-e_3)\).
They form a basis of \(E\) whenever \(x_1x_2\ne0\). Extend these
directions to a rational affine coordinate chart of \(\mathscr A\).
For any normalized rational \(x\) sufficiently close to \(p\), and any
rational choices of the other eleven coefficient coordinates sufficiently
close to their values at \(P\), solve the three fixed-point equations
linearly for these three selected coefficient coordinates. The solution
is rational, depends continuously on the inputs, and is as close to
\(P\) as desired. Hence every allowed coefficient remains positive.
The prescribed rational \(x\) is its unique simplex fixed point.

Strictly positive arrays are dense in the closed polytope, by mixing any
array with the fully positive symmetric array. Applying the preceding
construction near these mixtures proves the proposition. \(\square\)

Thus function-field generic degree eight must not be read as a claim that
the rational degree-one specializations are absent from some ordinary
open region. No Hilbert irreducibility theorem or density statement for
degree-eight rational specializations is used or claimed here.

### 3.3. A positive quadratic family, and a rational specialization with other signed roots

For \(0\le a\le1\), prescribe probability \(a\) of offspring 4 on
every parental row wholly inside \(\{1,2,3\}\). Distribute the remaining
probability \(1-a\) uniformly among the allowed active outputs. For a row
\(i4\), distribute uniformly among the two active outputs other than
\(i\); on row 44, distribute uniformly among all three active outputs.
This is a complete admissible array, with all allowed coefficients positive
when \(0<a<1\). It is invariant under all permutations of types 1,2,3.

Its unique simplex fixed point is

\[
p=\left(\frac{1-t}{3},\frac{1-t}{3},\frac{1-t}{3},t\right),
\qquad t=a(1-t)^2,
\tag{27}
\]

where the nonnegative solution is \(t=0\) at \(a=0\), and otherwise

\[
t=\frac{2a+1-\sqrt{1+4a}}{2a}
 =\frac{2a}{2a+1+\sqrt{1+4a}}.
\tag{28}
\]

To verify existence directly, permutation symmetry makes the three active
outputs equal at the state in (27), and their sum is \(1-V_4(p)=1-t\).
Thus the fourth equation implies every equation. At rational \(a=1/2\),

\[
\boxed{p=\left(\frac{\sqrt3-1}{3},\frac{\sqrt3-1}{3},
                   \frac{\sqrt3-1}{3},2-\sqrt3\right)}
\tag{29}
\]

has degree two, with all allowed coefficients positive. The exact script
checks every residual modulo \(t^2-4t+1\), irreducible modulo 5.
The endpoints and rational cases cause no division problem: (27) handles
\(a=0\), and a rational \(t\in[0,(3-\sqrt5)/2]\) gives the rational
parameter \(a=t/(1-t)^2\) and a rational fixed point. For example,
\(a=4/9\) yields \(t=1/4\).

For an especially transparent degree-one example with a different signed
root count, instead take the fully symmetric four-type array

\[
P_{ii,k}=1/3\ (k\ne i),\qquad
P_{ij,k}=1/2\ (i\ne j,\ k\notin\{i,j\}).
\tag{30}
\]

Every allowed coefficient is positive and the simplex point is
\((1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)\). In fact, **all complex normalized affine fixed
points** of (30) are exactly that point and the four permutations of
\((4,-1,-1,-1)\).

For completeness, on \(\sum_i x_i=1\), put \(Q=\sum_i x_i^2\).
The fixed-point equations become

\[
4x_i^2-12x_i+3-Q=0\quad(1\le i\le4).
\tag{31}
\]

All four coordinates are roots of one quadratic. If they are all equal,
normalization forces the uniform point. Otherwise they take two distinct
values \(a,b\) with \(a+b=3\). Multiplicity two of each is impossible,
since their sum would be six. Multiplicity one and three forces the single
value to be four and the other value to be minus one. These exhaust the
complex possibilities, including the repeated-quadratic-root case, and
all five points directly satisfy (31).

For the homogeneous projective equations
\(V_i(x)-x_i s=0\), \(s=\sum_i x_i\), a point at infinity has \(s=0\).
Then \(V_i=(4x_i^2-Q)/6\). A nonzero solution has \(Q\ne0\), and after
rescaling its entries are signs \(\pm1\), with exactly two plus signs.
Up to common sign there are precisely three projective points at infinity:

\[
[1:1:-1:-1],\quad[1:-1:1:-1],\quad[1:-1:-1:1].
\tag{32}
\]

Thus uniqueness in the simplex is compatible with four additional real
signed affine roots, while a symmetric specialization can move three of
the eight projective solutions to infinity. A raw generic affine count
must not be imposed on this specialization.

* An attempted triangular construction prescribed
  \(V_4=a(1-x_4)^2\) and \(V_3=b(1-x_3)^2\). The parental row 12
  then forces \(a+b=1\). Its exhausted diagonal probabilities remove
  the square term needed for a third independent quadratic: after the
  two equations, the remaining active split is linear. This construction
  therefore does not establish degree eight. It is not a universal proof
  that every triangular construction has degree at most four.
* Generic Bézout eight by itself would only bound isolated roots; it
  would not justify blindly applying an unsaturated eliminant at every
  parameter point. Tangent nonsingularity and the saturation in (7)
  address that gap. No example of an admissible array with an additional
  positive-dimensional affine signed component was constructed or is
  asserted; the universal argument does not require ruling one out.
