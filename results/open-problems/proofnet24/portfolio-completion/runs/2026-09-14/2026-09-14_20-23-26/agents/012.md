## Result

**The proposed continuous-time extension is valid**, with the parameter ranges
made explicit below. In addition, integration of the scalar coordinate envelope
gives uniform exponential estimates from time zero, including the endpoint rate
\(\sqrt5-2\). Finite entry into the endpoint cap \(K_c\) is false in general.
An explicit admissible operator below attains the scalar entrance bound and
demonstrates this endpoint limitation.

Let
\[
\Delta_3=\{x\in[0,\infty)^4:\ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^4x_i=1\},
\qquad
V_k(x)=\sum_{i,j=1}^4 P_{ij,k}x_ix_j,
\]
where
\[
P_{ij,k}=P_{ji,k}\ge0,\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^4 P_{ij,k}=1,\qquad
P_{ij,k}=0\quad\text{whenever }k\in\{i,j\}.
\tag{1}
\]
Zeros among the allowed coefficients are permitted. No interior, irreducibility,
genericity, nondegeneracy, or invariant-face assumption is imposed.

Set
\[
c=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
d=\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
\delta=d-c=\sqrt5,\qquad
r=1-2c=\sqrt5-2>0,
\tag{2}
\]
and, for real \(q\), define
\[
K_q=\{x\in\Delta_3:\ x_i\le q\text{ for every }i\}.
\]
Denote by \(\Phi_t(x^0)\) the solution of
\[
\dot x=V(x)-x,\qquad x(0)=x^0\in\Delta_3.
\tag{3}
\]

**Theorem.** For every coefficient tensor satisfying (1):

1. The flow (3) exists uniquely for every \(t\ge0\) and preserves \(\Delta_3\).
   Each \(K_q\) with \(q\ge c\) is forward invariant.
2. For \(c\le q<1/2\) and \(x^0,y^0\in K_q\),
   \[
   \|\Phi_t(x^0)-\Phi_t(y^0)\|_1
   \le e^{-(1-2q)t}\|x^0-y^0\|_1.
   \tag{4}
   \]
   The same inequality at \(q=1/2\) gives nonexpansion. The phrase
   “contraction rate \(1-2q\)” means a positive rate only for \(q<1/2\).
3. There is exactly one equilibrium \(x^*\), it is the unique fixed point of
   \(V\), and \(x^*\in K_c\).
4. Every trajectory enters \(K_q\) in finite time for every \(q>c\).
   For \(c<q<1\), a universal entrance time is
   \[
   T_q=\frac1{\sqrt5}
       \log\!\left(\frac{c(d-q)}{q-c}\right).
   \tag{5}
   \]
   This universal bound is attained by an admissible operator and initial
   condition. For \(q\ge1\), the entrance time is zero.
5. For all \(x^0,y^0\in\Delta_3\) and \(t\ge0\),
   \[
   \|\Phi_t(x^0)-\Phi_t(y^0)\|_1
   \le
   \left(\frac{1+c}{1+ce^{-\sqrt5t}}\right)^2
   e^{-rt}\|x^0-y^0\|_1
   \le 5c\,e^{-rt}\|x^0-y^0\|_1.
   \tag{6}
   \]
   The equilibrium estimate is stronger:
   \[
   \|\Phi_t(x^0)-x^*\|_1
   \le
   \frac{1+c}{1+ce^{-\sqrt5t}}
   e^{-rt}\|x^0-x^*\|_1
   \le (1+c)e^{-rt}\|x^0-x^*\|_1.
   \tag{7}
   \]
   Thus the equilibrium is globally exponentially stable in \(\ell^1\).

The prefactors and decay rate in (6)–(7) are proved sufficient constants;
their optimality is not claimed. The coordinate cap and universal entrance time
have the exact sharpness stated in item 4 and Section 7 below.

## 1. Well-posedness, boundary preservation, and the coordinate inequality

The vector field \(F=V-I\) is polynomial and hence locally Lipschitz. For
completeness, local preservation of nonnegativity follows from the integral form
\[
x(t)=e^{-t}x^0+\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)}V(x(s))\,ds.
\tag{8}
\]
On a bounded neighborhood of the constant path \(x^0\), the right-hand side is
a contraction on continuous paths for a sufficiently short interval. It also
maps every nonnegative path to a nonnegative path, because all coefficients of
\(V\) are nonnegative. Picard iteration starting from the constant path therefore
gives a nonnegative local solution, including when \(x^0\) is on the boundary.

For any solution, coefficient stochasticity gives
\[
\frac d{dt}\sum_i x_i
=\left(\sum_i x_i\right)^2-\sum_i x_i.
\]
The scalar initial value is 1; uniqueness of this scalar ODE forces
\(\sum_i x_i(t)=1\). Consequently the solution stays in \(\Delta_3\). Restarting
the local construction and using compactness of \(\Delta_3\) gives unique global
forward existence. In particular, all coordinates remain between 0 and 1.

The strict non-Volterra rule eliminates every summand of \(V_k\) having either
parent equal to \(k\). Since \(0\le P_{ij,k}\le1\),
\[
V_k(x)\le\sum_{i,j\ne k}x_ix_j=(1-x_k)^2.
\]
Thus along every trajectory,
\[
\dot x_k\le g(x_k),\qquad
g(s)=(1-s)^2-s=s^2-3s+1=(s-c)(s-d).
\tag{9}
\]

Scalar comparison is valid although (9) is an inequality: if \(u'=g(u)\) and
\(x_k(0)\le u(0)\), the positive part of \(x_k-u\) satisfies a Gronwall inequality
using the Lipschitz constant of \(g\) on the relevant bounded interval. Its
initial value is zero, so \(x_k(t)\le u(t)\). This comparison also applies to a
constant supersolution.

For \(q\in[c,1]\), \(g(q)\le0\), so the constant \(q\) is a supersolution. It
follows that \(x_k(0)\le q\) implies \(x_k(t)\le q\) for every \(t\ge0\). Hence
\(K_q\) is invariant. For \(q\ge1\) it is the whole simplex and invariance is
already established. In particular the equality case \(q=c\) is covered; no
strict inward inequality at that boundary is required.

An equilibrium satisfies \(x_k=V_k(x)\le(1-x_k)^2\). Since \(d>1\), this forces
\(x_k\le c\). Thus every equilibrium belongs to \(K_c\).

## 2. The transfer inequality for arbitrary cuts

For \(m\in\Delta_3\), define
\[
A(m)_{aj}=\sum_{b=1}^4 P_{bj,a}m_b.
\tag{10}
\]
This matrix is nonnegative, its columns sum to 1, and \(A(m)_{jj}=0\), by (1).

Let \(G\) be a nonempty proper subset of \(\{1,2,3,4\}\), let \(i\in G\), and
let \(j\notin G\). Write
\[
b_{Gij}(m)=\mathbf 1_G^{\mathsf T}A(m)(e_i-e_j).
\]
Then
\[
b_{Gij}(m)\le
\begin{cases}
0,& |G|=1\text{ or }3,\\
\max(m_i,m_j),& |G|=2.
\end{cases}
\tag{11}
\]

For \(|G|=1\), necessarily \(G=\{i\}\), so
\[
b_{Gij}=A_{ii}-A_{ij}=-A_{ij}\le0.
\]
For \(|G|=3\), the complement is \(\{j\}\), so column stochasticity and the zero
diagonal give
\[
b_{Gij}=(1-A_{ji})-(1-A_{jj})=-A_{ji}\le0.
\]

For \(|G|=2\), write \(G=\{i,k\}\) and \(G^c=\{j,\ell\}\), and put
\[
p_{ab}=\sum_{r\in G}P_{ab,r}\in[0,1].
\]
The strict rule gives \(p_{ki}=0\) and \(p_{\ell j}=1\). Parent symmetry gives
\(p_{ij}=p_{ji}\). Consequently
\[
\begin{aligned}
b_{Gij}(m)
&=\sum_a m_a(p_{ai}-p_{aj})\\
&=m_i(p_{ii}-p_{ij})+m_j(p_{ij}-p_{jj})
  -m_kp_{kj}+m_\ell(p_{\ell i}-1)\\
&\le m_i(1-p_{ij})+m_jp_{ij}\\
&\le\max(m_i,m_j).
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]
The omitted terms have nonpositive signs. Equivalently, the difference between
the first upper bound and the exact quantity is the manifestly nonnegative
expression
\[
m_i(1-p_{ii})+m_jp_{jj}+m_kp_{kj}
 +m_\ell(1-p_{\ell i}).
\]
This proof covers zero coordinates of \(m\) and zero coefficients.

## 3. The \(\ell^1\) differential inequality, including every zero-coordinate case

Take any two solutions and write
\[
x=x(t),\quad y=y(t),\quad z=x-y,\quad m=(x+y)/2.
\]
Parent symmetry gives the exact polarization identity
\[
V(x)-V(y)=2A(m)z,
\qquad
\dot z=(2A(m)-I)z.
\tag{13}
\]
Indeed \(x_ax_b-y_ay_b=m_az_b+z_am_b\), and the two coefficient sums are equal
by \(P_{ab,k}=P_{ba,k}\). Also \(\sum_i z_i=0\).

Use the finite maximum representation
\[
h(t)=\tfrac12\|z(t)\|_1
    =\sum_{i:z_i(t)>0}z_i(t)
    =\max_{G\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}}\sum_{i\in G}z_i(t).
\tag{14}
\]
For a finite maximum of differentiable functions, its right derivative is the
maximum of the derivatives of the functions active at that time. This follows
directly from first-order expansions: inactive functions have a strictly
positive gap and cannot attain the maximum for sufficiently small positive
increments. Therefore
\[
D^+h(t)
=\max_{G\text{ active at }t}\mathbf1_G^{\mathsf T}\dot z(t).
\tag{15}
\]
Here \(D^+\) may be read as the upper right Dini derivative; in this finite-maximum
setting the right derivative itself exists. An active \(G\) contains all strictly
positive coordinates of \(z\), contains no strictly negative coordinate, and may
contain any selection of its zero coordinates.

If \(z=0\), then \(\dot z=0\) by (13), and both sides of the desired differential
inequality below are zero. Alternatively, uniqueness makes the two trajectories
identical thereafter.

Suppose \(z\ne0\). Its positive set \(P\) and negative set \(N\) are both nonempty,
and \(h>0\). For each \(i\in P,j\in N\), set
\[
\theta_{ij}=\frac{z_i(-z_j)}{h^2}.
\]
Then
\[
\theta_{ij}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{i\in P,j\in N}\theta_{ij}=1,
\qquad
z=h\sum_{i\in P,j\in N}\theta_{ij}(e_i-e_j).
\tag{16}
\]
For every active \(G\), \(P\subseteq G\) and \(N\subseteq G^c\), so every pair in
(16) is eligible for (11). Such a \(G\) is nonempty and proper; its size can only
be 1, 2, or 3. Put \(\mu=\max_k m_k\). Since \(\mu\ge0\), (11) implies
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf1_G^{\mathsf T}\dot z
&=2h\sum_{i\in P,j\in N}\theta_{ij}b_{Gij}(m)-h\\
&\le(2\mu-1)h.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking the maximum in (15) and multiplying by 2 proves the stronger general
estimate
\[
\boxed{\quad
D^+\|x(t)-y(t)\|_1
\le\left(\max_k\{x_k(t)+y_k(t)\}-1\right)
       \|x(t)-y(t)\|_1.
\quad}
\tag{17}
\]

This is a complete treatment of \(\ell^1\) nondifferentiability. In particular,
when zero coordinates of \(z\) appear, it would be insufficient to differentiate
only the sum over its strictly positive coordinates while holding that set fixed.
Formula (15) instead checks every active assignment of zeros to a cut. The
one-versus-three, three-versus-one, two-versus-two, and all patterns containing
zeros are all covered by (11)–(16).

The norm in (17) is locally Lipschitz in time. Thus its ordinary derivative exists
almost everywhere, obeys (17), and can be integrated using an integrating factor.
This justifies all subsequent Gronwall estimates without assuming everywhere
differentiability or dividing by a possibly zero norm.

If both solutions start in \(K_q\), invariance gives
\(\max_k(x_k+y_k)\le2q\). Integrating (17) yields (4). In particular, the flow is
strictly contractive on \(K_c\) with rate \(r=\sqrt5-2\).

## 4. Existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium without using another report's theorem

The set \(K_c\) is nonempty because it contains
\((1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)\). It is closed in finite-dimensional space and therefore
complete in \(\ell^1\).

Fix any \(\tau>0\). By invariance and (4),
\(\Phi_\tau:K_c\to K_c\) is a contraction with constant \(e^{-r\tau}<1\).
The Banach fixed-point theorem provides a unique \(p\in K_c\) such that
\(\Phi_\tau(p)=p\).

For every \(s\ge0\), the semigroup identity gives
\[
\Phi_\tau(\Phi_s(p))=\Phi_s(\Phi_\tau(p))=\Phi_s(p).
\]
Invariance places \(\Phi_s(p)\) in \(K_c\), so uniqueness of the fixed point of
\(\Phi_\tau\) forces \(\Phi_s(p)=p\). Differentiation at \(s=0\) gives
\(V(p)-p=0\). Hence \(p\) is an equilibrium, rather than merely a periodic point
of a time map.

Section 1 proved that every equilibrium lies in \(K_c\). Such an equilibrium is
a fixed point of \(\Phi_\tau\), so it must equal \(p\). Put \(x^*=p\).

This establishes the equilibrium assertion independently of the fixed-point
uniqueness proof in `agents/006.md`. The only fixed-point theorem used here is
Banach's theorem for an explicitly proved contraction.

## 5. Explicit scalar envelope and finite entrance

For an initial point \(x^0\), put
\[
M_x=\max\{c,\|x^0\|_\infty\}\in[c,1],
\qquad
a_x=\frac{M_x-c}{d-M_x}\in[0,c].
\tag{18}
\]
The last interval follows because this fraction is increasing in \(M_x\), and
\((1-c)/(d-1)=c\). Solving \(u'=g(u)\), \(u(0)=M_x\), gives
\[
u_x(t)=c+\frac{\delta a_xe^{-\delta t}}{1+a_xe^{-\delta t}}.
\tag{19}
\]
For \(M_x=c\), this is the constant solution \(c\). Otherwise it decreases strictly
from \(M_x\) to \(c\) and stays strictly above \(c\) at finite times. Formula (19)
can be verified either by differentiation or by separation:
\[
\frac{u_x(t)-c}{d-u_x(t)}
 =\frac{M_x-c}{d-M_x}e^{-\delta t}.
\]
Coordinate comparison from (9) yields
\[
x_i(t)\le u_x(t)\quad\text{for all }i,t\ge0.
\tag{20}
\]

Let \(q>c\). If \(\|x^0\|_\infty\le q\), the point is already in the invariant cap.
Otherwise put \(M=\|x^0\|_\infty>q\), so necessarily \(c<q<M\le1\). The bound
\(u_M(t)\le q\) is equivalent to
\[
\frac{M-c}{d-M}e^{-\delta t}
\le\frac{q-c}{d-q}.
\]
Thus an explicit initial-condition-dependent entrance time is
\[
T_q(x^0)=
\begin{cases}
0,&\|x^0\|_\infty\le q,\\[2mm]
\displaystyle\frac1\delta
\log\!\left(\frac{(M-c)(d-q)}{(d-M)(q-c)}\right),
&M=\|x^0\|_\infty>q.
\end{cases}
\tag{21}
\]
At this time and thereafter, the trajectory is in \(K_q\). Taking \(M=1\)
gives (5), since \((1-c)/(d-1)=c\). The logarithm in (5) is positive for
\(c<q<1\), and tends to \(+\infty\) as \(q\downarrow c\).

As a simple rational cap, \(q=2/5\) gives strict contraction rate \(1/5\) after
the universal entrance time
\[
T_{2/5}=\frac1{\sqrt5}
\log\!\left(\frac{47+21\sqrt5}{2}\right).
\tag{22}
\]
No numerical approximation is needed for this exact guarantee.

## 6. Uniform global exponential estimates with explicit prefactors

There is no need to lose the endpoint decay rate by first entering a cap with
\(q>c\). Keep the time-dependent envelopes instead.

For two initial conditions, define \(a_x,a_y\) by (18), and put
\(E(t)=e^{-\delta t}\). Equations (17) and (20) give
\[
D^+\|x(t)-y(t)\|_1
\le (u_x(t)+u_y(t)-1)\|x(t)-y(t)\|_1.
\tag{23}
\]
The scalar excess integrates exactly:
\[
\int_0^t (u_x(s)-c)\,ds
=\log\!\left(\frac{1+a_x}{1+a_xE(t)}\right),
\tag{24}
\]
because the derivative of \(\log(1+a_xe^{-\delta s})\) is \(-(u_x(s)-c)\).
The same identity holds for \(y\). Integration of (23) therefore yields the
more precise initial-condition-dependent bound
\[
\boxed{\quad
\|\Phi_t(x^0)-\Phi_t(y^0)\|_1
\le e^{-rt}
\frac{1+a_x}{1+a_xE(t)}
\frac{1+a_y}{1+a_yE(t)}
\|x^0-y^0\|_1.
\quad}
\tag{25}
\]
For \(0\le E\le1\), the function \(a\mapsto(1+a)/(1+aE)\) is increasing on
\([0,c]\). Replacing both \(a\)'s by \(c\) gives the first inequality in (6).
The prefactors are bounded by \((1+c)^2\), and
\[
(1+c)^2=1+2c+c^2=5c,
\]
using \(c^2=3c-1\). This proves the second inequality in (6).

For the equilibrium trajectory, \(\|x^*\|_\infty\le c\), so \(a_y=0\) in (18).
Thus (25) becomes
\[
\|\Phi_t(x^0)-x^*\|_1
\le e^{-rt}\frac{1+a_x}{1+a_xe^{-\delta t}}
       \|x^0-x^*\|_1.
\tag{26}
\]
Replacing \(a_x\) by \(c\) proves (7). In particular the smaller universal
equilibrium prefactor is
\[
1+c=\frac{5-\sqrt5}{2},
\]
whereas the universal two-trajectory prefactor is
\[
5c=\frac{15-5\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

These estimates prove global convergence and Lyapunov stability at once. They
also rule out nonconstant periodic or other recurrent trajectories of the
continuous flow. They make no assertion that the flow is contractive with
prefactor 1 on the entire simplex.

## 7. An exact example: sharp entrance bound, failure of finite entry into \(K_c\), and a discrete two-cycle

Let \(H=\{2,3,4\}\). Specify the coefficients as follows:

- If both parents belong to \(H\), offspring type 1 has probability 1.
  This includes equal parents in \(H\).
- For the parent pair \((1,1)\), each offspring type in \(H\) has probability
  \(1/3\).
- For a parent pair \((1,j)\) or \((j,1)\) with \(j\in H\), each of the two
  offspring types in \(H\setminus\{j\}\) has probability \(1/2\).

All unspecified coefficients are zero. This describes every parent pair,
is symmetric in the parents, is stochastic, and never produces either parent.
It is therefore an admissible operator under (1).

The line
\[
L=\left\{\left(u,\frac{1-u}{3},\frac{1-u}{3},\frac{1-u}{3}\right):
               0\le u\le1\right\}
\]
is preserved both by \(V\) and by the flow. On this line,
\[
V_1=(1-u)^2,\qquad
V_j=\frac{2u-u^2}{3}\quad(j\in H).
\tag{27}
\]
Consequently the continuous equation on \(L\) is exactly
\[
\dot u=(1-u)^2-u=g(u),\qquad
\dot x_j=-\dot u/3.
\tag{28}
\]

Starting from \(e_1\) gives exactly the universal envelope
\[
u(t)=c+\frac{\sqrt5\,c\,e^{-\sqrt5t}}
                       {1+ce^{-\sqrt5t}}.
\tag{29}
\]
At each finite time, \(u(t)>c\). Thus this trajectory never enters \(K_c\)
in finite time, although it converges to
\[
\left(c,\frac{1-c}{3},\frac{1-c}{3},\frac{1-c}{3}\right).
\]
For \(c<q<1\), the other three coordinates are always less than \(c<q\), and
the first coordinate first reaches \(q\) precisely at time (5). Therefore (5)
is the best possible universal entrance bound from all initial points and all
admissible operators. More generally, starting on \(L\) at any \(M\in(q,1]\)
attains the initial-maximum bound (21).

The same example proves that \(c\) is the smallest universally invariant
nonempty coordinate cap: for \(1/4\le q<c\), start on \(L\) with \(u=q\).
The other coordinates satisfy \((1-q)/3\le q\), so this point is in \(K_q\),
but \(\dot u=g(q)>0\), and it immediately exits \(K_q\). Caps with \(q<1/4\)
are empty and their formal invariance has no content.

Finally, the original discrete iteration of this same operator has the exact
two-cycle
\[
e_1\ \longmapsto\ (0,1/3,1/3,1/3)\ \longmapsto\ e_1.
\tag{30}
\]
Thus the continuous global-convergence theorem demonstrably does not imply
convergence of \(V^n(x)\).
