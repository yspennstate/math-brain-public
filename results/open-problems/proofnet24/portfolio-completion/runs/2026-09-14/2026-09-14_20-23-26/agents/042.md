## Result and scope

Let $u,v\in\mathbb C^n$ be unit vectors, let

\[
 P=uu^*,\qquad Q=vv^*,\qquad K(t)=(1-t)P+tQ,\qquad
 F(t)=H(P_{K(t)}),\quad 0\le t\le1,
\]

where Shannon entropy uses natural logarithms. The following classification is
**PROVED**, including arbitrary complex phases and zero coordinates.

1. If $P=Q$, equivalently $v=e^{i\theta}u$, then $F$ is constant. Its
   curvature is zero and every chord equality holds.
2. If $P\ne Q$, then $F$ is real analytic on $(0,1)$, continuous on
   $[0,1]$, and **strictly concave in the chord sense on $[0,1]$**.
3. For $P\ne Q$ and $0<t<1$,

   \[
   \boxed{F''(t)=0
   \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
   t=\tfrac12\ \text{and}\ v=e^{i\theta}Du,}
   \tag{1}
   \]

   where $D$ is diagonal with entries in $\{+1,-1\}$. Both signs must
   occur on the support of $u$; signs on common zero coordinates are
   irrelevant. In every other interior case $F''(t)<0$.
4. In the exceptional case, let $\alpha,\beta>0$, $\alpha+\beta=1$, be
   the squared-norm masses of the two sign groups. With $x=t-1/2$,

   \[
   \boxed{
   F(\tfrac12+x)-F(\tfrac12)
   =-8x^4+\frac{32}{3}\frac{(\alpha-\beta)^2}{\alpha\beta}x^6
    +O(x^8).}
   \tag{2}
   \]

   In particular, the proposed universal quartic coefficient $-8$ is
   correct. The nonnegative entropy **loss** $F(1/2)-F(1/2+x)$ has leading
   coefficient $+8$. The remainder is for each fixed pair of endpoints;
   it is not uniform as a sign-group mass tends to zero.

The basic concavity for these endpoints was already available from workers
019/027. It is rederived only to expose its equality conditions, and is not
presented as new work. The additional quantitative chord bound (11) below
is an immediate consequence of the same exact decomposition. No historical
novelty is claimed, and this restricted classification does not solve the
general Lyons conjecture.

## 1. Exact atoms and their fixed interior support

Put

\[
 a_i=|u_i|^2,\quad b_i=|v_i|^2,\quad c=u^*v,\quad
 w_{ij}=|u_iv_j-v_iu_j|^2\quad(i<j),
\]

extend $w$ symmetrically with $w_{ii}=0$, and define

\[
 \gamma=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}=1-|c|^2,\qquad
 d_i=\sum_jw_{ij},\qquad s=t(1-t).
 \tag{3}
\]

The identity for $\gamma$ is the two-vector Gram determinant identity.
If $\gamma=0$, equality in Cauchy–Schwarz gives proportional vectors and
the constant case of the theorem. Assume henceforth $\gamma>0$.

The kernel $K(t)$ has rank two and trace one when $0<t<1$. Its two
nonzero eigenvalues are positive and sum to one, so both are strictly less
than one. Thus $I-K(t)>0$, even though $K(t)$ can have a nontrivial nullspace.
The DPP is supported on subsets of size at most two, and its atoms are

\[
 p_\varnothing=s\gamma,\qquad
 p_{ij}=s w_{ij},\qquad
 p_i=(1-t)a_i+tb_i-sd_i.
 \tag{4}
\]

Indeed, the pair principal minor is $s w_{ij}$, and no larger set can
occur because all principal minors of order at least three vanish. Subtracting
pair atoms from the one-point inclusion probability yields the singleton
formula. Since $\sum_i d_i=2\gamma$, normalization gives the empty atom.

For positivity and subsequent equality conditions, define the positive
semidefinite rank-two matrix

\[
 L=K(I-K)^{-1}.
\]

The standard $L$-ensemble atom formula is

\[
 p_S=\det(I-K)\det L_S=p_\varnothing\det L_S.
 \tag{5}
\]

It follows by expanding

\[
 \det(I-K+KZ)=\det(I-K)\det(I+LZ)
\]

in diagonal variables $Z$. This formula also applies to positive
semidefinite $K$ with zero eigenvalues, since only $I-K$ is inverted.
It is equation (2.12) in [Lyons (2014)](https://arxiv.org/html/1406.2707v1).

Let $E_0=\{i:a_i+b_i>0\}$. For $i\in E_0$, $K_{ii}>0$, and

\[
 L-K=K^2(I-K)^{-1}\succeq0
\]

implies $L_{ii}>0$. Therefore $p_i=p_\varnothing L_{ii}>0$. The
positive interior support is exactly

\[
 \{\varnothing\}\ \cup\ \{\{i\}:i\in E_0\}\ \cup\
 \{\{i,j\}:w_{ij}>0\}.
 \tag{6}
\]

This support is independent of $t\in(0,1)$. All other atoms vanish
identically and may be omitted from derivative sums. The finitely many
positive atom polynomials have analytic logarithms locally at every interior
point, proving real analyticity of $F$ there. No differentiation of a
zero atom or $0/0$ expression occurs below. Polynomial atom probabilities
and continuity of $-p\log p$ at zero prove continuity on the closed interval.

## 2. Curvature decomposition and a strict chord bound

On the fixed support (6), differentiating entropy and using normalization gives

\[
 F''=-\sum_S\frac{(p'_S)^2}{p_S}-\sum_Sp''_S\log p_S.
\]

Equation (4) gives $p_\varnothing''=-2\gamma$, $p_{ij}''=-2w_{ij}$,
and $p_i''=2d_i$. Grouping by pairs yields the exact identity

\[
 \boxed{
 F''(t)=-\operatorname{FI}(t)
 -2\sum_{i<j:w_{ij}>0}w_{ij}
       \log\frac{p_i p_j}{p_\varnothing p_{ij}},
 \qquad
 \operatorname{FI}(t)=\sum_{S:p_S>0}\frac{(p'_S)^2}{p_S}.}
 \tag{7}
\]

For a contributing pair, equation (5) gives

\[
 p_i p_j-p_\varnothing p_{ij}
 =p_\varnothing^2 |L_{ij}|^2\ge0.
 \tag{8}
\]

Every term subtracted in (7) is nonnegative. Moreover, the logarithm for a
positive-weight pair vanishes **if and only if $L_{ij}=0$**. This exact
criterion, rather than a strict version of a generic log-submodularity
inequality, is needed for the classification.

The empty atom and all pair atoms alone give

\[
 \frac{(p'_\varnothing)^2}{p_\varnothing}
 +\sum_{w_{ij}>0}\frac{(p'_{ij})^2}{p_{ij}}
 =\frac{2\gamma(1-2t)^2}{t(1-t)}.
\]

Consequently

\[
 F''(t)\le-\frac{2\gamma(1-2t)^2}{t(1-t)}<0
 \quad\text{when }t\ne\tfrac12.
 \tag{9}
\]

This already precludes any nondegenerate affine interval. Concavity on the
open interval follows from (7), and continuity extends it to $[0,1]$.
Equality at an interior point of any nontrivial chord of a concave function
would force the function to be affine on that entire chord interval.
Equation (9) excludes this. More explicitly, for any $0\le a<b\le1$
and $0<\lambda<1$,

\[
 F((1-\lambda)a+\lambda b)
 >(1-\lambda)F(a)+\lambda F(b)
 \qquad(P\ne Q).
 \tag{10}
\]

The equality-to-affinity implication can be checked without endpoint
derivatives: subtract the chord so that the concave function is nonnegative
and zero at both ends. If it is zero at one interior point, expressing that
point as a mixture of any point on one side and the opposite endpoint forces
its value at the arbitrary point to be zero. Repeat on the other side.

There is also a quantitative bound. Write

\[
 h_{\mathrm{bin}}(t)=-t\log t-(1-t)\log(1-t),\qquad
 g(t)=2\gamma[h_{\mathrm{bin}}(t)-2t(1-t)].
\]

Then $g(0)=g(1)=0$ and $g''$ is the right side of (9). Thus $F-g$
is concave, including endpoints by continuity, and

\[
 \boxed{
 F(t)-(1-t)F(0)-tF(1)
 \ge 2\gamma[h_{\mathrm{bin}}(t)-2t(1-t)]>0
 \quad(0<t<1).}
 \tag{11}
\]

For the last strict sign, apply $-\log x>1-x$ to $x=t,1-t\in(0,1)$.
This bound is compatible with zero curvature at the midpoint. It is a bound
on the chord gap, not a strictly negative uniform upper bound for $F''$.

## 3. Complete classification of zero interior curvature

Suppose $F''(t)=0$. All terms in (7) must vanish.

### Step 1: Fisher information fixes the parameter and the magnitudes

Since $p_\varnothing>0$, its Fisher summand vanishes only if

\[
 p'_\varnothing=\gamma(1-2t)=0.
\]

Thus $t=1/2$. At that point $p'_i=b_i-a_i$, so the singleton Fisher
terms force

\[
 a_i=b_i\quad\text{for every }i.
 \tag{12}
\]

For a common zero coordinate this is already true. Conversely, (12) makes
every atom derivative zero at $1/2$, so it makes the entire Fisher term zero
there. Equal coordinate magnitudes alone need not make $F''$ zero: the
logarithmic terms still have to vanish.

### Step 2: the phase ratios have exactly two distinct values

Discard common zero coordinates for this argument. On the remaining support,
write

\[
 v_i=z_i u_i,\qquad |z_i|=1,\qquad c=\sum_i a_i z_i.
\]

Now

\[
 w_{ij}=a_i a_j|z_i-z_j|^2,
\]

so a pair contributes to (7) exactly when its phase ratios differ. Equality
therefore forces $L_{ij}=0$ between any two different phase groups. After
permuting coordinates by phase group, $L$ is block diagonal. Each nonempty
block has positive diagonal and hence positive rank. Since $\operatorname{rank}L=2$,
there can be at most two groups. There cannot be only one, because
that would make $u,v$ proportional and $\gamma=0$. Hence exactly two
phase values occur.

For an optional direct algebra check of the same conclusion, the pair equation
(14) below determines $z_j$ uniquely for a fixed $z_i$ whenever

\[
 1+\overline c z_i\ne0.
\]

The inequality $|c|<1$ makes this denominator nonzero. Thus all phases
different from a fixed phase must coincide. This is independent confirmation
of the rank argument.

### Step 3: the two phases must be opposite

Let the two phase values be $z_1,z_2$, with masses

\[
 \alpha=\sum_{i:z_i=z_1}a_i>0,\qquad
 \beta=\sum_{i:z_i=z_2}a_i>0,
 \qquad \alpha+\beta=1.
\]

Thus $c=\alpha z_1+\beta z_2$. To compute the cross entry of $L$,
let $\Pi$ be the orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{span}(u,v)$.
Since the two nonzero eigenvalues of $K$ have sum one and product

\[
 \sum_{i<j}\det K_{\{i,j\}}=s\gamma,
\]

Cayley–Hamilton on that range gives

\[
 K(I-K)=s\gamma\Pi,\qquad L=\frac{K^2}{s\gamma}.
\]

At the midpoint this becomes

\[
 L=\frac{P+Q+PQ+QP}{\gamma}
  =\frac{P+Q+cuv^*+\overline c vu^*}{\gamma}.
 \tag{13}
\]

For coordinates in different phase groups,

\[
 L_{ij}=\frac{u_i\overline u_j}{\gamma}
 [1+z_i\overline z_j+c\overline z_j+\overline c z_i].
\]

Because the prefactor is nonzero, $L_{ij}=0$ is equivalent to

\[
 z_1+z_2+c+\overline c z_1z_2=0.
 \tag{14}
\]

But the two-group expression for $c$, and $|z_1|=|z_2|=1$, give

\[
 c+\overline c z_1z_2
 =\alpha z_1+\beta z_2+\alpha z_2+\beta z_1
 =z_1+z_2.
\]

Thus (14) is exactly $2(z_1+z_2)=0$. The two phases are opposite:

\[
 z_2=-z_1.
\]

This proves the necessity of the sign relation in (1). Notice that no
assumption about real vectors, nonzero coordinates outside the common
support, or equal sign-group masses was made.

### Step 4: sufficiency

Conversely, suppose $v=e^{i\theta}Du$ with both signs present on its
support. The global phase does not affect $Q$, so it may be removed.
The magnitudes agree and all Fisher summands vanish at $t=1/2$. The only
positive $w_{ij}$ connect opposite sign groups. Equation (14) vanishes for
such pairs, so all their $L_{ij}$ vanish and every logarithm in (7) is zero.
Hence $F''(1/2)=0$, proving both directions of (1).

## 4. Exact reduction in the exceptional case and the universal quartic

Retain the sign partition $E_+,E_-$, and write

\[
 \alpha=\sum_{i\in E_+}a_i,\qquad
 \beta=\sum_{j\in E_-}a_j,\qquad
 x=t-\tfrac12,\qquad r=4x^2.
\]

Then $c=\alpha-\beta$ after removing the global phase, and

\[
 \gamma=4\alpha\beta,\qquad
 w_{ij}=\begin{cases}4a_i a_j,&i,j\text{ in opposite groups},\\
 0,&i,j\text{ in the same group}.
 \end{cases}
\]

At most one point from each group can occur. Let $Y_+,Y_-\in\{0,1\}$
record which groups are occupied. The exact group-occupation law $R_r$ is

| $(Y_+,Y_-)$ | Probability under $R_r$ |
|---|---:|
| $(0,0)$ | $\alpha\beta(1-r)$ |
| $(1,0)$ | $\alpha^2+\alpha\beta r$ |
| $(0,1)$ | $\beta^2+\alpha\beta r$ |
| $(1,1)$ | $\alpha\beta(1-r)$ |

For example, summing the singleton formula over $E_+$ gives

\[
 \sum_{i\in E_+}p_i=\alpha^2+\alpha\beta r,
\]

and summing cross-group pair probabilities gives $\alpha\beta(1-r)$.
The marginals of $Y_+,Y_-$ are fixed at $\alpha,\beta$.

Conditioned on a group being occupied, its site is drawn with probabilities

\[
 A_i=a_i/\alpha\quad(i\in E_+),\qquad
 B_j=a_j/\beta\quad(j\in E_-).
\]

When both groups are occupied these choices are independent, because the
pair atoms are a common scalar times $a_i a_j$. These conditional laws
are independent of $t$. The entropy chain rule consequently gives

\[
 F(t)=H(R_r)+\alpha H(A)+\beta H(B).
 \tag{15}
\]

At $r=0$, $R_0=\operatorname{Ber}(\alpha)\otimes\operatorname{Ber}(\beta)$.
Since all $R_r$ have these marginals,
their cross entropy with $R_0$ is constant. Therefore

\[
 F(t)-F(\tfrac12)=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(R_r\Vert R_0).
 \tag{16}
\]

This holds continuously for the entire interval $0\le r\le1$. In full,

\[
\begin{aligned}
 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(R_r\Vert R_0)={}&
 2\alpha\beta(1-r)\log(1-r)\\
 &+(\alpha^2+\alpha\beta r)
          \log\!\left(1+\frac\beta\alpha r\right)\\
 &+(\beta^2+\alpha\beta r)
          \log\!\left(1+\frac\alpha\beta r\right),
\end{aligned}
 \tag{17}
\]

with $0\log0=0$ at $r=1$. Thus the whole nonconstant entropy shape,
up to the additive constant in (15), depends only on the two group masses.

For the expansion, set

\[
 R_r=R_0+r\Delta,\qquad
 \Delta=\alpha\beta(-1,1,1,-1),
\]

in the table's order. The fixed marginals cancel the cross-entropy term,
and the scalar logarithm series gives

\[
 D(R_r\Vert R_0)
 =\frac{r^2}{2}\sum_y\frac{\Delta_y^2}{(R_0)_y}
 -\frac{r^3}{6}\sum_y\frac{\Delta_y^3}{(R_0)_y^2}
 +O(r^4).
\]

The two exact sums are

\[
 \sum_y\frac{\Delta_y^2}{(R_0)_y}
 =2\alpha\beta+\alpha^2+\beta^2=1,
\]

\[
 \sum_y\frac{\Delta_y^3}{(R_0)_y^2}
 =\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha^3}{\beta}-2\alpha\beta
 =\frac{(\alpha-\beta)^2}{\alpha\beta}.
\]

Substituting $r=4x^2$ proves (2). The expansion converges, for example,
whenever

\[
 |r|<\min\{1,\alpha/\beta,\beta/\alpha\}.
\]

It follows in particular that

\[
 F''(\tfrac12+x)=-96x^2+
 320\frac{(\alpha-\beta)^2}{\alpha\beta}x^4+O(x^6),
 \qquad F^{(4)}(\tfrac12)=-192.
 \tag{18}
\]

Also, $R_1$ is supported on $(1,0),(0,1)$ with probabilities

\[
 (\alpha,\beta),
\]

so the exact midpoint chord gap is

\[
 F(\tfrac12)-F(0)=F(\tfrac12)-F(1)=h_{\mathrm{bin}}(\alpha)>0.
 \tag{19}
\]

In this case $F(0)=F(1)$, because the endpoint singleton magnitudes
agree. Equations (16)–(19) show explicitly why zero second derivative at the
midpoint is fully compatible with strict chords and a strict maximum there.

## 5. Boundary behavior and the obstruction to uniform negative curvature

The classification of ordinary finite curvature in (1) concerns $0<t<1$.
For distinct endpoints the boundary derivatives are singular, rather than
additional zero-curvature cases. As $t\downarrow0$, the combined entropy
contribution of the empty and pair atoms is exactly

\[
 -2\gamma s\log s
 -s\left[\gamma\log\gamma+
          \sum_{w_{ij}>0}w_{ij}\log w_{ij}\right].
\]

For a singleton with $a_i>0$, its entropy summand is analytic at zero.
If $a_i=0<b_i$, then $u_i=0$, $d_i=b_i$, and its exact probability
is $p_i=b_i t^2$. Common zero coordinates remain absent. These explicit
forms give

\[
 F(t)=H(a)-2\gamma t\log t+O(t),\qquad
 F'(t)=-2\gamma\log t+O(1),
\]

\[
 F''(t)=-\frac{2\gamma}{t}+O(|\log t|+1).
 \tag{20}
\]

Swapping endpoints gives $F'(t)\to-\infty$ and

\[
 F''(t)=-\frac{2\gamma}{1-t}+O(|\log(1-t)|+1)
 \quad(t\uparrow1).
\]

These statements are derived from explicit atom forms, not by differentiating
an arbitrary $O(t)$ remainder. The proof of strict chords above only needs
continuity at endpoints and does not rely on these asymptotics.

For the simplest zero-curvature example, take

\[
 u=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^T,\qquad
 v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^T.
\]

Then $\gamma=1$, $K(1/2)=I_2/2$, and the four atoms are

\[
 p_\varnothing=p_{12}=\tfrac14-x^2,\qquad
 p_1=p_2=\tfrac14+x^2.
\]

Thus

\[
 F(\tfrac12+x)=2\log2-8x^4-\frac{64}{3}x^8+O(x^{12}),
\]

and $F''(1/2)=0$. More generally,

\[
 u=(\sqrt\alpha,\sqrt\beta)^T,\qquad
 v=(\sqrt\alpha,-\sqrt\beta)^T
\]

realizes zero midpoint curvature for every $\gamma=4\alpha\beta\in(0,1]$.
Consequently **no bound $F''(t)\le-B(\gamma)$ with $B(\gamma)>0$**
can hold uniformly over these paths and all interior $t$, for any chosen
nonzero $\gamma$. Formula (9), whose right side vanishes at the midpoint,
is a valid replacement. The universal quartic coefficient does not supply
a uniform Taylor neighborhood as $\gamma\downarrow0$.
