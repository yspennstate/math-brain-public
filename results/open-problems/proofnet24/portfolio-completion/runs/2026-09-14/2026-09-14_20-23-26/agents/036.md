## 1. Conditional kernels and the exact Hessian identity

Consider an interior kernel and an arbitrary fixed-outside-diagonal direction

\[
K=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B^*&c\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D=\begin{pmatrix}E&V\\V^*&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0<K<I.
\]

Here `A,E` are two-by-two Hermitian and `B,V` are two-component complex columns. Necessarily `0<c<1`. Write `a=1-c` for a weight, not a matrix entry. Conditional on absence or presence of the outside site, respectively, the kernels are

\[
C_0=A+BB^*/a,\qquad C_1=A-BB^*/c.
\tag{3}
\]

The weights are `a,c`. The Schur complement of `K` gives `C1>0`; the Schur complement of `I-K` gives `I-C0>0`. Also `C0≥A>0`, and `I-C1=I-A+BB*/c>0`. Thus both conditional kernels are strict positive contractions. The exact entropy chain rule is

\[
H_3(K)=h(c)+aH_2(C_0)+cH_2(C_1).
\tag{4}
\]

Along `K+tD`, put

\[
W=VB^*+BV^*,\quad
U_0=E+W/a,\quad U_1=E-W/c.
\]

Direct differentiation gives `C0'=U0`, `C1'=U1`,

\[
C_0''=2VV^*/a,\qquad C_1''=-2VV^*/c.
\]

Use the real Hilbert-space pairing `⟨X,Y⟩=tr(XY)` on Hermitian matrices and define

\[
G=\nabla H_2(C_0)-\nabla H_2(C_1).
\]

Differentiating (4), with the weights held fixed, yields

\[
\boxed{
D^2H_3(K)[D,D]
=aD^2H_2(C_0)[U_0,U_0]
 +cD^2H_2(C_1)[U_1,U_1]
 +2V^*GV.
}
\tag{5}
\]

In particular the last term is `+2 tr(G VV*)`, with no additional factor of `c` or `1-c`. The previously proved two-site theorem makes the first two terms nonpositive, but does not alone determine the last term.

The conditional pair satisfies the ordered rank-one relation

\[
C_0-C_1=\frac{BB^*}{c(1-c)}.
\tag{6}
\]

Conversely any strict ordered pair with a rank-one difference can be realized this way: choose `B` with `BB*=c(1-c)(C0-C1)` and set `A=(1-c)C0+cC1`. The Schur complements are `C1` and `I-C0`, so the resulting three-site kernel is a strict positive contraction. There is therefore no hidden compatibility issue in using ordered rank-one pairs to test this route.

## 2. Joint concavity with a fixed cross-vector direction

**Theorem.** Let `b∈C²` and `c∈[0,1]` be fixed. Entropy is concave on the closed-contraction set (1).

**Proof in the interior.** Take an arbitrary affine segment in (1). At a given point write `B=z b` and `V=v b`, with `v` the fixed complex derivative of `z`. If `z=0`, then `C0=C1` and the last term in (5) vanishes. If `z≠0`, set

\[
\rho=\frac{|z|^2}{c(1-c)}>0,
\qquad F(s)=H_2(C_1+sbb^*),\quad 0\le s\le\rho.
\]

Both endpoints are strict contractions and the segment between them is feasible. Two-site entropy concavity gives scalar concavity of `F`, hence

\[
b^*Gb=F'(\rho)-F'(0)\le0.
\tag{7}
\]

Consequently the actual correction in (5) is

\[
2V^*GV=2|v|^2b^*Gb\le0.
\]

The two conditional Hessians in (5) are also nonpositive, for the arbitrary jointly varying block direction `E`. Thus the entropy second derivative is nonpositive at every point of the segment. Integration proves its chord inequality.

**Boundary.** For endpoints `Kj` in the closed slice use

\[
K_j^{(\varepsilon)}=(1-2\varepsilon)K_j+\varepsilon I_3.
\]

Both regularized endpoints have the same outside diagonal `cε=(1-2ε)c+ε`, and their cross-vectors remain complex multiples of `b`. Apply the interior result and pass to the limit by continuity of the finitely many atom probabilities and `−p log p`. If `c=0` or `c=1`, positivity directly forces `B=0`; the same conclusion also follows from regularization. This proves the whole closed-slice theorem. ∎

This proof uses only the scalar monotonicity (7) along the displayed rank-one segment. It does **not** infer a matrix inequality for `G` from scalar concavity and makes no rotation of the point basis.

The result genuinely allows directions beyond the earlier slices. For example, `E=I2` and `V=b≠0` give

\[
\det\begin{pmatrix}I_2&b\\b^*&0\end{pmatrix}=-\|b\|^2<0.
\]

This direction has rank three, whereas a single row/column direction has rank at most two. It also varies the cross block, unlike the fixed-principal-block result. Feasible small segments with this direction exist around suitable interior points.

**Corollary.** If two three-site kernels have the same outside diagonal and one endpoint has zero cross-vector, their connecting segment satisfies the entropy-concavity inequality, with no restriction on the two endpoint blocks `A`. Choose `b` equal to the other endpoint's cross-vector. This is a zero-*cross-block* result, not an assertion that Gu's known zero-*kernel* endpoint result is new.

## 3. Two-site gradient and exact failure of operator antitonicity

For

\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&z\\\bar z&\delta\end{pmatrix},
\]

write the four atoms in the order `00,10,01,11` as

\[
x=(1-\alpha)(1-\delta)-|z|^2,\quad
y=\alpha(1-\delta)+|z|^2,\quad
q=(1-\alpha)\delta+|z|^2,\quad
w=\alpha\delta-|z|^2,
\]

and let `L=log(yq/(xw))`. Differentiation gives the exact Hermitian gradient

\[
\nabla H_2(C)=
\begin{pmatrix}
(1-\delta)\log(x/y)+\delta\log(q/w)&-Lz\\
-L\bar z&(1-\alpha)\log(x/q)+\alpha\log(y/w)
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{8}
\]

Now take

\[
\boxed{K_*=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/5&3/20&1/4\\
3/20&9/10&0\\
1/4&0&1/2
\end{pmatrix}.}
\tag{9}
\]

The leading principal minors of `K*` are `1/5,63/400,9/400`; those of `I-K*` are `4/5,23/400,9/400`. Sylvester's criterion proves `0<K*<I` exactly. Here `c=1/2`, `B=(1/4,0)^T`, and

\[
C_0=\begin{pmatrix}13/40&3/20\\3/20&9/10\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
C_1=\begin{pmatrix}3/40&3/20\\3/20&9/10\end{pmatrix},
\qquad C_0-C_1=\tfrac14 e_1e_1^*.
\tag{10}
\]

Their conditional atom vectors in the order above are

\[
p(C_0)=(9,11,126,54)/200,
\qquad p(C_1)=(14,6,171,9)/200.
\tag{11}
\]

Substitution in the lower-right entry of (8) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
40e_2^*Ge_2
&=27\log(1/14)+13\log(11/54)
-37\log(14/171)-3\log(2/3)\\
&=\log\frac{3^{38}19^{37}11^{13}}{2^{80}7^{64}}.

\tag{12}
\]

An exact integer comparison in `036_exact_certificate.py` establishes

\[

\]

Thus `e2*G e2>0`; numerically it is `0.04685309401474015`. For orientation only, the floating-point matrix is

with eigenvalues approximately `−2.00429862,0.05892786`. The proof of the violation is the exact scalar identity (12) and integer inequality (13), not those eigenvalue approximations.

This disproves `∇H2(C0)−∇H2(C1)≤0` even for a real rank-one ordered pair realizable by a strict three-site kernel. It is compatible with (7): the positive witness `e2` is transverse to the actual cross-vector `B`, while (7) controls the direction `e1` here.

## 7. Exact remaining compensation inequality

The following reduction preserves all conditional Hessian information and identifies what the failed operator-gradient route omitted. Treat

\[
S_j=-D^2H_2(C_j)
\]

as positive operators on the four-real-dimensional space of two-by-two Hermitian matrices. In the generic case where both conditional off-diagonal entries are nonzero, these operators are positive definite. To check this strictness using the two-site proof: if a nonzero direction has negative determinant, its `2 det(D)L` term is strictly negative since `L>0`; if determinant zero, its nonzero diagonal marginal derivative forces a positive Fisher term; if determinant positive, worker 016 gives the strict bound `−8 det(D)`.

For a fixed `V` let `W=VB*+BV*`. Minimize the conditional nonnegative cost over the unrestricted block direction `E`:

\[
Q(E,V)=a\langle E+W/a,S_0(E+W/a)\rangle
+c\langle E-W/c,S_1(E-W/c)\rangle.
\]

Writing `X=E+W/a`, `Y=E−W/c` turns the constraint into `X−Y=W/(ac)`. A Lagrange-multiplier calculation (valid without commutativity) gives

\[
\boxed{
\min_E Q(E,V)
=\frac1{c(1-c)}
\left\langle W,
\big(cS_0^{-1}+(1-c)S_1^{-1}\big)^{-1}W\right\rangle.
}
\tag{21}
\]

For clarity, if `Λ=aS0 X=−cS1 Y`, then
`X−Y=(S0^{-1}/a+S1^{-1}/c)Λ`; substituting the constraint gives (21). This establishes the operator-order placement of the weights and inverses directly.

By (5), full two-row-slice concavity at this base is therefore equivalent to the four-real-variable inequality

\[
\boxed{
2V^*GV\le\frac1{c(1-c)}
\left\langle VB^*+BV^*,
\big(cS_0^{-1}+(1-c)S_1^{-1}\big)^{-1}
(VB^*+BV^*)\right\rangle
\quad\text{for every }V\in\mathbb C^2.
}
\tag{22}
\]

The non-generic case can be approached by limits or handled by the original minimization; no inverse of a singular Hessian is asserted here. Formula (22) is a precise remaining obligation, not a proof that it always holds. Section 2 proves it when `V` belongs to the complex span of `B`, because the left side is then nonpositive. The example in Section 3 proves that this sign argument cannot cover all `V`. The certificate in Section 5 verifies the stronger full Hessian claim at one obstructing base, while leaving (22) unresolved universally.
