## 2. The nonsingularity dependency is valid at boundaries

For a fixed point \(p\), parent exclusion implies
\[
p_i=V_i(p)\le(1-p_i)^2,
\qquad
p_i\le c=(3-\sqrt5)/2<1/2.
\]
Let \(A(p)_{rj}=\sum_a P_{aj,r}p_a\). Its entries are nonnegative, its
columns sum to one, and its diagonal is zero. Parent symmetry gives
\(DV(p)=2A(p)\). Both \(A(p)\) and \(DV(p)\) preserve the real tangent
space \(E=\{z:\sum_i z_i=0\}\).

For a two-pair partition \(G=\{i,k\}\), \(H=\{j,\ell\}\), put
\(r_{ab}=\sum_{s\in G}P_{ab,s}\). Exclusion and normalization give
\(r_{ki}=0\), \(r_{\ell j}=1\), and \(0\le r_{ab}\le1\). Expanding
\(\mathbf1_G^{\mathsf T}A(p)(e_i-e_j)\) and dropping nonpositive terms
gives
\[
\mathbf1_G^{\mathsf T}A(p)(e_i-e_j)
\le p_i(1-r_{ij})+p_jr_{ij}
\le\max(p_i,p_j)\le c.
\]
This is the precise transfer inequality needed here.

Suppose \(z\ne0\) lies in \(E\) and \(DV(p)z=z\).
A singleton positive sign support is impossible: at that coordinate,
the nonnegative zero-diagonal matrix \(DV(p)\) sends \(z\) to a
nonpositive number. A singleton negative support is excluded by replacing
\(z\) with \(-z\). In four coordinates, the only remaining case has
exactly two positive and two negative entries, with no zeros. If
\(h=\sum_{i\in G}z_i>0\), write
\[
z=h\sum_{i\in G,j\in H}
 \frac{z_i(-z_j)}{h^2}(e_i-e_j).
\]
The weights sum to one. Taking the sum over \(G\) in \(DV(p)z=z\)
now gives \(h\le2ch<h\), a contradiction. Thus \(I-DV(p)|_E\)
is invertible. This proves the needed dependency directly from the
coefficient conditions and cap, for all of \(E\); feasible two-sided
motions inside the simplex are never assumed. Its real determinant is
nonzero, hence so is the complexified determinant.

The same transfer proof, applied to
\(V(x)-V(y)=2A((x+y)/2)(x-y)\), proves uniqueness of simplex fixed
points. Existence is the standard Brouwer application to the continuous
simplex self-map. This closes the relevant logical dependency rather
than using a numerical fixed point as evidence for nonsingularity.

In the chart \(y=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\), \(x_4=1-\sum y_i\), the first three
components of \(x-V(x)\) represent the tangent residual in the basis
\(e_i-e_4\). Therefore their Jacobian determinant \(D\) is precisely
the determinant of the tangent operator just proved invertible.

## 3. Universal coordinate-field degree and saturation

There are three equations
\[
f_i(y)=x_i-V_i(x)=0,\quad i=1,2,3,
\]
of total degree at most two. Normalization makes the fourth equation
dependent. Nonsingular common zeros are isolated. At most eight such
zeros can occur even if other components are positive dimensional:
any finite chosen collection of nonsingular zeros survives a sufficiently
small generic perturbation of the three quadrics by the complex implicit
function theorem, and the generic product-of-degrees bound is eight.
Nine chosen simple zeros would contradict that bound.

The cited isolated-point formulation is accurate. Mondal's
[General Bezout-type theorems, Theorem 3.1 and Example 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.0614)
was independently opened. The theorem counts isolated points with
multiplicity in every fiber of a generically finite map; unit variable
weights give the ordinary degree-product inequality. Here the map
\(f:\mathbb A^3\to\mathbb A^3\) is generically finite because its
Jacobian is nonzero at the simplex zero. The elementary perturbation
argument already suffices for the nonsingular count used in this proof.

Set \(I=(f_1,f_2,f_3)\) and
\[
J=I:D^\infty=(f_1,f_2,f_3,uD-1)\cap\mathbb Q[y].
\]
The localized zero set \(V(I)\cap\{D\ne0\}\) is finite, with at most
eight points. It is therefore already closed, so taking its closure in
the saturation introduces no points with \(D=0\). At every retained
point the local algebra is reduced of length one by the invertible
Jacobian. Equivalently, adjoining \(uD=1\) produces a finite reduced
algebra; contracting its defining ideal remains radical. Thus
\[
B=\mathbb Q[y]/J,\qquad 1\le\dim_{\mathbb Q}B=N\le8.
\]
The positivity of \(N\) follows from the actual simplex zero and
\(D(p)\ne0\).

This argument proves algebraicity rather than assuming it.
Evaluation at \(p\) maps \(B\) onto \(\mathbb Q[p_1,p_2,p_3]\),
a finite-dimensional domain over \(\mathbb Q\); such a domain is a
field. Since \(p_4=1-p_1-p_2-p_3\),
\[
[\mathbb Q(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4):\mathbb Q]\le N\le8.
\]
The field bound is for the joint coordinates. Separate coordinate
bounds are not multiplied. Conversely, its distinct embeddings give
distinct normalized nonsingular complex fixed points; they need not be
simplex points.

There is no requirement that the unsaturated ideal be finite for every
admissible coefficient array. No argument here relies on an unproved
such assertion or on an unspecialized resultant remaining valid after
degeneration.

## 5. Exact selection: why the 57 trials suffice

Because \(B\) is finite and geometrically reduced,
\(B\otimes\mathbb C\cong\mathbb C^N\). Coordinate multiplication is
diagonal in the point-evaluation basis. For
\[
\ell_m=y_1+my_2+m^2y_3,
\]
a distinct point pair has equal values only when a nonzero polynomial
of degree at most two in \(m\) vanishes. There are
\(\binom N2\) unordered pairs, so at most \(N(N-1)\le56\) integers
are bad. Testing \(m=0,\ldots,N(N-1)\) guarantees a separator; testing
\(0,\ldots,56\) is a uniform version.

The characteristic polynomial of multiplication by \(\ell_m\) is
\(\prod_{z}(t-\ell_m(z))\). It is squarefree exactly when the values
are pairwise distinct. For a separator, the Vandermonde determinant
shows that \(1,\ell_m,\ldots,\ell_m^{N-1}\) is a basis after extension
to \(\mathbb C\), hence already a basis over \(\mathbb Q\).
Rational linear algebra expresses each coordinate as \(g_i(\ell_m)\)
with \(\deg g_i<N\).

Real-root isolation and exact signs of
\(g_1,g_2,g_3,1-g_1-g_2-g_3\) select exactly one root: Brouwer gives
existence, saturation retains it, and simplex uniqueness excludes a
second. Its irreducible factor is the minimal polynomial of the
separating value at \(p\), whose field is exactly the coordinate field.
Zero coordinate signs can be determined algebraically. The analogous
two-variable boundary forms \(y_1+my_2\) need at most seven trials.

These are valid finite algorithmic instructions. The original Python
and this audit program implement the favorable example construction,
not general saturation, arbitrary-input real algebraic sign selection,
or a bit-complexity theorem.

## 8. Incidence irreducibility and generic degree

The signed normalized coefficient space is
\(\mathscr A\cong\mathbb A^{14}_{\mathbb Q}\), since four diagonal
rows contribute two parameters each and six distinct-parent rows one

\[

\]

Every other normalized state has two nonzero coordinates \(x_i,x_j\).
Variations of diagonal row \(ii\) span \(x_i^2U_i\), where
\(U_i=\{v\in E:v_i=0\}\). These are two-dimensional spaces, and
\(U_i+U_j=E\). Nonzero complex coordinates have nonzero squares.
Thus the coefficient-to-output derivative has rank three at every
possible state, including states with zero coordinates.

The affine fixed-point coefficient fiber over each nonvertex state is
nonempty of dimension eleven: the affine output map is onto the
normalized target hyperplane. Inverting any nonzero coefficient minor
gives a rational algebraic trivialization with eleven free coordinates.
Therefore \(\mathcal I\) is an affine-space bundle over
\(X\setminus\{e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4\}\), and is geometrically irreducible,
smooth, and 14-dimensional. All possible normalized states have been
covered. There are no omitted incidence components on a coordinate
hyperplane.

The exact octic fiber has eight nonsingular complex points. Each
continues as a local complex branch over a neighborhood of its
coefficient value. This gives dominance of
\(\mathcal I\to\mathscr A\); equal dimensions then give a generically
finite map. The nonempty Zariski open set with the generic number of
reduced points intersects that complex neighborhood, so the generic

\[
[\mathbb Q(\mathcal I):\mathbb Q(\mathscr A)]=8.
