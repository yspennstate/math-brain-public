## 4. New theorem — strip confinement has a uniform exponential lifetime

### Claim `NM13-12-P0483-STRIP-EXIT-TAIL-01` — PROVED, PARTIAL relative to P0483

Let `omega` be any nearest-neighbor environment on `Z^d` satisfying uniform ellipticity

`omega(x,e) >= kappa > 0`

for every site `x` and nearest-neighbor direction `e`. Let

`S = {x : a <= x_j <= a+w-1}`

be a coordinate strip of integer width `w >= 1`, and let `A subseteq S`. Set

`m = ceil(w/2)`,  `p = kappa^m`,  `q = 1-p`.

Then for every `x in A` and every integer `r >= 0`,

`P_{x,omega}(tau_A > r m) <= q^r`.                                      (4.1)

Consequently, for all `n >= 0`,

`P_{x,omega}(tau_A > n) <= q^{-1} exp(-c_0 n)`,                         (4.2)

where

`c_0 = -log(1-kappa^m)/m > 0`.                                          (4.3)

In particular,

`c_slab(kappa,w) >= -log(1-kappa^ceil(w/2))/ceil(w/2)`.                 (4.4)

Also,

`E_{x,omega}[tau_A] <= m kappa^{-m}`,                                   (4.5)

and for every `0 <= lambda < c_0`,

`E_{x,omega}[exp(lambda tau_A)]
 <= p exp(lambda m) / (1-q exp(lambda m))`.                             (4.6)

No sharpness is claimed for (4.3)–(4.6).

### Proof

Fix any state `y in A`. Its `j`-th coordinate lies in the integer interval `[a,a+w-1]`. One of the two coordinate directions `+e_j` or `-e_j` reaches the complement of this interval in at most

`m = ceil(w/2)`

nearest-neighbor steps: move toward the nearer of the two strip boundaries. Let the required number of steps be `s(y) <= m`.

If the walk follows that deterministic monotone coordinate path, it leaves the strip and hence leaves `A` no later than step `s(y)`. By uniform ellipticity, conditional on the current state and entire past, the probability of following this path is at least

`kappa^{s(y)} >= kappa^m = p`,

because `0<kappa<=1`. Therefore, from **every** state still in `A`, regardless of the previous trajectory,

`P_omega(tau_A <= t+m | F_t, tau_A>t) >= p`.                            (4.7)

Equivalently,

`P_omega(tau_A > t+m | F_t, tau_A>t) <= q`.                             (4.8)

Apply (4.8) at block times `t = 0,m,2m,...` and iterate using the Markov property (indeed the conditional estimate itself is enough). This gives (4.1):

`P(tau_A > r m) <= q^r`.

For arbitrary `n`, put `r=floor(n/m)`. Since `rm <= n`,

`P(tau_A>n) <= P(tau_A>rm) <= q^r`.

Because `r >= n/m - 1` and `log q < 0`,

`q^r <= q^{-1} exp((n/m) log q)
      = q^{-1} exp(-c_0 n)`,

which proves (4.2)–(4.4).

For the mean, use `E tau_A = sum_{n>=0} P(tau_A>n)` and group times into blocks of length `m`:

`E tau_A
 <= sum_{r>=0} m q^r
 = m/(1-q)
 = m/p
 = m kappa^{-m}`,

proving (4.5).

Finally let `N=ceil(tau_A/m)`. Equation (4.1) says

`P(N>r) <= q^r`,

so `N` is stochastically dominated by a geometric random variable `G` on `{1,2,...}` with success probability `p`. Since `tau_A <= mN`, for `lambda>=0`,

`E exp(lambda tau_A) <= E exp(lambda m G)`.

The latter geometric series converges exactly when `q exp(lambda m)<1`, i.e. `lambda<c_0`, and then equals

`p exp(lambda m)/(1-q exp(lambda m))`.

This proves (4.6). QED.

## 5. B30 — localize one transverse scale

The theorem immediately gives a path-range statement that is more useful for testing candidate traps.

For a coordinate direction `e_j`, define the one-coordinate range width up to time `n` by

`W_n^(j) = 1 + max_{0<=t<=n} X_t·e_j - min_{0<=t<=n} X_t·e_j`.

If `W_n^(j) <= w`, then, because `X_0=0`, the entire path up to time `n` lies in at least one integer strip of width `w` containing zero. There are exactly `w` such strips in this coordinate: their lower endpoints are `-(w-1),...,0`. Applying (4.1) to each strip and taking a union bound gives the **quenched**, environment-uniform estimate

`P_{0,omega}(W_n^(j) <= w)
 <= w (1-kappa^ceil(w/2))^floor(n/ceil(w/2)).`                           (5.1)

The same estimate therefore holds after averaging over any environment law, including the i.i.d. law in P0483.

A useful logarithmic corollary is explicit. Put `b=log(1/kappa)>0`. For fixed `eta in (0,1)` and large `n`, let

`m_n = floor((1-eta) log n / b) >= 1`,  `w_n=2m_n`.

Then `kappa^{m_n} >= n^{-(1-eta)}`. Also `floor(n/m_n) >= n/(2m_n)` for all sufficiently large `n`. Since `1-u <= exp(-u)`, (5.1) yields

`P_{0,omega}(W_n^(j) <= 2m_n)
 <= 2m_n exp(- n^eta/(2m_n)).`                                          (5.2)

Thus, uniformly in every `kappa`-elliptic environment, remaining for `n` steps inside a coordinate strip narrower than approximately

`2(1-eta) log n / log(1/kappa)`

has stretched-exponentially small probability. This is a genuine quantitative movement of the frozen metric and a complete one-scale theorem; it is **not** a regeneration-time estimate.

## 6. B2 — attack the negation first

The counterexample attempt was to transplant the classical one-dimensional transient-zero-speed mechanism into higher dimension as a long bad corridor: make longitudinal progress require crossing a long interval-like trap while keeping the transverse geometry narrow.

The strip theorem kills this simplest mechanism under uniform ellipticity. If a trap `A_L` of longitudinal scale `L` is contained in a coordinate strip of width `w_L`, and its quenched mean residence time were at least `exp(aL)` for some fixed `a>0`, then (4.5), with `m_L=ceil(w_L/2)`, forces

`exp(aL) <= m_L kappa^{-m_L}`,

hence

`aL <= log m_L + m_L log(1/kappa)`.                                    (6.1)

In particular `w_L=o(L)` is impossible for an `exp(aL)` mean-delay mechanism. More quantitatively, if `w_L/L` stays bounded, (6.1) implies

`liminf_{L->infinity} w_L/L >= 2a/log(1/kappa)`.                        (6.2)

If `w_L/L` is unbounded, (6.2) is trivially satisfied. Therefore any exponentially strong corridor trap must widen linearly in its longitudinal scale, up to lower-order terms.

This is an obstruction to a proposed counterexample mechanism, **not** evidence that all counterexamples are impossible.

## 7. B6 — deform the one-dimensional trap and identify exactly what breaks

In one dimension, a transient walk can be forced through a long bad interval because there is no transverse bypass. A zero-speed mechanism can therefore be built from rare long barriers whose crossing times dominate their occurrence probabilities.

Deform that picture into `d>=2` by replacing the bad interval with a finite-width tube. Uniform ellipticity supplies a transverse escape path of bounded length from every point of the tube. The proof above makes the failure quantitative: for fixed tube width, residence time has a uniform exponential moment, independent of the longitudinal length. To retain an exponential-in-length delay, the transverse width itself must grow linearly with the longitudinal scale.

So the one-dimensional construction does not survive the deformation unless the trap becomes genuinely high-dimensional. This isolates the missing phenomenon much more sharply than the prior finite-set mean bound: a potential zero-speed construction under uniform ellipticity must exploit **widening transverse geometry, repeated returns, or global exploration**, not a fixed-width local corridor.

## 8. What this does and does not solve

What is proved in this run:

1. A uniform quenched exponential exit-tail and exponential-moment bound for arbitrary (even infinite) sets contained in a fixed-width coordinate strip.
2. A quenched/annealed bound on the probability that the walk's transverse range remains narrow for a long time.
3. A necessary linear-width condition for any strip-contained trap that creates exponentially large mean delay in its longitudinal scale.

These are theorem-sized claims and the complete proof is on this page. No finite checker is needed for their logical core; no numerical computation is being promoted to a theorem.

What remains completely open here:

- P0483 asks whether **mere directional transience** forces positive speed in every i.i.d. uniformly elliptic environment.
- The strip estimate does not control trajectories whose transverse range is large.
- It does not bound the full regeneration time, because a no-regeneration path can wander through widening regions, leave and revisit candidate trapping regions, or exploit correlated geometry along its explored path even though the environment itself is i.i.d.
- It does not prove a tail estimate for regeneration-excursion diameter from transience alone.
- It does not produce a source-valid counterexample.

The exact next mathematical obligation is therefore:

> Use directional transience to control the probability of a long no-regeneration trajectory **conditioned on having large transverse range**, or construct a genuine uniformly elliptic i.i.d. mechanism showing that such wide/global excursions can still make the regeneration mean infinite.

That is where the local strip theorem stops.
