## 4. B18 — SAY IT IN ANOTHER LANGUAGE: the maxdet path is a Gaussian graphical model

Claim 16 fixed, for `t>theta(overline H)`, the unique maximum-determinant completion `S_t` with

\[
(S_t)_{ii}=t-1,\qquad (S_t)_{ij}=-1\quad(ij\in E(H)),
\]

and showed that `K_t=S_t^{-1}` vanishes on distinct nonedges. Put

\[
z=\frac1{t-1},\qquad
C_t=\frac{S_t}{t-1},\qquad
P_t=C_t^{-1}=(t-1)K_t.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Then `C_t` is a correlation matrix with

\[
(C_t)_{ij}=-z\quad(ij\in E(H)),
\]

and `P_t` is supported only on the diagonal and graph edges. In Gaussian language, `C_t` is the covariance of the maximum-entropy centered Gaussian with unit marginal variances and prescribed equal negative edge covariances, while `P_t` is its sparse precision matrix. Precision zeros on nonedges are the usual Gaussian conditional-independence Markov property. This language is classical; its value here is that it exposes exact local score equations.

Taking the diagonal of `C_tP_t=I` gives, vertex by vertex,

\[
\boxed{
(P_t)_{ii}-z\sum_{j\sim i}(P_t)_{ij}=1.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Summing gives the conserved scalar identity

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Tr}P_t-2z\sum_{ij\in E(H)}(P_t)_{ij}=n.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Since `P_t` has no distinct-nonedge entries, the canonical primal value is

\[
\frac{\langle J,P_t\rangle}{\operatorname{Tr}P_t}
=1+\frac{2\sum_{ij\in E(H)}(P_t)_{ij}}{\operatorname{Tr}P_t}.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Equations (4.2)--(4.4) are exactly the previous `t-n/Tr(K_t)` identity in local graphical-model form. They show what a source proof must control: not just a spectrum, but the signed edge precision field generated by the uniform negative covariance constraint.

## 5. The standard Paley strict Gram and exact two-anchor conditioning

Let `chi` be the quadratic character on `F_p`, and let the Paley conference matrix be

\[
C_{xy}=\begin{cases}\chi(x-y),&x\ne y,\\0,&x=y.\end{cases}
\]

For `p == 1 mod 4`,

\[
C\mathbf 1=0,\qquad C^2=pI-J.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Put `s=sqrt(p)` and define the standard Paley strict-coloring Gram

\[
Q=\frac{p}{p-1}\left(I-\frac1pJ-\frac1sC\right).
\tag{5.2}
\]

On `1^perp`, the conference eigenvalues are `+/-s`, so (5.2) is PSD; on the all-ones direction it vanishes. Its diagonal is one. For distinct vertices,

\[
Q_{xy}=\begin{cases}
-\dfrac1{s-1},&\chi(x-y)=1,\\[2mm]
\dfrac1{s+1},&\chi(x-y)=-1.
\end{cases}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Let

\[
V=\{x\in F_p\setminus\{0,1\}:\chi(x)=\chi(x-1)=1\},
\qquad m=|V|=\frac{p-5}{4}.
\tag{5.4}
\]

The anchors `0,1` are adjacent, and every `x in V` is adjacent to both. Write

\[
r=-\frac1{s-1}.
\]

The anchor Gram is `A=[[1,r],[r,1]]`; every cross row from `V` to the two anchors equals `r(1,1)`. Therefore the Schur complement of the anchor block subtracts the same number

\[
h=r^2\,\mathbf 1^TA^{-1}\mathbf 1
=\frac{2}{(s-1)(s-2)}
\tag{5.5}
\]

from every entry of the `V x V` block. Its diagonal is

\[
d=1-h=\frac{s(s-3)}{(s-1)(s-2)}.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Normalize the Schur complement by `d`. Direct algebra gives exactly (1.1): edge correlation `-1/(s-3)` and nonedge correlation `(s-5)/((s+1)(s-3))`.

This recovers the known additive cap `sqrt(p)-2` in strict-vector language. The key question is whether a further common-mode subtraction can turn it into the `2sqrt(p)/3` cap. The answer is no at the asymptotic scale.

## 6. B12 — FIND WHAT IS CONSERVED: localized degree is the cubic character trace

For `x in V`, define

\[
S_x=\sum_{y\in F_p}\chi\bigl(y(y-1)(y-x)\bigr).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Let `d_x` be the degree of `x` in `G_{p,2}`. Consider

\[
T_x=\frac18\sum_y
(1+\chi(y))(1+\chi(y-1))(1+\chi(y-x)).
\tag{6.2}
\]

Away from `0,1,x`, this is exactly the adjacency indicator for a localized neighbor. At each of `0,1,x` it contributes `1/2`, so `T_x=d_x+3/2`. Expanding (6.2), the three linear character sums vanish and each of the three distinct quadratic character sums is `-1`. Hence

\[
T_x=\frac{p-3+S_x}{8},
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
 d_x=\frac{p-15+S_x}{8}.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

The polynomial in (6.1) has three distinct roots, so the quadratic Weil bound gives

\[
|S_x|\le 2s.
\tag{6.4}
\]

If `E` is the number of localized edges and `SigmaS=sum_{x in V}S_x`, then

\[
2E=\frac{m(p-15)}8+\frac{\Sigma S}{8}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Now use the two constant off-diagonal values in (1.1). Exact counting yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf 1^TQ_p^{(2)}\mathbf 1
&=m+2E\left(-\frac1{s-3}\right)
 +(m(m-1)-2E)\frac{s-5}{(s+1)(s-3)}\\
&=\frac{s^4-2s^3-2s^2+10s-15+(8-4s)\Sigma S}
{16(s-3)(s+1)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.6}
\]

For `s>2`, the coefficient of `SigmaS` is negative. From (6.4),

\[
\Sigma S\ge -2ms.
\]

Substitution into (6.6) gives the clean exact upper bound

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf 1^TQ_p^{(2)}\mathbf 1
\le
\frac{3(s-1)^2(s^2-5)}{16(s-3)(s+1)}.
}
\tag{6.7}
\]

This all-ones energy is the conserved quantity that kills the common-mode route.

There is also a useful character-language explanation for why the cubic trace is the first genuinely new two-anchor coordinate. In the four-dimensional two-point character algebra generated by

`1`, `chi(y)`, `chi(y-1)`, `chi(y(y-1))`,

the first three synthesis directions under the full Gram (5.2) are respectively zero and the two anchor directions: using `C^2=pI-J`,

\[
Q\mathbf 1=0,\qquad
Q\chi(\cdot)=-s\,Qe_0,\qquad
Q\chi(\cdot-1)=-s\,Qe_1.
\tag{6.8}
\]

For `h(y)=chi(y(y-1))`, however, and `x in V`,

\[
(Qh)_x
=\frac{p}{p-1}\left(1+\frac1p-\frac{S_x}{s}\right).
\tag{6.9}
\]

Thus after the two anchor directions are projected out, the first surviving natural two-point character feature is exactly the elliptic/cubic trace `S_x`, equivalently the degree fluctuation in (6.3). This explains why the earlier degree-aware route was not an arbitrary feature choice: it is the first non-anchor coordinate in the natural two-point character algebra.

## 7. B7 — TAKE THE LIMIT: required common mode is `p^{-1/2}`, available common mode is only `p^{-1}`

Suppose the uniform deflation numerator is PSD:

\[
Q_p^{(2)}-\delta J\succeq0.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Testing (7.1) on the all-ones vector gives

\[
\delta m^2\le\mathbf 1^TQ_p^{(2)}\mathbf 1.
\]

Using `m=(s^2-5)/4` and (6.7),

\[
\boxed{
\delta\le
\frac{3(s-1)^2}{(s-3)(s+1)(s^2-5)}.
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

On the other hand, the normalized deflated edge correlation is

\[
\frac{-1/(s-3)-\delta}{1-\delta}.
\]

For this to equal the source-cap correlation `-3/(2s-3)`, direct solution gives exactly

\[
\delta_* = \frac{s-6}{2s(s-3)}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

For `s>=11`, comparison of (7.2) and (7.3) reduces to

\[
6s(s-1)^2<(s-6)(s+1)(s^2-5).
\tag{7.4}
\]

The difference of the right and left sides factors as

\[
(s-2)(s^3-9s^2-17s-15).
\tag{7.5}
\]

The cubic equals `40` at `s=11`, and its derivative `3s^2-18s-17` is positive for all `s>=11`. Thus (7.4) holds for `s>=11`, proving claim 17.

The limiting scale mismatch is now explicit:

\[
\delta_* = \frac1{2s}+O(s^{-2}),
\qquad
\delta_{\max}^{\rm common}\le\frac3{s^2}+O(s^{-3}).
\tag{7.6}
\]

So the standard anchor-projected Gram has **a full square-root factor too little removable uniform mode**. A successful multiplicative proof cannot be “project the anchors, then project one more approximately constant direction.” It must use nonuniform correlations across the localized graph.

A second B7 diagnostic comes from the maximum-entropy correlation path itself. Write its edge correlation as a parameter `r` near zero. Analyticity at `r=0` and the precision-zero stationarity equations give the formal expansion

\[
C(r)=I+rA+r^2\bigl(N\circ A^2\bigr)+O(r^3),
\tag{7.7}
\]

where `A` is the localized adjacency matrix and `N` is the distinct-nonedge mask. Indeed `C'(0)=A`, while the order-`r^2` nonedge covariance must equal the two-step path count so that the order-`r^2` precision remains zero on nonedges.

At the source scale `r=-Theta(p^{-1/2})`, (6.3) gives `d_x=Theta(p)` and hence the diagonal order-`r^2 A^2` contribution to the precision is already `Theta(1)`. Therefore a fixed finite Taylor/walk truncation is not a uniform small-parameter proof at the desired scale. The correct limit requires a genuinely resummed nonlinear precision equation (or an arithmetic substitute), not another bounded-order moment calculation.

**B7 outcome:** the limit analysis does not prove Conjecture 27, but it rules out two tempting simplifications at the correct scale: uniform common-mode deflation and finite-order high-temperature expansion.
