## 1. Exact target

Let `G=(V,E)` be a finite simple graph and let `F` be uniform over **all** acyclic edge subsets of `E`, isolated vertices allowed.  For every two distinct edges `e,f`, prove or refute

\[
\Pr(e,f\in F)\le \Pr(e\in F)\Pr(f\in F).
\]

Equivalently, for the multivariate forest polynomial

\[
I_G(\mathbf y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E:\,A\text{ acyclic}}\mathbf y^A,
\]

the unit-weight Rayleigh difference

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}:=(I_G)_e(I_G)_f-I_G(I_G)_{ef}

should be nonnegative for every finite simple graph and every edge pair.

\[

## 5. NEW LEMMA L31 — possible fiber partitions at `|R|=4`; `2+1+1` is impossible

Because every root fiber is nonempty and the fibers partition `R`, the possible fiber-size partitions of four are

\[
4,\qquad 3+1,\qquad 2+2,\qquad 2+1+1,\qquad 1+1+1+1.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The partition `2+1+1` cannot occur.

### Proof

Assume a size-two fiber is

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+h\}.
\]

By (4.2), `h` has an endpoint outside the root cycle `C_P`.  Moreover `h` is the **only** edge of `H` having any endpoint outside `V(C_P)`: every second such edge `g` would give another one-edge extension `P+g\in R`, contradicting the fiber size two.

Consequently every endpoint of `h` outside `V(C_P)` has degree one in the full graph `G`: neither `e` nor `f` meets such a vertex, and no other H-edge meets it.  Hence `h` lies on no cycle.

Let `Q` be any other minimal R-root.  Its common cycle `C_Q=Q+e+f` cannot use `h`, since a simple cycle cannot pass through a degree-one outside endpoint.  Therefore `C_Q` is contained in `V(C_P)`, while `h` has an endpoint outside `V(C_Q)`.  Applying (4.2) to the root `Q` gives

\[
Q+h\in R.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Thus **every** other root has a nonminimal extension by the same edge `h`.  In particular, if there were two singleton root fibers `\{Q\}` and `\{S\}`, then `Q+h` and `S+h` would be two additional R-forests.  The total cardinality would be at least six, not four.

Therefore the partition `2+1+1` is impossible.  QED.

## 6. NEW LEMMA L32 — the `2+2` pattern is a common bridge factor

Suppose

\[
R=\{P,P+h,Q,Q+k\}
\]

with two root fibers of size two.  Then

\[
\boxed{h=k,}
\tag{6.1}
\]

this common edge is a bridge of `G`, and deleting it leaves exactly two R-forests.

### Proof

Apply the proof of L31 to the first fiber.  The edge `h` is the unique H-edge meeting a vertex outside `V(C_P)` and lies on no cycle.  Hence the other root cycle `C_Q` avoids `h`, so (4.2) gives

\[
Q+h\in R.
\]

The only strict member of `Q`'s size-two fiber is `Q+k`.  Therefore `Q+h=Q+k`, hence `h=k`.

Since an outside endpoint of `h` is incident to no other edge, `h` is a bridge (possibly the sole edge of an otherwise isolated component).  Every forest chooses `h` independently, so

\[
I_G=(1+y_h)I_{G-h},
\]

and for `e,f\ne h`

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_G\{e,f\}=(1+y_h)^2\Delta_{G-h}\{e,f\}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

The four R-forests occur in the two pairs `P,P+h` and `Q,Q+h`; after deleting `h`, exactly `P,Q` remain.  QED.

### Consequence for a minimal counterexample

If `G,e,f` were an edge-minimal unit-weight counterexample, then `G-h` has fewer edges and therefore

\[
\Delta_{G-h}\{e,f\}(\mathbf1)\ge0.
\]

Equation (6.2) gives

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}(\mathbf1)=4\Delta_{G-h}\{e,f\}(\mathbf1)\ge0,
\]

a contradiction.  Therefore an edge-minimal counterexample with `|R|=4` cannot have fiber pattern `2+2`.

## 7. NEW LEMMA L33 — exact structure of the `3+1` pattern

Suppose the two root fibers have sizes `3` and `1`:

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+h,P+k\},
\qquad
\mathcal F(Q)=\{Q\}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then:

1. `h,k` are exactly the two H-edges having an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`;
2. the singleton root cycle `C_Q` uses **both** `h` and `k`;
3. the two edges form a two-edge ear
   \[
   h=uw,\qquad k=wv,
   \tag{7.2}
   \]
   where `w\notin V(C_P)` and `u,v\in V(C_P)`;
4. suppressing the degree-two vertex `w` to a virtual edge `g=uv` produces a strictly smaller simple base graph whose R-family has size one or two.

### Proof

Because a three-element down-set above a root cannot contain a two-edge extension without also containing both one-edge subextensions, the only possible form of the fiber is exactly (7.1) with distinct one-edge extensions `h,k`.  By (4.2), both meet vertices outside `V(C_P)`.  Conversely every edge meeting the outside of `C_P` would give another one-edge extension, so `h,k` are the only such edges.

Consider the other common cycle `C_Q`.  A simple cycle using an outside vertex must enter and leave the outside subgraph.  Since `h,k` are the only edges meeting the outside of `C_P`, `C_Q` either uses both of them or neither.

It cannot use neither.  If it avoided both, its vertex set would lie in `V(C_P)`, while each of `h,k` has an endpoint outside `V(C_Q)`.  By (4.2), both `Q+h` and `Q+k` would belong to `R`, contradicting the singleton fiber `\{Q\}`.

Thus `C_Q` uses both.  With only two available outside edges, the outside part of a simple cycle must be a length-two path: the edges share one outside vertex `w`, and their other endpoints `u,v` lie on `C_P`.  This is (7.2).  The vertex `w` has degree exactly two in `G`.

Now suppress `w`.  There are two cases.

### Case A: `uv` is already an edge of `G`

Let `B` be obtained by deleting `w,h,k` and retaining the existing edge `g=uv`.  Every R-forest of `B` is also an R-forest of `G` containing neither `h` nor `k`.  Among the four forests in (7.1), only `P` has this property.  Hence

\[
|R(B)|=1.
\tag{7.3}
\]

The original graph `G` is obtained from `B` by replacing its edge `g` with the two-terminal network consisting of the direct edge `g` in parallel with the path `u-w-v`.

### Case B: `uv` is not an edge of `G`

Let `B` be obtained by deleting `w,h,k` and adding the new edge `g=uv`.  If an R-forest `A` of `B` does not contain `g`, then it is also an R-forest of `G` containing neither `h` nor `k`, hence `A=P`.

If `g\in A`, replace `g` by the path `h,k`:

\[
A'=(A-g)\cup\{h,k\}.
\]

Subdividing an edge preserves acyclicity and preserves whether adding `e`, `f`, or both creates a cycle.  Therefore `A'\in R(G)`.  It contains both `h,k`, and the only member of (7.1) with that property is the singleton root `Q`.  Hence

\[
A=(Q-\{h,k\})\cup\{g\}.
\]

Thus

\[
|R(B)|=2.
\tag{7.4}
\]

The two members are distinct minimal roots, hence incomparable.  The base graph `B` has one fewer edge than `G`.  QED.

## 10. NEW THEOREM L36 — structure of the four-minimal-root obstruction

Assume

\[
R=\{P_1,P_2,P_3,P_4\}
\]

and every member is inclusion-minimal.

Then:

1. the distinguished edges `e,f` are vertex-disjoint;
2. all four common cycles `P_i+e+f` have the same vertex support;
3. relative to any root `P`, every edge of another root not in `P` crosses the two path components of `P`;
4. exactly two roots realize each of the two terminal pairing types.

### Proof

Items 1–3 are the earlier common-support/no-chord arguments, which use only the hypothesis that **every** member of `R` is minimal, not the special cardinality three.

Indeed, if an H-edge had an endpoint outside the cycle support of a root `P`, equation (4.2) would give the nonminimal extension `P+h\in R`, contradiction.  Hence every other common cycle is supported inside `V(C_P)`; symmetry gives equal support for all roots.

Likewise, if an edge of another root had both endpoints on the same path side of `P=C_P\setminus\{e,f\}`, the tree-exchange argument from the 15:33 checkpoint produces an R-forest with a deletable pendant branch, hence a nonminimal R-forest, contradiction.  Thus all new edges cross the two path sides.

If `e,f` shared a vertex, each minimal root would be a single path between the two outer distinguished endpoints.  A second common cycle on the same support necessarily uses an edge whose endpoints lie on that path, exactly the forbidden same-side chord situation.  Therefore `e,f` are disjoint.

For disjoint distinguished edges there are exactly two possible cross-terminal pairings.  Among four roots, two roots `P,Q` share a pairing by pigeonhole.  By the common-support/cross-only properties just proved, the hypotheses of the previous boundary-inversion Lemma L28 apply.  That lemma produces **two distinct** R-forests of the opposite pairing.  Hence there are at least two roots of the opposite pairing.

The four total roots therefore split exactly

\[
\boxed{2+2}
\tag{10.1}
\]

between the two pairing types.  QED.

### Resulting exact remaining obstruction

If a minimal counterexample exists with `|R|=4`, it must now have all of the following simultaneously:

* `e,f` vertex-disjoint;
* four common-cycle roots, all minimal;
* common vertex support;
* exactly two roots of each terminal pairing;
* no same-side chord relative to any root;
* choosing the two same-pair roots, the two boundary inversion switches of L28 are exactly the two roots of the opposite pairing.

This is the first still-open stratum after the present run.

## 14. Next attack

The next exact obligation is now sharply isolated:

> **Four-root separator theorem.**  Let `R` consist of four minimal roots.  Prove that the root-union graph has a two-vertex separator `{s,t}` separating the distinguished edge `e` from `f`, with the four roots corresponding to the four combinations of two binary terminal linkages on the two sides.

There is strong existing structure to exploit.

Choose same-pair roots `P,Q`.  Relative to the two ordered path sides of `P`, every edge of `Q\setminus P` is a cross edge.  Every inversion pair produces an opposite-pair R-root.  Since there are exactly two opposite roots, there can be only two **distinct inversion-switch forests**.  The previous exact path-cover checks show that in every compatible instance through nine support vertices this forces each of the two Q-paths to cross between the P-sides exactly twice.  The shared P-subpaths between those crossings behave as the two central rails of a 2-separation.

A promising proof route is therefore:

1. prove combinatorially that a same-pair spanning two-path cover with more than two cross edges on either path necessarily yields at least three distinct inversion-switch forests;
2. conclude that each Q-path has exactly two cross edges;
3. use full vertex support to show the intervals between the boundary crossings partition the two P-sides, giving a pair of central rail segments;
4. choose one vertex on each central rail and prove that deleting those two vertices separates the `e` side from the `f` side;
5. invoke the exact cross-2-separation factorization
   \[
   \Delta_G\{e,f\}=\alpha_K(e;s,t)\alpha_L(f;s,t).
   \]
   In an edge-minimal counterexample, the two virtual-edge augmented side problems are strictly smaller; after handling a possible existing separator edge by the archived two-terminal replacement identity, their terminal responses should be nonnegative.

Completion of this separator theorem would plausibly eliminate the final `|R|=4` case and advance the minimal-counterexample lower bound to `|R|>=5`.
