## 1. Exact target and main result

Let `G=(V,E)` be a finite simple graph and let `F` be uniform over **all** acyclic edge subsets of `E`, including forests with isolated vertices.  For every distinct `e,f\in E`, the target is

\[
\Pr(e,f\in F)\le \Pr(e\in F)\Pr(f\in F).
\]

Equivalently, for the multivariate forest polynomial

\[
I_G(\mathbf y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E:\,A\text{ acyclic}}\mathbf y^A,
\]

the unit-weight Rayleigh difference

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}:=(I_G)_e(I_G)_f-I_G(I_G)_{ef}
\]

## 4. Root-fiber language

Fix `e,f` and put

\[
H=G\setminus\{e,f\}.
\]

The class `R` consists of H-forests `A` such that `A+e` and `A+f` are forests but `A+e+f` is cyclic.

For every `A\in R`, the unique cycle of `A+e+f` contains both distinguished edges.  Removing `e,f` from that cycle gives the unique root `P\subseteq A`.  The fiber of `P` is

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{A\in R:P\subseteq A\}.
\]

It is a finite down-set above `P`: if

\[
P\subseteq B\subseteq A\in\mathcal F(P),
\]

then `B\in\mathcal F(P)`.

A useful point used repeatedly below is that if an extension set is absent from the fiber of `P`, then it is absent from `R` altogether: any `R`-forest containing `P` has unique root `P`.

## 5. NEW LEMMA L68 — a bad pair of one-edge extensions is exactly a two-edge ear

### Statement

Let `P` be a root, and let `h,k` be two distinct edges such that

\[
P+h\in R,\qquad P+k\in R,
\]

but

\[
P+h+k\notin R.
\]

Then there is one vertex `w\notin V(C_P)` and distinct vertices `u,v\in V(C_P)` such that

\[
\boxed{h=uw,\qquad k=wv.}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Thus the two individually valid extensions fail jointly exactly by forming a two-edge ear whose endpoints return to the root cycle.

### Proof

By the one-edge criterion (3.1), each of `h,k` has an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`.

The root `P=C_P\setminus\{e,f\}` is the disjoint union of two simple path components.  With only two new edges, joint failure can occur in only two ways:

* `P+h+k` ceases to be a forest; or
* it remains a forest but joins the two path components of `P`, destroying separate addability of `e,f`.

If `h,k` do not share an outside endpoint, their union is merely two independent tree attachments, or a longer dangling tree attached to at most one old component; it can neither create a cycle nor join the two old path components.  Therefore they must share an outside vertex `w`.

For the joint extension to create a cycle or join the two old path components, the other endpoint of each new edge must lie on `C_P`.  Thus

\[
h=uw,\qquad k=wv
\]

with `u,v\in V(C_P)`.  Simplicity gives `u\ne v`.  QED.

This elementary ear lemma is the local mechanism behind all of the small-fiber classifications below.

\{P,P+a,P+b,P+c,P+a+b\}.
\tag{6.2}

## 9. NEW THEOREM L72 — incidence parity in an all-minimal four-root family

### Statement

Suppose `R` consists of exactly four minimal roots and hence satisfies the archived four-root structural hypotheses.  Then every graph edge belongs to an even number of the four roots:

\[
\boxed{
\#\{P\in R:g\in P\}\in\{0,2,4\}
\quad\text{for every edge }g.
}
\tag{9.1}
\]

In particular no edge can occur in exactly one or exactly three roots.

### Proof

The archived L38 theorem in

`proofnet24/HARD10-01/runs/2026-09-15/2026-09-15T17-15-24Z__report.md`

constructs a proper two-vertex separator `{s,t}` separating the distinguished edge `e` from `f`.  Write the two sides as `K,L`, with `e\in E(K)` and `f\in E(L)` and

\[
V(K)\cap V(L)=\{s,t\}.
\]

Every simple common cycle containing `e,f` decomposes uniquely as

\[
\text{an }s-t\text{ path in }K\text{ containing }e
\quad\cup\quad
\text{an }s-t\text{ path in }L\text{ containing }f.
\tag{9.2}
\]

Conversely, the union of any such two paths is a simple common `e,f` cycle, because the interiors of the two sides are disjoint.  Therefore the set of the four roots is the Cartesian product of the admissible `e`-side paths and the admissible `f`-side paths.

Let the numbers of such paths be `m,n`.  Then

\[
mn=4.
\tag{9.3}
\]

The separator proof is built from two distinct roots `P,Q` of the same terminal pairing.  On the `e` side, `P` reaches the separator straight along the two root paths while `Q` uses the first two cross edges; the two induced `s-t` paths through `e` are distinct and have opposite orientation through `e`.  Hence

\[
m\ge2.
\]

Because `P,Q` have the same **global** terminal pairing, changing the orientation on the `e` side forces the orientation on the `f` side to change as well.  Thus their `f`-side paths are also distinct and

\[
n\ge2.
\]

Equation (9.3) now gives

\[
\boxed{m=n=2.}
\tag{9.4}
\]

Take any edge `g` on the `K` side.  It lies in `0`, `1`, or `2` of the two admissible `K` paths.  Each chosen `K` path combines with **both** admissible `L` paths.  Thus `g` lies in `0`, `2`, or `4` of the four common cycles, equivalently the four roots.  The same argument applies on the `L` side.

An actual separator edge `st`, if present, lies in no common cycle containing both `e,f`: using `st` as one `s-t` arc would leave the other arc to contain both separated distinguished edges, which would have to cross the separator again.  Thus it has incidence zero.

This proves (9.1). QED.

### Interpretation

The four-root family is not just separated: its common cycles form a `2\times2` Cartesian rectangle of two local linkage choices.  Root-edge incidence therefore has an automatic mod-2 parity.

Suppose two singleton roots use pair `ab` and the third uses a pair involving `c`, say `ac`.  Delete the spoke `wc`.  The third singleton disappears, while the other two remain, and the large fiber becomes

\[
\{P,P+wa,P+wb\}.
\]

The resulting graph has exact fiber partition `3+1+1`, contradicting the archived universal L42 theorem.  The `bc` case is identical.

### Step 3: all three spoke pairs distinct

Assume the three singleton roots are

\[
Q_{ab},\qquad Q_{ac},\qquad Q_{bc}.
\]

Call a pair edge, such as `ab`, **direct** if it is an actual graph edge.

If `ab` is direct, suppressing the ear `a-w-b` in `Q_{ab}` gives an `R`-forest avoiding all three spokes.  The only such member is `P`.  Hence

\[
ab\in P,
\qquad
Q_{ab}=P-ab+\{wa,wb\}.
\tag{10.2}
\]

The same statement holds for any other direct pair edge.

There cannot be two direct pair edges.  If, for example, `ab,ac\in P`, they are the two consecutive root-cycle edges incident to `a`.  Replacing the cycle segment

\[
b-a-c
\]

by

\[
b-w-c
\]

creates a common `e,f` cycle using the pair `bc`.  If this is not `Q_{bc}`, it is an extra root.  If it is `Q_{bc}`, then `a` is absent from its common-cycle support, so the unused spoke `wa` is a one-edge extension of `Q_{bc}` by (3.1), contradicting that `Q_{bc}` is singleton.  Thus at most one direct pair edge exists.

Suppose exactly one direct edge exists, say `ab`, while `ac,bc` are absent.  Delete `w` and all three spokes and add the two new edges `ac,bc`, obtaining a simple graph `B`.

Any `R(B)` forest has one of four states on the two new edges:

* state `00` is exactly `P`;
* state `10` is exactly the suppression of `Q_{ac}`;
* state `01` is exactly the suppression of `Q_{bc}`;
* state `11` cannot occur: the two edges `ac,bc` form a tree connecting the three terminals `a,b,c`; replacing that tree by the three-spoke tree `wa,wb,wc` preserves the connectivity relation on all old vertices, and would produce an `R(G)` forest containing all three spokes, of which there is none.

Therefore `B` has exactly three `R`-forests, all three minimal and distinct.  This contradicts the universal theorem that a three-element `R` family has a unique minimal root.

Finally suppose **none** of `ab,ac,bc` exists.  Delete `w` and the three spokes and add the whole triangle

\[
ab,ac,bc,
\]

obtaining a simple graph `B`.

The only `R(B)` states on this triangle are:

\[
000,\quad100,\quad010,\quad001.
\]

The zero state is `P`, and the three one-edge states are the suppressed singleton roots.  A state containing two triangle edges is impossible by the same terminal-tree replacement argument: two triangle edges form a spanning tree on `a,b,c`, and replacing it by the three-spoke star would create a forbidden `R(G)` forest containing all three spokes.  The state `111` is cyclic.

Thus `B` has exactly four `R`-forests, all minimal.  But each newly added triangle edge belongs to **exactly one** of the four roots.  This contradicts the four-root incidence parity theorem L72.

Hence the all-distinct spoke-pair case is impossible.

### Step 4: all three singleton roots use the same spoke pair

Suppose all three singleton roots use `{wa,wb}`.

The direct edge `ab` cannot exist.  If it did, suppressing the common ear `a-w-b` in each singleton root would yield an `R(G)` forest avoiding all spokes, hence `P`.  Re-expanding would make all three singleton roots identical, contradiction.

Therefore `ab` is absent.  Delete `w` and all three spokes and add one new edge

\[
g=ab,
\]

obtaining a simple graph `B`.

Every `R(B)` forest avoiding `g` is `P`.  Every `R(B)` forest containing `g`, after subdividing `g` to the ear `a-w-b`, becomes one of the three singleton roots, and conversely each singleton suppresses to a distinct `R(B)` root.  Hence `B` has exactly four minimal roots.

The new edge `g` belongs to exactly **three** of them and not to `P`.  This contradicts L72, which allows only incidences `0,2,4`.

All three possible spoke-pair multiplicity patterns have now been eliminated.  Therefore `4+1+1+1` is impossible. QED.
