## 5. NEW LEMMA L37 — every inversion is elementary

Call two cross edges

\[
g=x_i y_j,
\qquad
h=x_k y_\ell
\]

an **inversion** relative to the path orders (4.4) when

\[
i<k,\qquad j>\ell.
\tag{5.1}
\]

### Statement

In the four-minimal-root configuration,

\[
\boxed{
 i<k,\ j>\ell
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 k=i+1,\ j=\ell+1.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Thus every inversion occupies the opposite corners of one elementary cell between consecutive vertices of `X` and consecutive vertices of `Y`.

### Proof

Given an inversion, form two paths

\[
A=X[x_0,x_i]\cup\{g\}\cup Y[y_j,y_q]
\]

and

\[
B=Y[y_0,y_\ell]\cup\{h\}\cup X[x_k,x_p].
\]

Because `i<k` and `\ell<j`, these paths are vertex-disjoint.  They connect `a` to `d` and `b` to `c`, respectively.  Hence

\[
W=A\cup B
\]

is a forest of the opposite terminal pairing.  Therefore `e` and `f` are each individually addable to `W`, while adding both closes the common cycle; so `W\in R`.

Moreover `W` is itself inclusion-minimal: it consists exactly of the two terminal paths, with no branch edge.  Since in the surviving `|R|=4` case **all** R-forests are minimal roots and the previous theorem says all roots have the same cycle-vertex support as `P`, `W` must contain every vertex of the two paths `X,Y`.

But `W` omits every strict intermediate vertex

\[
x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
\]

and

\[
y_{\ell+1},\ldots,y_{j-1}.
\]

Therefore both lists are empty, which is exactly

\[
k=i+1,\qquad j=\ell+1.
\]

QED.

### Additional consequence

Different inversion pairs give different minimal roots, because the resulting root contains the two chosen cross edges and no other cross edges in their place.  Since only two roots have the opposite terminal pairing, there are at most two inversion pairs globally.  The next theorem only needs the stronger elementary-inversion conclusion (5.2).

## 6. NEW THEOREM L38 — the four-root core has a proper two-vertex separator

### Statement

In the surviving all-minimal `|R|=4` configuration there exist two vertices

\[
s,t\notin\{a,b,c,d\}
\]

such that deleting `s,t` disconnects the edge `e=ab` from the edge `f=cd`.  More precisely, one component of `G-\{s,t\}` contains `a,b` and another contains `c,d`.

### Proof

Let the two components of the second same-pair root `Q` be

\[
A_Q:a\leadsto c,
\qquad
B_Q:b\leadsto d.
\]

By (4.5), every edge of `Q\setminus P` crosses between `X` and `Y`.

Both `A_Q` and `B_Q` must use at least one cross edge.  Indeed, if `A_Q` used none, then it would use only P-edges on `X`; since `X` is the unique `a-c` path in P, this would force `A_Q=X`.  Because `Q` has the same support as `P` and its two components are vertex-disjoint, `B_Q` would then use all of `Y` and no vertex of `X`, hence `B_Q=Y` and `Q=P`, contradiction.  The same argument applies symmetrically.

Traverse `A_Q` from `a`.  Before its first cross edge it follows the unique initial segment of `X`.  Write its first cross edge as

\[
h_A=x_i y_j.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Traverse `B_Q` from `b`; write its first cross edge as

\[
h_B=y_\ell x_k.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The two Q-components are vertex-disjoint.  The prefix of `A_Q` already contains `x_0,\ldots,x_i`; therefore the endpoint `x_k` of `h_B` cannot lie in that prefix, so

\[
k>i.
\tag{6.3}
\]

Similarly the prefix of `B_Q` contains `y_0,\ldots,y_\ell`, so the endpoint `y_j` of `h_A` cannot lie there, and

\[
j>\ell.
\tag{6.4}
\]

Thus `h_A,h_B` form an inversion.  Lemma L37 forces

\[
k=i+1,
\qquad
j=\ell+1.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Set

\[
\boxed{
s=x_{i+1},\qquad t=y_{\ell+1}.}
\tag{6.6}
\]

These are not distinguished-edge endpoints.  Certainly `s\ne a` and `t\ne b`.  Also `s\ne c`: the edge `h_B` belongs to the `b-d` component of `Q`, so its endpoint `s` cannot equal `c`, which lies on the disjoint `a-c` component.  Similarly `t\ne d`.  Hence

\[
s,t\notin\{a,b,c,d\}.
\tag{6.7}
\]

Define the two sides after removing `s,t`:

\[
L_0=\{x_0,\ldots,x_i\}\cup\{y_0,\ldots,y_\ell\},
\tag{6.8}
\]

\[
R_0=\{x_{i+2},\ldots,x_p\}\cup\{y_{\ell+2},\ldots,y_q\}.
\tag{6.9}
\]

The edge `e=ab` lies entirely on `L_0`, while `f=cd` lies entirely on `R_0`.

We claim no edge of `G-\{s,t\}` joins `L_0` to `R_0`.

P-edges cannot do so: along `X` and `Y`, the only boundary edges between the two portions are incident to `s` or `t`, which have been removed.  Therefore any hypothetical bypass edge belongs to `H\setminus P`, and by (4.5) it is a cross edge.

There are only two possible orientations.

* Suppose
  \[
  g=x_r y_v,
  \qquad r\le i,\quad v\ge \ell+2.
  \]
  Compare `g` with
  \[
  h_B=x_{i+1}y_\ell.
  \]
  They form an inversion, but the Y-indices differ by at least two.  This contradicts the elementary-inversion conclusion (5.2).

* Suppose
  \[
  g=x_u y_r,
  \qquad u\ge i+2,\quad r\le \ell.
  \]
  Compare `g` with
  \[
  h_A=x_i y_{\ell+1}.
  \]
  Again they form an inversion with an index gap at least two, contradicting (5.2).

These exhaust all possible cross edges from `L_0` to `R_0`.  Hence no such edge exists.

Finally, every nonisolated vertex relevant to a root lies on the common support `X\cup Y`; an edge incident to a vertex outside that support would create a nonminimal R-extension of a root, contradicting the all-minimal hypothesis from the previous checkpoint.  Thus outside vertices are irrelevant isolated vertices and cannot reconnect the sides.

Therefore `G-\{s,t\}` separates `a,b` from `c,d`.  QED.

## 7. NEW THEOREM L39 — exact elimination of `|R|=4` from an edge-minimal counterexample

### Statement

No edge-minimal counterexample to Grimmett Conjecture 4.2 can have

\[
|R|=4.
\]

Consequently every edge-minimal counterexample satisfies

\[
\boxed{|R|\ge5.}
\tag{7.1}
\]

### Proof

The previous checkpoint already eliminated every `|R|=4` root-fiber pattern containing a nonminimal R-forest.  Thus in an edge-minimal counterexample with `|R|=4`, the four roots satisfy the hypotheses of L38.

Let `s,t` be the separator from L38.  Let `K` be the side containing `e=ab`, together with the separator vertices, and `L` the side containing `f=cd`, together with the separator vertices.  Any actual edge `st`, if present, is assigned to one side, say `K`.  Then

\[
G=K\cup L,
\qquad
V(K)\cap V(L)=\{s,t\},
\qquad
E(K)\cap E(L)=\varnothing,
\tag{7.2}
\]

with `e\in E(K)` and `f\in E(L)`.

Use the exact archived cross-2-separation identity.  For a two-terminal graph `M` with terminals `s,t`, write

\[
D_M=\sum_{A\text{ forest}:s\not\leftrightarrow t}\mathbf y^A,
\qquad
C_M=\sum_{A\text{ forest}:s\leftrightarrow t}\mathbf y^A
\]

and

\[
\alpha_M(g;s,t)=D_M(C_M)_g-C_M(D_M)_g.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_G\{e,f\}=\alpha_K(e;s,t)\alpha_L(f;s,t).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

We show both factors are nonnegative at unit weights by edge-minimality.

If a side, say `K`, does **not** contain the edge `st`, add a fresh simple edge `g=st`.  The archived identity gives

\[
\alpha_K(e;s,t)=\Delta_{K+g}\{e,g\}.
\tag{7.5}
\]

The graph `K+g` has strictly fewer edges than `G`: the opposite side contains `f` and, because `s,t` are internal separator vertices distinct from `c,d`, the root paths from `s,t` to the `f` endpoints contribute at least two further edges.  Thus the opposite side contains at least three edges.  Hence

\[
|E(K+g)|<|E(G)|.
\]

Since `G` is edge-minimal among counterexamples, the unit-weight Rayleigh difference in (7.5) is nonnegative.

If `K` **does** contain the actual edge `g=st`, then its terminal split is

\[
D_K=D_{K-g},
\qquad
C_K=C_{K-g}+y_gD_{K-g}.
\]

A direct substitution in (7.3) shows

\[
\alpha_K(e;s,t)
=
D_{K-g}(C_{K-g})_e-C_{K-g}(D_{K-g})_e
=
\Delta_K\{e,g\}.
\tag{7.6}
\]

The side `K` itself is a strictly smaller simple graph than `G`, so edge-minimality again gives

\[
\alpha_K(e;s,t)\ge0.
\]

The same argument applies to `L`; at most one side contains the actual simple edge `st`, and the other can be augmented by a fresh `st` edge as in (7.5).  Therefore

\[
\alpha_K(e;s,t)\ge0,
\qquad
\alpha_L(f;s,t)\ge0.
\]

Equation (7.4) gives

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}(\mathbf1)\ge0,
\]

contradicting that `G,e,f` is a counterexample.

Thus an edge-minimal counterexample cannot have `|R|=4`.  Together with the earlier elimination of `|R|<=3`, this proves (7.1).  QED.

## 10. New structural observations toward `|R|=5`

The earlier one-edge-extension lemma immediately gives one rigorous simplification.

If an R-root has a fiber of size exactly two,

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+h\},
\]

then `h` is the unique edge meeting a vertex outside the root cycle and is a bridge.  Toggling a bridge preserves the R-condition, so the entire R-family is paired by inclusion/exclusion of `h`.  Therefore

\[
\boxed{
|R|\text{ odd }\Longrightarrow\text{ no root fiber has size }2.
}
\tag{10.1}
\]

This already rules out every `|R|=5` fiber partition containing a part `2`.

The exact finite data above point to `(4,1)` as the only surviving five-element partition.  A size-four down-set above a root can have only two abstract shapes:

* three distinct one-edge extensions and no higher extension (`3`-star);
* two one-edge extensions together with their two-edge union (Boolean square).

All exact `|R|=5` examples through seven vertices use the first shape.  Proving this universally, then classifying how the singleton root must pass through the three outside extension edges, is the next concrete attack.
