## 1. Exact target

Let `G=(V,E)` be any finite simple graph. Let `F` be uniformly distributed over **all** acyclic edge subsets of `E`, with isolated vertices allowed. For every pair of distinct edges `e,f`, prove or refute

\[
\Pr(e,f\in F)\le \Pr(e\in F)\Pr(f\in F).
\]

Equivalently, with the multivariate forest polynomial

\[
I_G(\mathbf y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E:\,A\text{ acyclic}}\mathbf y^A,
\]

the unit-weight Rayleigh difference

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}:=(I_G)_e(I_G)_f-I_G(I_G)_{ef}
\]

should be nonnegative for every finite simple graph and every distinct edge pair.

## 4. Reused R-root language

Fix distinct `e,f` and put

\[
H=G\setminus\{e,f\}.
\]

The class `R` consists of H-forests `A` such that `A+e` and `A+f` are forests but `A+e+f` is cyclic.

Every `A\in R` has a unique inclusion-minimal root. If the unique cycle of `A+e+f` is `C`, then the root is

\[
P=C\setminus\{e,f\}.
\]

Thus roots are in bijection with simple cycles of `G` containing both `e,f`.

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{A\in R:P\subseteq A\}

The previous one-edge extension lemma says that if

\[

## 5. NEW LEMMA L41 — cardinality-free all-minimal separator principle

### Statement

Assume **every** member of `R` is an inclusion-minimal root and `|R|>=3`. Then an edge-minimal unit-weight counterexample is impossible.

### Proof

The common-support/no-chord argument from the earlier `|R|=3,4` checkpoints does not depend on the number of roots:

1. all roots have the same cycle-vertex support, since if a root `P` used a vertex absent from another root `Q`, adding a `P`-edge incident to that missing vertex would create a nonminimal R-extension of `Q`;
2. relative to any root `P`, every edge belonging to another root but not `P` crosses the two path components of `P=C_P\setminus\{e,f\}`; a same-component chord can be switched against its fundamental path to create a nonminimal R-forest, contradicting the all-minimal hypothesis.

If `e,f` are adjacent, an R-root is a single path between the two outer endpoints, while the common vertex of `e,f` is isolated in the root. The cross-edge property therefore forbids a second root on the same support. Hence the all-minimal case with at least three roots necessarily has vertex-disjoint `e,f`.

Write

\[
e=ab,\qquad f=cd.
\]

There are only two possible root pairings,

\[
ac\mid bd
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
ad\mid bc.
\]

With at least three roots, two roots `P,Q` have the same pairing. Orient the two path components of `P` as

\[
X:a\to c,
\qquad
Y:b\to d.
\]

Every `Q\setminus P` edge crosses between `X` and `Y`. The earlier inversion-switching construction shows that any inversion pair

\[
x_i y_j,\qquad x_k y_\ell,
\qquad i<k,\ j>\ell,
\]

produces an R-root of the opposite pairing. Since all roots have the same support, that switched root cannot omit a strict interval of either `X` or `Y`. Therefore every inversion is elementary:

\[
k=i+1,\qquad j=\ell+1.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Now take the first cross edge on the `a-c` component of `Q`, say `x_i y_j`, and the first cross edge on its disjoint `b-d` component, say `y_\ell x_k`. Disjointness of the two Q-path prefixes forces

\[
k>i,\qquad j>\ell,
\]

so they form an inversion and (5.1) gives

\[
k=i+1,\qquad j=\ell+1.
\]

Set

\[
s=x_{i+1},\qquad t=y_{\ell+1}.
\]

Exactly as in the previous four-root separator proof, any edge bypassing `s,t` from the `e` side to the `f` side would form a non-elementary inversion with one of these first-cross edges. Hence `{s,t}` is a proper two-vertex separator with `e` and `f` on opposite sides.

The archived exact two-separation identity is

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}
=\alpha_K(e;s,t)\alpha_L(f;s,t),
\tag{5.2}
\]

where `K,L` are the two sides. Appending a virtual terminal edge when necessary identifies each `alpha` factor with the Rayleigh difference of a strictly smaller simple graph. Edge-minimality therefore makes both factors nonnegative, contradicting `\Delta_G<0`.

Thus an edge-minimal counterexample cannot have an all-minimal R-family of cardinality at least three. QED.

## 6. NEW LEMMA L42 — the fiber pattern `3+1+1` is impossible when `|R|=5`

### Statement

There is no graph/pair with `|R|=5` and root-fiber partition

\[
3+1+1.
\]

### Proof

Suppose

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+h,P+k\}
\]

and the other two fibers are singleton roots `Q,S`.

A three-element downward-closed fiber cannot contain a two-edge extension: if `P+h+k` were in the fiber, downward closure would force the four distinct members

\[
P,\ P+h,\ P+k,\ P+h+k.
\]

Hence the displayed form is exact. By (4.1), `h,k` are exactly the two H-edges having an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`.

A singleton root cannot avoid these outside edges. If, say, `C_Q` avoided `h`, then an outside endpoint of `h` would lie outside `V(C_Q)` and (4.1), now applied to `Q`, would give `Q+h\in R`, contradicting the singleton fiber. Therefore `C_Q` uses outside vertices, and because `h,k` are the only edges meeting the outside of `C_P`, the simple cycle `C_Q` must use both. The same holds for `C_S`.

Consequently

\[
h=uw,\qquad k=wv
\]

for one outside vertex `w` and two vertices `u,v\in V(C_P)`: both `Q` and `S` use the same two-edge ear `u-w-v`.

There are two cases.

### Case A: the edge `uv` is absent

Delete `w,h,k` and add a new edge `g=uv`, obtaining a smaller simple graph `B`.

If `A\in R(B)` and `g\notin A`, then `A` is also an R-forest of `G` containing neither `h` nor `k`; the only such member is `P`.

If `g\in A`, subdivide `g` by replacing it with `h,k`. Subdivision preserves acyclicity and whether `e`, `f`, or both close a cycle. Hence the subdivided set belongs to `R(G)` and contains both `h,k`; the only such members are `Q,S`.

Conversely, suppressing the ear in `Q,S` produces two distinct R-forests of `B` containing `g`, and `P` is an R-forest of `B` not containing `g`. Thus

\[
|R(B)|=3,
\]

and all three are minimal roots (otherwise subdivision would contradict minimality of `Q` or `S`). This contradicts the archived universal `|R|=3` theorem, which says every three-element R-family has a unique minimal root.

### Case B: the edge `uv` is already present

Delete `w,h,k` and retain the existing edge `g=uv`, obtaining `B`.

Every R-forest of `B` is also an R-forest of `G` avoiding `h,k`, so

\[
R(B)=\{P\}.
\]

Suppressing the ear in `Q` and in `S` gives R-forests of `B`, hence both suppressions equal `P`. Therefore `g\in P` and both original roots are obtained from the same set by the identical replacement

\[
g\mapsto \{h,k\}.
\]

Thus `Q=S`, contradiction.

Both cases are impossible. QED.

## 8. NEW LEMMA L44 — a size-four fiber cannot be a Boolean square in the `4+1` case

A four-element downward-closed fiber above a root has only two possible shapes:

1. a three-spoke star
   \[
   \{P,P+h_1,P+h_2,P+h_3\};
   \]
2. a Boolean square
   \[
   \{P,P+h,P+k,P+h+k\}.
   \]

### Statement

If `|R|=5` with partition `4+1`, the square shape is impossible. Hence the size-four fiber is necessarily the three-spoke star.

### Proof

Assume the square shape and let `Q` be the singleton root. By (4.1), `h,k` are exactly the two H-edges having an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`.

The root cycle `C_Q` cannot avoid the outside. Otherwise an outside endpoint of `h` is absent from `C_Q`, and (4.1) gives `Q+h\in R`, contradicting singleton `Q`.

Thus `C_Q` uses outside vertices. Since `h,k` are the only edges meeting the outside of `C_P`, a simple cycle can enter and leave the outside only through these two edges. Therefore

\[
h=uw,\qquad k=wv
\]

for an outside vertex `w` and `u,v\in V(C_P)`.

But then `P+h+k` cannot belong to `R`:

* if `u,v` lie in the same component of the forest `P=C_P\setminus\{e,f\}`, the path `u-w-v` plus the unique P-path from `u` to `v` creates a cycle, so `P+h+k` is not a forest;
* if `u,v` lie in different components of `P`, then adding the path `u-w-v` connects those two components. The endpoints of each distinguished edge lie one in each component, so at least one of `e,f` (indeed both in the common-cycle geometry) is no longer separately addable.

Either way `P+h+k\notin R`, contradicting the square assumption. QED.

## 9. NEW LEMMA L45 — two common cycles with no third differ by one path exchange

### Statement

Let `C,C'` be two distinct simple cycles containing both `e,f`. Assume there is no third simple cycle containing both. Then there are vertices `u,v` and internally vertex-disjoint `u-v` paths `A,B,R` such that

\[
C=A\cup R,
\qquad
C'=B\cup R,
\tag{9.1}
\]

and both distinguished edges lie on the common path `R`.

### Proof

Traverse `C` and `C'` and decompose their union into maximal divergence-reconvergence bubbles: at each bubble the cycles use two distinct internally vertex-disjoint paths with the same endpoints, while outside the bubbles they coincide.

The distinguished edges `e,f` belong to both cycles, so neither distinguished edge lies in the symmetric difference of the cycles.

If there were at least two bubbles, switch the choice of branch in exactly one bubble and retain the original branch choices elsewhere. The result is a simple cycle distinct from both `C,C'`; since the switch changes no common edge, it still contains both `e,f`. This contradicts the hypothesis.

Hence there is exactly one bubble. Calling its two alternative paths `A,B` and the common complementary path `R` gives (9.1). Since `e,f` are common to both cycles, they lie on `R`. QED.

## 10. NEW THEOREM L46 — exact three-spoke / topological-K4 geometry of the surviving `|R|=5` obstruction

### Statement

Assume `|R|=5` with root-fiber partition `4+1`. Let

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+h_1,P+h_2,P+h_3\},
\qquad
\mathcal F(Q)=\{Q\}.
\tag{10.1}
\]

Then there exist one outside vertex `w` and three distinct vertices `u,v,z\in V(C_P)` such that, after relabelling the extension edges,

\[
\boxed{
 h_1=uw,\qquad h_2=vw,\qquad h_3=zw.
}
\tag{10.2}
\]

Moreover:

1. `w` has degree exactly three in `G`; all other vertices outside `V(C_P)` are isolated;
2. the singleton root cycle `C_Q` uses exactly the two spokes `uw,vw` and does not use `zw`;
3. `C_P` splits into two `u-v` arcs `A,R` with
   \[
   C_P=A\cup R,
   \qquad
   C_Q=(u-w-v)\cup R;
   \tag{10.3}
   \]
4. the arc `A` contains neither `e` nor `f`, while `R` contains both;
5. the third attachment vertex `z` lies in the interior of `R` (allowing adjacency to `u` or `v` only in the edge sense, but `z\ne u,v`), and `z` separates `e` from `f` along `R`: one distinguished edge lies on the `u-z` subpath and the other lies on the `z-v` subpath.

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
C_P\cup\{uw,vw,zw\}
\text{ is a subdivision of }K_4
}
\tag{10.4}
\]

with branch vertices `u,v,z,w`; the distinguished edges lie on the two **adjacent rim branches** `uz` and `zv`, while the rim branch `uv` contains neither distinguished edge.

### Proof

By L44 the fiber has the star shape (10.1). By the one-edge extension criterion (4.1), `h_1,h_2,h_3` are exactly the H-edges meeting vertices outside `V(C_P)`.

There are exactly two roots, hence exactly two common `e,f` cycles: `C_P` and `C_Q`. Apply L45.

The cycle `C_Q` must use the outside. If it avoided all three extension edges, then any `h_i` would have an endpoint outside `V(C_Q)` and (4.1) would give the forbidden extension `Q+h_i\in R`.

The alternative bubble path of `C_Q` cannot use all three outside edges. With only three outside edges, a simple outside excursion using all three is a length-three path from the old cycle back to the old cycle. Its first two edges form a tree attached to `P` at a single cycle vertex, so adding those two edges to `P` preserves acyclicity and does not change the two terminal components. Therefore that two-edge extension would lie in `R`, contradicting the fact that the size-four fiber contains only one-edge extensions.

The alternative path also cannot use only one extension edge because every extension edge has an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`. Hence `C_Q` uses exactly two extension edges. They must form a two-edge path

\[
u-w-v
\]

with one internal outside vertex `w` and `u,v\in V(C_P)`.

Let `h_3` be the unused extension edge. Since `Q` is a singleton fiber, `Q+h_3\notin R`. By (4.1) applied to the root `Q`, the edge `h_3` therefore has no endpoint outside `V(C_Q)`. But `h_3` does have an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`, and the only outside vertex used by `C_Q` is `w`. Thus

\[
h_3=wz
\]

for some `z\in V(C_P)`. Simplicity of `G` gives `z\ne u,v`. Since these three spokes are all H-edges meeting the outside of `C_P`, the vertex `w` has degree exactly three and every other vertex outside `V(C_P)` is isolated.

Now apply the one-bubble conclusion L45. The two cycles have the form

\[
C_P=A\cup R,
\qquad
C_Q=(u-w-v)\cup R,
\]

where `A,R` are the two `u-v` arcs of `C_P`. Both `e,f` lie on the common arc `R`, so `A` contains neither.

The unused spoke `wz` has both endpoints on `C_Q`, and `z\ne w`; since `z\in V(C_P)` but the vertices internal to `A` are not on `C_Q`, it follows that `z\in R`.

Finally suppose both `e,f` lay on the same side of `z` along `R`, say on the `u-z` subpath. Then

\[
(u-w-z)\cup R[u,z]
\]

would be a third simple cycle containing both `e,f`, using the two spokes `uw,wz`. This contradicts that `P,Q` are the only roots. The same argument applies to the `z-v` side. Hence `e,f` lie on opposite sides of `z` along `R`.

The cycle `C_P` is the triangle rim subdivided into the three arcs `uv=A`, `vz`, `zu`; adjoining the three spokes from `w` gives a subdivision of `K_4`. The edge placement follows from the preceding paragraph. QED.

## 11. NEW exact three-terminal transfer identity for the final degree-three vertex

The structural theorem reduces the remaining `|R|=5` obstruction to adding one degree-three vertex `w` to a graph `B=G-w` having a **unique** common `e,f` cycle.

Let the neighbors of `w` be `u,v,z`. Classify each forest of `B` by the connectivity partition it induces on these terminals. Write

* `A_0`: all three terminals lie in distinct components;
* `A_{uv}`, `A_{uz}`, `A_{vz}`: exactly the indicated pair is connected and the third terminal is separate;
* `A_3`: all three terminals are connected.

Use the same symbols for their generating polynomials in the B-edge variables. Give the three spokes weights `x,y,zeta` respectively on `wu,wv,wz`.

For a fixed B-forest:

* if all terminals are separate, every spoke subset is allowed;
* if, say, `u,v` are already connected, a spoke subset is allowed exactly when it does not contain both `wu,wv`;
* if all three terminals are connected, at most one spoke may be selected.

Therefore the full forest polynomial is exactly

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
I_G={}&W_0A_0+W_{uv}A_{uv}+W_{uz}A_{uz}+W_{vz}A_{vz}+W_3A_3,\\
W_0={}&(1+x)(1+y)(1+\zeta),\\
W_{uv}={}&W_0-xy(1+\zeta),\\
W_{uz}={}&W_0-x\zeta(1+y),\\
W_{vz}={}&W_0-y\zeta(1+x),\\
W_3={}&1+x+y+\zeta.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{11.1}
\]

At the unit point `x=y=zeta=1`,

\[
\boxed{
I_G=8A_0+6(A_{uv}+A_{uz}+A_{vz})+4A_3.
}
\tag{11.2}
\]

This identity is exact and isolates the remaining algebraic difficulty in a five-state three-terminal transfer operator on a unique-common-cycle base.

A useful comparison is obtained by adding to `B` a triangle on `u,v,z` with each triangle-edge weight `1/4`. Its compatibility weights on the five terminal partitions are

\[
\frac{31}{16},\quad \frac32,\quad \frac32,\quad \frac32,\quad 1.
\]

Thus if `J` denotes that weighted-triangle forest polynomial,

\[
\boxed{
I_G=4J+\frac14 A_0
}
\tag{11.3}
\]

at unit spoke weights. The extra `A_0/4` term is exactly the obstruction to a naive star-triangle replacement by an ordinary weighted triangle.

Equation (11.3) is **not** by itself a Rayleigh proof, because a positive linear combination of Rayleigh polynomials need not preserve a Rayleigh difference. It does, however, identify the precise residual term to control.
