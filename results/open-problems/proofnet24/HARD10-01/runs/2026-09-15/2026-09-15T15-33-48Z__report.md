## 5. NEW LEMMA L22 — every `R` forest has a unique minimal root, and each root fiber is downward closed

### Statement

For every `A\in R`, there is a unique inclusion-minimal `R` forest `P\subseteq A`.  If `P\subseteq B\subseteq A`, then `B\in R` and has the same minimal root `P`.

Consequently `R` is the disjoint union of root fibers

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{A\in R:P\subseteq A\},
\]

and after deleting the root `P` each fiber is a finite down-set in the Boolean lattice of extra edges.

### Proof

Because `A` is a forest and `A+e` is a forest, adding `f` to `A+e` creates exactly one cycle `C_A`.  Since `A+f` is also a forest, that unique cycle contains **both** `e` and `f`.  Put

\[
P=C_A\setminus\{e,f\}.
\]

Then `P\subseteq A`, `P+e` and `P+f` are forests, while `P+e+f=C_A` is cyclic.  Thus `P\in R`.  By the common-cycle/minimal-witness lemma in the 14:44 report, `P` is inclusion-minimal.

Uniqueness follows because `A+e+f` has exactly one cycle.  Any minimal `R` subset of `A` would, after adding `e,f`, yield a simple cycle contained in `A+e+f`; it must therefore be `C_A` and hence the same `P`.

Now let `P\subseteq B\subseteq A`.  Then

* `B+e\subseteq A+e`, so `B+e` is a forest;
* `B+f\subseteq A+f`, so `B+f` is a forest;
* `B+e+f` contains `P+e+f=C_A`, so it is cyclic.

Thus `B\in R`.  Its unique minimal root is necessarily `P`.  QED.

### Immediate cardinality consequence

If `|R|=3` and there are at least two minimal roots, the only possible root-fiber size patterns are

\[
2+1
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
1+1+1.
\tag{5.1}
\]

In a size-two fiber the unique nonminimal member must be exactly `P+h` for a single edge `h`: if it differed from `P` by two or more edges, L22 would put at least two distinct one-edge extensions into the same fiber.

The rest of the proof rules out both patterns in (5.1).

## 6. NEW LEMMA L23 — a one-edge root extension must leave the root cycle

Let `P` be a minimal `R` witness and let

\[
C=P+e+f
\]

be its corresponding simple common cycle.  If `P+h\in R`, then `h` has at least one endpoint outside `V(C)`.

Conversely, **every** edge `h\in E(H)` with an endpoint outside `V(C)` satisfies `P+h\in R`.

### Proof

First suppose both endpoints of `h` lie on `C`.

Removing `e,f` from the simple cycle `C` leaves either:

* two path components, when `e,f` are vertex-disjoint or merely nonadjacent on `C`; or
* one path plus the common endpoint of `e,f` as an isolated cycle vertex, when `e,f` are adjacent.

If the endpoints of `h` lie on the same path component, then `P+h` contains a cycle because `h` is a chord of a tree path.  Since `G` is simple, `h` cannot be a second copy of an existing path edge.

If the endpoints lie in the two distinct path components, `h` merges the two terminal components, so at least one of `e,f` is no longer individually addable; hence `P+h\notin R`.

In the adjacent case, if `h` joins the isolated common endpoint of `e,f` to the path, then that common endpoint becomes connected to the two outer endpoints through the path, so `e` and `f` cease to be individually addable.  Again `P+h\notin R`.

Therefore `P+h\in R` forces an endpoint of `h` outside `V(C)`.

Conversely, if `h` has an endpoint outside `V(C)`, that endpoint is isolated in `P`.  Hence `P+h`, `P+h+e`, and `P+h+f` are all forests: `h` only attaches a previously isolated vertex/component to the cycle forest, or joins two isolated vertices.  But `P+h+e+f` still contains the cycle `C`.  Thus `P+h\in R`.  QED.

This simple observation is the key to excluding the `2+1` fiber pattern.

## 7. NEW THEOREM L24 — the `2+1` root-fiber pattern is impossible

Assume `|R|=3` and there are exactly two minimal roots `P,Q`.  By L22 the only possible fiber sizes are `2+1`; without loss of generality

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+h\},
\qquad
\mathcal F(Q)=\{Q\}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

This configuration cannot occur.

### Proof

Let `C=P+e+f`.  By L23, `h` has an endpoint outside `V(C)`.

Because `P+h` is the **only** strict member of the `P` fiber, L23 also implies:

\[
\boxed{
 h\text{ is the only edge of }H\text{ having any endpoint outside }V(C).
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Indeed every such edge would itself give a distinct one-edge `R` extension of `P`.

The other minimal root `Q` corresponds to a simple common cycle

\[
C_Q=Q+e+f.
\]

That cycle cannot use an edge incident to a vertex outside `V(C)`.  If it contained such an outside vertex, a simple cycle would need **two** incident cycle edges there, while (7.2) provides only the single edge `h`.  Therefore

\[
V(C_Q)\subseteq V(C),
\qquad h\notin Q.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Now add `h` to `Q`.  One endpoint of `h` is outside `V(C)` and hence outside `V(C_Q)`.  Exactly as in the converse direction of L23,

* `Q+h`, `Q+h+e`, and `Q+h+f` are forests;
* `Q+h+e+f` still contains the common cycle `C_Q`.

Thus

\[
Q+h\in R.
\]

It is a strict extension of `Q`, so it is distinct from `P`, `P+h`, and `Q`.  This is a fourth member of `R`, contradicting `|R|=3`.

Therefore the `2+1` pattern is impossible.  QED.

### Lemma L26 — no extra edge may be a chord inside one side of a root

\[
h=uv\in E(H)\setminus P

Because `h\notin P` and the graph is simple, the `P` subpath

u=p_0,p_1,\ldots,p_m=v

\[
g=p_{j-1}p_j.

Then

\[
T=P+h-g

Since `m\ge2` and only one old subpath edge was removed, at least one old `u-v` edge survives as a pendant branch off that terminal path.  Deleting that branch edge leaves the terminal pairing unchanged.  Thus `T` strictly contains its minimal `R` witness.

## 10. Boundary-inversion lemma for two same-pair roots

Write

\[
e=ab,\qquad f=cd.
\]

Fix a minimal root `P` realizing pairing

\[
ac\mid bd.
\]

Let its two path components be oriented

\[
X:a\to c,
\qquad
Y:b\to d.
\]

Let `Q` be a second minimal root with the **same** pairing `ac|bd`.  Assume:

1. `P,Q` have the same vertex support `W`;
2. every edge of `Q\setminus P` crosses between `X` and `Y`.

Condition 1 is supplied by L25 and condition 2 by L26 in the `1+1+1` case.

The inversion-switching lemma from the 14:37 report says that if two cross edges

\[
h_1=x_1y_1,\qquad h_2=x_2y_2
\]

satisfy

\[
x_1<x_2\text{ along }X,
\qquad
y_2<y_1\text{ along }Y,
\tag{10.1}
\]

then the two paths

\[
X[a,x_1]+h_1+Y[y_1,d]
\]

and

\[
Y[b,y_2]+h_2+X[x_2,c]
\]

form an `R` forest of the opposite pairing `ad|bc`.

The new point is that **two distinct inversion pairs are forced**.

### Lemma L28 — two boundary inversion pairs

Under the assumptions above, if `Q\ne P`, there exist four distinct cross edges

\[
h_a,h_c\subset Q\text{'s }a-c\text{ path},
\qquad
h_b,h_d\subset Q\text{'s }b-d\text{ path},
\]

such that

\[
(h_a,h_b)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(h_d,h_c)
\]

are two distinct inversion pairs relative to the orders on `X,Y`.

### Proof

Let `A` be the `Q` path from `a` to `c` and `B` the `Q` path from `b` to `d`.

First, both `A` and `B` meet **both** `X` and `Y`.  Suppose, for example, that `A` uses no vertex of `Y`.  Then `A` is an `a-c` path entirely inside `X`.  Every `Q` edge with both endpoints in `X` belongs to `P`, because all non-`P` edges of `Q` are cross edges.  Since `X` itself is a simple path, the only `a-c` path using its edges is all of `X`.  Hence `A=X`.  Because `Q` has the same support and its two components are vertex-disjoint, `B` then uses all vertices of `Y` and no vertex of `X`; the same argument gives `B=Y`.  Thus `Q=P`, contradiction.  Therefore both `Q` components cross between `X,Y`, and because each starts and ends on its original side, each contains at least two cross edges.

Traverse `A` from `a` to `c`.  Let

* `h_a=x_a y_a` be its **first** cross edge; it leaves the initial `X` run and enters `Y`;
* `h_c=x_c y_c` be its **last** cross edge; it leaves `Y` and enters the terminal `X` run.

Traverse `B` from `b` to `d`.  Let

* `h_b=x_b y_b` be its first cross edge; it leaves the initial `Y` run and enters `X`;
* `h_d=x_d y_d` be its last cross edge; it leaves `X` and enters the terminal `Y` run.

Before `h_a`, the path `A` follows a prefix of `X` using `P` edges.  Therefore every vertex of `X` up to and including `x_a` lies in the `Q` component `A`.  The vertex `x_b` lies in the disjoint component `B`, so it occurs strictly later on `X`:

\[
x_a<x_b.
\tag{10.2}
\]

Similarly, before `h_b`, the path `B` follows a prefix of `Y`, so all vertices through `y_b` lie in `B`; the vertex `y_a\in A` must occur strictly later:

\[
y_b<y_a.
\tag{10.3}
\]

Thus `(h_a,h_b)` is an inversion pair.

At the terminal end, after `h_c` the path `A` follows the final suffix of `X` to `c`, so every vertex from `x_c` to `c` lies in `A`.  The endpoint `x_d\in B` must therefore occur strictly before `x_c`:

\[
x_d<x_c.
\tag{10.4}
\]

Likewise after `h_d`, the path `B` follows the final suffix of `Y` to `d`, so the endpoint `y_c\in A` must occur strictly before `y_d`:

\[
y_c<y_d.
\tag{10.5}
\]

Equations (10.4)–(10.5) say `(h_d,h_c)` is a second inversion pair.

The four edges are distinct: `A` has at least two cross edges, so `h_a\ne h_c`; `B` has at least two, so `h_b\ne h_d`; and edges of the two disjoint `Q` components cannot coincide.  QED.

### Corollary L28.1 — two same-pair roots force at least four `R` forests

Apply the inversion-switching construction to `(h_a,h_b)` and `(h_d,h_c)`.  It gives two `R` forests `F_1,F_2` of the opposite pairing.

They are distinct because their selected cross-edge pairs are disjoint.  They are different from `P` because each contains cross edges not in `P`.  They are also different from `Q`: each of `A,B` has at least two cross edges, so `Q` contains at least four cross edges, while each switched forest contains exactly the two selected cross edges and otherwise only `P` edges.

Hence

\[
\boxed{
P,Q,F_1,F_2
\text{ are four distinct members of }R.
}
\tag{10.6}
\]

## 12. NEW MAIN THEOREM L30 — every `|R|=3` family has a unique minimal witness

### Statement

\[
