## 1. Target and new result

Let `G=(V,E)` be a finite simple graph and let `F` be uniform over **all** acyclic edge subsets of `E`, including forests with isolated vertices.  For distinct edges `e,f`, write

\[
I_G(\mathbf y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E:\,A\text{ acyclic}}\mathbf y^A,
\qquad
\Delta_G\{e,f\}=(I_G)_e(I_G)_f-I_G(I_G)_{ef}.
\]

At unit weights Grimmett Conjecture 4.2 is `Delta_G{e,f}(1)>=0`, equivalently

\[
\Pr(e,f\in F)\le \Pr(e\in F)\Pr(f\in F).
\]

## 4. New theorem: `5+4` is excluded from an edge-minimal counterexample

Assume for contradiction that an edge-minimal unit-weight counterexample has exact root-fiber partition

\[
5+4.
\]

Let `P` be the size-five root and `Q` the size-four root.

The size-five classification gives a four-spoke star

\[
\mathcal F(P)=
\{P,P+wu,P+wv,P+wa,P+wb\},
\tag{4.1}
\]

where `w` is outside `C_P` and `u,v,a,b` are four distinct vertices of `C_P`.

In an edge-minimal counterexample the size-four fiber cannot be a Boolean square, because its atom edges are graph bridges and the bridge factorization reduces the Rayleigh difference to a strictly smaller graph.  Hence the size-four fiber is a three-spoke star with center `x`.

### 4.1 Mutual traversal of the two star centers

The cycle `C_Q` cannot avoid `w`: if it did, all four edges in (4.1) would be one-edge extensions of `Q`, but the `Q`-fiber has only three atoms.  Therefore `C_Q` contains `w` and, being simple, uses exactly two `w`-spokes.  Relabel so they are

\[
wu,\quad wv,
\]

while `wa,wb` are unused by `Q`.

The two star centers are distinct.  Indeed the center of the `Q`-star lies outside `C_Q`, whereas `w\in V(C_Q)`.

Similarly `C_P` cannot avoid the center `x` of the `Q`-star.  If it did, all three `x`-spokes would be atoms of the `P`-fiber.  But every atom of the size-five fiber has the common outside endpoint `w`, and `x\ne w`; in a simple graph at most the single edge `wx` can belong to both stars.  Hence `C_P` contains `x` and uses exactly two `x`-spokes.  Write those two as

\[
xr,\quad xs,
\]

and the unique unused `Q`-spoke as `xc`.

Thus, after deleting the three unused spokes `wa,wb,xc`, the exact surviving partition is `3+3`, with two cross ears centered at `w` and `x`.

### 4.2 The center connector `wx` is impossible

Suppose `wx` were an edge.  Since `w` lies outside `C_P` and `x` lies outside `C_Q`, while each opposite root cycle contains the other center, `wx` is an unused atom in both star fibers.

Delete the other unused spokes.  We obtain the corrected cross-ear `3+3` geometry plus the connector between the two ear centers.

If the two ears have the same endpoint pair `{s,t}`, the connector creates the two mixed `s-t` paths

\[
s-w-x-t,\qquad s-x-w-t.
\]

Adjoining either appropriate mixed path to the common complementary `s-t` path creates an additional simple cycle containing `e,f`.

If the endpoint pairs are distinct, suppress both ears to the exact-two decorated-`K_4` graph.  The connector joins the two subdivision vertices of two literal exchange edges; the same four-cell construction used in the archived corrected cross-ear proof produces an additional common `e,f` cycle.  Re-expanding the exchange edges preserves that cycle.

Either way `wx` creates an additional root, contradicting the fact that the full `5+4` family has only the two roots `P,Q`.  Hence

\[
\boxed{wx\notin E(G).}
\tag{4.2}
\]

### 4.3 The three unused attachments lie on the opposite root supports

Because `wx` is absent, `wa` and `wb` are not `x`-star atoms.  If, say, `a\notin V(C_Q)`, then `wa` has an endpoint outside `C_Q` and the one-edge extension criterion would make `wa` an additional atom of the size-four `Q`-fiber.  Therefore

\[
a,b\in V(C_Q).
\tag{4.3}
\]

Symmetrically,

\[
c\in V(C_P).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Delete `wa,wb,xc`.  We have an exact `3+3` cross-ear graph.

### 4.4 Distinct ear endpoint pairs are impossible by L52

Suppose the two reduced ears have different endpoint pairs.  The corrected cross-ear theorem suppresses them to an exact-two decorated `K_4` whose two suppressed ear edges are literal exchange edges.

The vertex `a` lies on the common support of the compressed roots by (4.3).  Re-expand the exchange edge corresponding to the `w`-ear and add the third spoke `wa`.  The archived third-spoke lemma L52 gives a third simple cycle containing both distinguished edges.  If the other exchange edge lies on that cycle, re-expand it to the `x`-ear; path subdivision preserves the common cycle.  Thus the full graph already has a third root, contradiction.

Therefore the reduced cross ears must have the **same** endpoint pair.  Relabel that pair `{u,v}`.  The corrected same-endpoint cross-ear theorem then gives a common simple `u-v` path `R`, containing both distinguished edges, such that

\[
C_P=R\cup(u-x-v),
\qquad
C_Q=R\cup(u-w-v).
\tag{4.5}
\]

By (4.2)–(4.4), the unused attachments satisfy

\[
a,b,c\in V(R),
\qquad a\ne b.
\tag{4.6}
\]

### 4.5 Every unused attachment separates `e` from `f` on `R`

Take `a`.  If one of the two subpaths `R[u,a]` or `R[a,v]` contained both distinguished edges, adjoining that subpath to the two-spoke path through `w` would form a new simple common `e,f` cycle distinct from `C_Q`.  Therefore `a` lies between `e` and `f` along `R`.  The same argument applies to `b` and, using center `x`, to `c`.

So all three vertices `a,b,c` lie in the interval of `R` separating the two distinguished edges.

Since `a\ne b`, at least one of `a,b` is different from `c`.  Choose such a vertex and call it `y`.  Orient `R` from `u` to `v` so that `e` occurs before `f`.

If `y` occurs before `c`, then

\[
R[u,y]\cup yw\cup wv\cup R[v,c]\cup cx\cup xu
\tag{4.7}
\]

is a simple cycle: the two `R`-segments are internally disjoint because `y<c`.  The first segment contains `e`, the second contains `f`, so (4.7) contains both distinguished edges.

If `c` occurs before `y`, then symmetrically

\[
R[u,c]\cup cx\cup xv\cup R[v,y]\cup yw\cup wu
\tag{4.8}
\]

is a simple cycle containing both `e,f`.

In either order a third common cycle exists.  This contradicts the partition `5+4`.

Hence

\[
\boxed{\text{No edge-minimal counterexample has root-fiber partition }5+4.}
\tag{4.9}
\]

The only use of edge minimality in this theorem is the initial exclusion of the Boolean-square size-four fiber through its bridge atom.

## 5. New theorem: `3+3+3` is structurally impossible

Assume there are three size-three roots `P_1,P_2,P_3`.  Let `E_i` be the two-edge ear of `P_i`.

For `i\ne j`, the simple cycle `C_{P_j}` either avoids the degree-two center of `E_i`, in which case both edges of `E_i` are precisely the two atoms of the `P_j`-fiber and therefore

\[
E_i=E_j,
\tag{5.1}
\]

or `C_{P_j}` traverses `E_i` in full.  Thus the three ears fall into exactly one of three equality patterns.

### 5.1 All three ears are equal

If

\[
E_1=E_2=E_3=:E,
\]

delete the two edges of `E`.  Exactly the three roots `P_1,P_2,P_3` remain in `R`; every non-root fiber member used one half of `E`.  Hence the deleted graph has three distinct minimal `R`-roots.

This contradicts the archived universal `|R|=3` theorem, which says a three-member `R`-family has a unique minimal root.

### 5.2 Exactly two ears are equal

Suppose

\[
E_1=E_2=:E,
\qquad E_3=:F\ne E.
\]

Then `P_1,P_2` traverse `F` in full while `P_3` traverses `E` in full.

If `E` and `F` have the same endpoint pair, replacing the full `F` path in `C_{P_1}` by the full `E` path produces another simple common cycle.  Its root uses `E` in full and avoids `F`.  Among the nine `R`-members, the only member with that `0/full` ear pattern is `P_3`.  Thus this swap sends `P_1` to `P_3`.  The same swap sends `P_2` to `P_3`.  But replacing one internally disjoint two-edge path by the other is injective, so `P_1=P_2`, contradiction.

Hence the endpoint pairs of `E,F` are distinct.

Let `g_E` be the direct edge joining the endpoints of `E`.  If `g_E` existed, suppress `E` inside `P_3`.  Suppression preserves the `R` tests.  The result avoids `E` and also still avoids `F`, because `P_3` is the root of the `F`-fiber.  But in the original nine-member family every member avoiding `E` belongs to one of the first two fibers, whose roots `P_1,P_2` both traverse `F` in full; their non-root extensions also traverse `F`.  Thus there is no `R`-member avoiding both ears.  Contradiction.  Therefore `g_E` is absent.  The same argument with `E,F` interchanged shows that `g_F` is absent.

Suppress both ears to two fresh distinct direct edges.  The only original `R`-members using each ear in `0/full` fashion are the three roots.  They compress to three distinct minimal roots with direct-edge states

\[
(0,1),\quad(0,1),\quad(1,0),
\]

where the first two remain distinct because the same suppression was applied to both.  This again contradicts the universal three-member unique-root theorem.

### 5.3 All three ears are distinct

Each root traverses the other two ears in full.  No two distinct ears can have the same endpoint pair, because the third root would then contain two internally disjoint two-edge paths with the same endpoints, which already form a cycle.

Let `g_i` be the direct endpoint edge for `E_i`.  If `g_i` existed, suppress `E_i` inside `P_j`, `j\ne i`.  The result avoids `E_i` and still avoids `E_j`, whereas the only root avoiding `E_i` is `P_i`, which traverses `E_j` in full.  Thus the suppressed forest cannot equal any member of `R`, contradiction.  Hence all three `g_i` are absent.

Suppress all three ears simultaneously to three fresh, pairwise distinct direct edges.  Exactly the three roots have `0/full` usage on every ear; all six non-root fiber members use one half of their own ear.  The compressed graph therefore has exactly three distinct minimal roots, with states

\[
(0,1,1),\qquad(1,0,1),\qquad(1,1,0).
\]

Again this contradicts the universal `|R|=3` unique-root theorem.

All equality patterns are impossible.  Therefore

\[
\boxed{\text{There is no graph/pair with root-fiber partition }3+3+3.}
\tag{5.2}
\]

This theorem is structural and does not use edge minimality.

## 6. New theorem: `3+3+1+1+1` is structurally impossible

Let `P,Q` be the two size-three roots and let `S_1,S_2,S_3` be the three singleton roots.  Write their ears as `E_P,E_Q`.

As in the corrected `3+3` theorem, the two size-three roots are either common-ear or cross-ear.

### 6.1 Common-ear case

Suppose

\[
E_P=E_Q=:E=u-w-v.
\]

Every singleton root must traverse `E` in full.  A singleton cannot avoid the degree-two center `w`, since either ear edge would then be a one-edge extension; and a simple cycle through `w` must use both incident ear edges.

If the direct edge `g=uv` existed, suppress `E` in each singleton.  Each suppressed forest belongs to `R(G)` and avoids both ear edges.  The only `R`-members avoiding both edges are the two roots `P,Q`.  Suppression on full-ear forests is injective, because expansion is the inverse operation.  Three distinct singleton roots cannot inject into the two-element set `{P,Q}`.  Therefore

\[
uv\notin E(G).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Delete the ear and add a fresh edge `g=uv`, obtaining `B`.  Subdivision gives an exact bijection between `R(B)` and the original `R(G)` members that use the ear in either zero or two edges.  These are precisely

\[
P,Q,S_1,S_2,S_3.
\]

All five are distinct minimal roots after compression.  Thus `B` is an all-minimal five-root graph.

The structural part of the archived all-minimal separator theorem applies to any all-minimal `R`-family with at least three roots: the common cycles factor across a proper two-separator as a Cartesian product of an `e`-side path family and an `f`-side path family.  Consequently the number of minimal roots is `mn` with `m,n>=2`.  The prime number `5` cannot occur.  Contradiction.

### 6.2 Cross-ear case

Suppose `P` traverses `E_Q` in full and `Q` traverses `E_P` in full.  Every singleton root must traverse **both** ears in full.

The two ears cannot have the same endpoint pair: a singleton containing both complete internally disjoint length-two paths would already contain a cycle.  Thus their endpoint pairs are distinct.

Neither direct endpoint edge can exist.  For example, if the direct edge for `E_P` existed, suppress `E_P` in `Q` and in each of the three singleton roots.  All four suppressed forests avoid `E_P`, while the only original `R`-member avoiding both edges of `E_P` is the root `P`.  Suppression is injective on full-ear forests, so this is impossible.  The same holds for `E_Q`.

Suppress both ears to two fresh direct edges `g_P,g_Q`.  The original roots and singletons are exactly the members with `0/full` use of both ears, so the compressed graph has exactly five minimal roots, with direct-edge states

\[
P:(0,1),\qquad Q:(1,0),\qquad S_i:(1,1)\quad(i=1,2,3).
\]

Again an all-minimal five-root family is impossible by the Cartesian-product factorization.

Therefore both cases are impossible:

\[
\boxed{\text{There is no graph/pair with partition }3+3+1+1+1.}
\tag{6.2}
\]

This theorem is structural and does not use edge minimality.
