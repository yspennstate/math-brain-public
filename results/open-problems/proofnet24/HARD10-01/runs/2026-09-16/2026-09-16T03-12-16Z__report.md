## 4. Elimination of `4+1+1+1+1`

Assume an edge-minimal unit-weight counterexample has partition

\[
4+1+1+1+1.
\]

A size-four down-set is either a Boolean square or a three-atom star. The archived Boolean-square lemma makes both atom edges graph bridges. A nondistinguished bridge `b` gives

\[
I_G=(1+y_b)I_{G-b},
\qquad
\Delta_G\{e,f\}=(1+y_b)^2\Delta_{G-b}\{e,f\}.
\]

Hence a Boolean-square fiber cannot occur in an edge-minimal counterexample.

We are left with a three-spoke star

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+wa,P+wb,P+wc\},
\]

with `w` outside the principal root cycle and `a,b,c` on it.

Every singleton root must pass through `w`; otherwise each spoke would extend it. A simple cycle through `w` uses exactly two incident spokes. Therefore each of the four singleton roots is labelled by one of the three spoke pairs

\[
ab,\quad ac,\quad bc.
\]

By pigeonhole, some pair is used by `m>=2` singleton roots. Say the duplicated pair is `ab`.

### 4.1 If the direct edge `ab` exists, duplicates collapse

Replace the ear `a-w-b` in any singleton root using that pair by the direct edge `ab`. The resulting forest is still in `R` and avoids all three spokes. The only `R`-forest avoiding all three spokes is the root `P`: the three non-root members of the large fiber use one spoke each, and every singleton uses two.

Thus every singleton using `ab` is forced to equal

\[
P-ab+\{wa,wb\}.
\]

There can be at most one such singleton, contradicting `m>=2`.

### 4.2 If `ab` is absent, compress the duplicated ear

Delete all three spokes and insert a new edge

\[
g=ab.
\]

Call the compressed graph `B`.

Any `R(B)` forest avoiding `g` is an `R(G)` forest avoiding all spokes, hence is exactly `P`.

Any `R(B)` forest containing `g` expands `g` to `a-w-b`; the expanded forest is in `R(G)` and uses exactly the spoke pair `ab`, hence is one of the `m` duplicated singleton roots. Conversely each duplicated singleton suppresses to an `R(B)` forest containing `g`.

Therefore `B` has exactly `m+1` `R`-forests, all minimal: one root `P` avoiding `g` and `m` roots containing `g`.

The all-minimal product theorem now gives a contradiction in every possible multiplicity:

* `m=2`: `|R(B)|=3`, but an all-minimal family must factor `rs` with `r,s>=2`, impossible;
* `m=3`: `|R(B)|=4=2x2`, so every edge has even root incidence, but `g` occurs in exactly three roots;
* `m=4`: `|R(B)|=5`, again impossible to factor with both factors at least two.

Hence no pair can be duplicated. But four singleton roots choose among only three spoke pairs. Contradiction.

Thus

\[
\boxed{
4+1+1+1+1\text{ cannot occur in an edge-minimal counterexample.}
}
\]

## 5. Elimination of `6+1+1`

Assume an edge-minimal counterexample has partition

\[
6+1+1.
\]

### 5.1 Classification of six-element down-sets

Let `m` be the number of one-edge atoms above the size-six root `P`.

Because the fiber is a six-element down-set, the only abstract possibilities are:

1. `m=5`: root plus five singleton extensions;
2. `m=4`: root plus four singleton extensions and exactly one double extension;
3. `m=3`: root plus three singleton extensions and exactly two double extensions.

The `m=4` shape is structurally impossible. If `a+b` is the unique allowed double and `c` is another atom, then both `a+c` and `b+c` are forbidden. The bad-pair ear lemma forces `a,c` and `b,c` to be two-edge ears with the same outside center, which then makes `a+b` an ear as well, contradicting its being allowed.

For `m=3`, relabel so `a+b` and `a+c` are allowed while `b+c` is forbidden. The bad pair `b,c` is an ear

\[
b=wu,\qquad c=wv.
\]

The atom `a` cannot also be incident with `w`, because then `a+b` and `a+c` would be bad ears. Hence `a` has an outside endpoint distinct from `w`. Since `a,b,c` are all graph edges meeting vertices outside `C_P`, that outside endpoint has no other incident graph edge. Thus `a` is a graph bridge. The bridge factorization above excludes this shape from an edge-minimal counterexample.

Therefore the only surviving possibility is a pure five-spoke star

\[
\mathcal F(P)=
\{P,P+wu_1,\ldots,P+wu_5\}.
\]

### 5.2 The two singleton spoke pairs are distinct and nonoverlapping

Each singleton root passes through `w` and hence uses exactly two spokes.

They cannot use the same pair. If the corresponding direct edge exists, ear suppression collapses both singleton roots to `P`, making them equal. If the direct edge is absent, delete all five spokes and insert that direct edge. The compressed graph has exactly three all-minimal roots: `P` and the two suppressed singleton roots. This contradicts the all-minimal product theorem.

They also cannot overlap in exactly one spoke. Suppose their pairs are `ab` and `ac`. There are two spokes unused by either singleton. Delete one of them. The resulting subgraph has exact partition `5+1+1` with overlapping singleton spoke pairs. The archived `5+1+1` structural theorem proves that such overlap is impossible: the two pairs in that stratum must be disjoint.

Hence the two singleton pairs are disjoint. Relabel them

\[
\{u,v\},\qquad \{z,t\},
\]

and let `x` be the fifth attachment vertex, unused by both singleton roots.

### 5.3 The fifth spoke forces another common cycle

Delete the fifth spoke `wx`. The remaining graph has exact partition `5+1+1`, so the archived structural theorem applies. In particular:

* `uv` and `zt` are literal edges of the principal common cycle;
* removing those two edges splits the rest of that cycle into two paths
  \[
  X:v\leadsto z,
  \qquad
  Y:t\leadsto u;
  \]
* after relabelling, `e` lies on `X` and `f` lies on `Y`;
* the center `w` is adjacent to `u,v,z,t`.

The fifth attachment `x` lies on the principal cycle and is distinct from `u,v,z,t`, so it lies in the interior of `X` or `Y`.

Assume `x` lies on `X`. If `e` belongs to the subpath `X[x,z]`, then

\[
wx\cup X[x,z]\cup zt\cup Y[t,u]\cup uw
\]

is a simple cycle containing both `e` and `f`.

If instead `e` belongs to `X[v,x]`, then

\[
wx\cup X[x,v]\cup vu\cup Y[u,t]\cup tw
\]

is a simple cycle containing both distinguished edges.

Either cycle uses the fifth spoke and is different from the two archived singleton cycles. The case `x` on `Y` is symmetric.

Thus restoring the fifth spoke necessarily creates an additional minimal common-cycle root, contradicting `|R|=8`.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
6+1+1\text{ cannot occur in an edge-minimal counterexample.}
}
\]

## 6. Elimination of `3+3+1+1`

Let `P,Q` be the two size-three roots and `S,T` the singleton roots.

Write the ear of `P` as

\[
E_P=\{uw,wv\},
\]

and the ear of `Q` as `E_Q`.

The outside center of a size-three ear has degree exactly two in the non-isolated graph: any further edge incident with it would be another one-edge extension of the same root.

Every singleton root uses each size-three ear in full. Indeed, a singleton cycle cannot avoid the ear center, because then either ear edge would extend it; once the cycle passes through a degree-two ear center, it must use both ear edges.

### 6.1 Common-ear / cross-ear dichotomy

Consider `Q` relative to the ear `E_P`.

If `C_Q` avoids the center `w`, then both edges of `E_P` have an endpoint outside `V(C_Q)` and are therefore the two one-edge atoms of the size-three `Q` fiber. Hence `E_Q=E_P`.

Otherwise `C_Q` passes through `w` and uses the entire ear `E_P`.

By symmetry, exactly two possibilities remain:

1. **common ear:** `E_P=E_Q`;
2. **cross ears:** `P` uses the full ear of `Q` and `Q` uses the full ear of `P`.

### 6.2 Cross ears are structurally impossible

First note that the two ears cannot have the same endpoint pair with different centers. Every singleton root uses both ears in full, and two internally disjoint two-edge paths with the same endpoints already form a cycle; the singleton would not be a forest.

Thus the endpoint pairs are distinct. Let the corresponding direct edges be `g_P` and `g_Q`.

Neither direct edge is present in `G`. For example, if `g_P` existed, suppress `E_P` inside the root `Q`. The resulting `R(G)` forest avoids `E_P` and also avoids `E_Q`, because `Q` is the root of its own fiber. The only `R(G)` member avoiding both edges of `E_P` is the root `P`; but in the cross-ear case `P` uses `E_Q` in full. Contradiction. The argument for `g_Q` is symmetric.

Delete both ears and insert the two distinct direct edges `g_P,g_Q`, obtaining a simple graph `B`.

The four `R(B)` roots have the following new-edge states:

\[
P':(0,1),\qquad
Q':(1,0),\qquad
S':(1,1),\qquad
T':(1,1).
\]

There is no `(0,0)` state, because no original `R(G)` forest avoids both ears. All four compressed roots remain minimal.

Hence `B` is an all-minimal four-root graph, but `g_P` occurs in exactly three of its four roots. The Cartesian-product theorem forces a four-root all-minimal family to be `2x2`, so every graph edge has even root incidence. Contradiction.

Thus the cross-ear case cannot occur.

### 6.3 Common ear with the direct endpoint edge present

Now suppose both size-three fibers use the same ear

\[
u-w-v,
\]

and let `g=uv`.

If `g` is present, delete the two ear edges. The only `R` members avoiding the whole ear are the two roots `P,Q`; therefore the resulting graph `B` has exactly two minimal `R` roots.

The original graph is obtained from `B` by replacing the direct edge `g` with the two-terminal triangle network consisting of `g` in parallel with the length-two path `u-w-v`.

At unit edge weights this terminal network has

\[
D=3,
\qquad
C=4,
\]

so its effective weight is `4/3` and the exact replacement identity gives

\[
\boxed{
\Delta_G(\mathbf1)
=9\,\Delta_B\bigl(y_g=4/3,\ y_{r\ne g}=1\bigr).
}
\]

The archived exact-two theorem is fully weighted, so the right-hand side is nonnegative. Thus this common-ear subcase is pair-Rayleigh, not a counterexample.

### 6.4 Common ear with the direct endpoint edge absent

Assume now `g=uv` is absent. Delete the ear and insert `g`, producing `B`.

The `R(B)` family consists exactly of four minimal roots:

* the two old size-three roots, which avoid `g`;
* the two singleton roots, after suppressing the full ear to `g`, which contain `g`.

Thus `B` is all-minimal with four roots. By the archived all-minimal theorem, `B` has a proper two-vertex separator `{s,t}` separating `e` from `f`.

The edge `g` lies entirely on one separator side. Replace it there by the path `u-w-v`. This reconstructs `G` without changing the separator: the new vertex `w` is placed on the same side as `g`, and the two sides still meet only in `{s,t}`.

Therefore `G` itself has the same proper cross-two-separation. The exact identity is

\[
\Delta_G\{e,f\}
=
\alpha_K(e;s,t)\alpha_L(f;s,t).
\]

In an edge-minimal counterexample each terminal response is nonnegative: if the terminal edge is present, the response is a Rayleigh difference in the corresponding smaller side graph; if absent, adjoining a fresh terminal edge gives the same response as a Rayleigh difference in a strictly smaller simple graph. Properness of the separator guarantees strict size reduction.

Hence `Delta_G>=0`, contradiction.

Combining the cross-ear and common-ear cases gives

\[
\boxed{
3+3+1+1\text{ cannot occur in an edge-minimal counterexample.}
}
\]
