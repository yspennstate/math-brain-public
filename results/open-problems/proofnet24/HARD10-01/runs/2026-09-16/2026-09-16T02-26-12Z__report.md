## 4. A new general consequence of the all-minimal separator: Cartesian-product divisibility

This is a reusable strengthening of the four-root parity observation from the `23:29:32Z` checkpoint.

### Theorem 4.1 — product structure

Assume every member of `R` is minimal and `|R|\ge3`.  Let `{s,t}` be the proper two-vertex separator supplied by the archived all-minimal separator theorem, with

\[
G=K\cup L,
\qquad
V(K)\cap V(L)=\{s,t\},
\]

and with `e\in E(K)`, `f\in E(L)`.

Let `\mathcal P_K` be the set of simple `s-t` paths in `K` that occur on a common `e,f` cycle and contain `e`, and let `\mathcal P_L` be the corresponding `s-t` paths in `L` containing `f`.

Every minimal common cycle decomposes uniquely as

\[
P_K\cup P_L,
\qquad
P_K\in\mathcal P_K,
\quad
P_L\in\mathcal P_L.
\]

Conversely, every pair `P_K\in\mathcal P_K`, `P_L\in\mathcal P_L` gives a simple common `e,f` cycle: the two paths meet only at `s,t`, because the separator sides have no other common vertex.  Therefore the minimal root family is the Cartesian product

\[
\boxed{R\cong\mathcal P_K\times\mathcal P_L.}
\]

The separator construction comes from two roots that differ on both sides, so both local path sets contain at least two members.  Writing

\[
m=|\mathcal P_K|,\qquad n=|\mathcal P_L|,
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{|R|=mn,\qquad m,n\ge2.}
\]

### Corollary 4.2 — edge-incidence divisibility

Let `h` be any graph edge.  If `h\in E(K)`, let `a_h` be the number of paths in `\mathcal P_K` containing `h`.  Then every such local choice combines with every one of the `n` paths on the other side, so

\[
\#\{P\in R:h\in P\}=a_h n.
\]

Likewise, if `h\in E(L)`, the incidence is `b_h m` for an integer `b_h`.

Hence every edge incidence in an all-minimal root family is divisible by the number of local path choices on the opposite separator side.

In particular, if `|R|=6`, then `{m,n}={2,3}` and every graph edge occurs in a number of roots divisible by `2` or by `3`.  Therefore

\[
\boxed{
|R|=6\text{ all-minimal}
\Longrightarrow
\#\{P\in R:h\in P\}\ne5
\text{ for every edge }h.
}
\]

This new divisibility statement will be used in Section 7.

## 5. Elimination of `5+3`

### Theorem 5.1

There is no graph whose root-fiber partition is

\[
\boxed{5+3}.
\]

### Proof

Let `Q` be the size-three root.  By the archived size-three theorem,

\[
\mathcal F(Q)=\{Q,Q+ux,Q+xv\},
\]

where

\[
x\notin V(C_Q),\qquad u,v\in V(C_Q).
\]

The two ear edges `ux,xv` are the only graph edges with an endpoint outside `V(C_Q)`.  In particular the non-isolated degree of `x` is exactly two.

Let `P` be the size-five root.  The archived size-five classification says its four one-edge extensions form a pure four-spoke star.

We first show that `C_P` uses the full ear `u-x-v`.  Since the degree of `x` is two, a simple cycle either avoids `x` or uses both `ux,xv`.

Assume `C_P` avoids `x`.  Then both `ux` and `xv` have an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`, so by the one-edge extension criterion they are atoms of the size-five fiber of `P`.  All four atoms of that fiber share one outside center.  Since `ux,xv` already share `x`, the remaining two atoms must also be incident with `x`.  But `x\notin V(C_Q)`, so those two further edges would also be one-edge extensions of `Q`, contradicting that `Q` has exactly two atoms.  Therefore `C_P` passes through `x` and hence uses both `ux,xv`.

Set

\[
g=uv.
\]

#### The direct edge `g` cannot already exist

Suppose `g\in E(G)`.  Every member of the size-five fiber contains the full ear, because every member contains the root `P`.  Replacing `ux,xv` by the direct edge `g` preserves acyclicity and all old-vertex connectivity relations, hence sends each of the five members of `\mathcal F(P)` to a distinct member of `R(G)` that avoids both ear edges.

But among the original eight `R`-forests, the only member avoiding both ear edges is the root `Q`: the other two members of the size-three fiber use one ear edge each, while all five members of the `P` fiber use both.  Five distinct suppressed forests cannot all equal `Q`.  Contradiction.

Thus

\[
\boxed{uv\notin E(G).}
\]

#### Suppression creates `5+1`

Delete the two ear edges and add the new simple edge `g=uv`; call the resulting graph `B`.

If `A\in R(B)` avoids `g`, then it is an `R(G)` forest avoiding both ear edges, hence `A=Q`.

If `g\in A`, replace `g` by the two-edge path `u-x-v`.  The replacement preserves forestness and the three tests “add `e`”, “add `f`”, “add both”, so the expanded forest lies in `R(G)` and contains both ear edges.  The only such members are the five members of `\mathcal F(P)`.

Conversely suppressing the ear in each of those five members gives five distinct `R(B)` forests containing `g`.  Therefore `R(B)` has exactly six members.

The suppressed image `P'` is still minimal.  Any smaller `R(B)` witness containing `g` would expand to a smaller `R(G)` witness inside `P`; any smaller witness avoiding `g` would be `Q`, which would imply `Q\subset P` after expansion and contradict minimality of `P`.  Similarly `Q` stays minimal.  Inclusion inside the five-member suppressed family is preserved exactly.

Hence the root-fiber partition of `B` is

\[
\boxed{5+1}.
\]

But the corrected `20:45:10Z` checkpoint proves universally that `5+1` is impossible.  Contradiction.

Therefore no `5+3` graph exists.  QED.

## 6. Elimination of `4+3+1`

Assume now that `G,e,f` is an edge-minimal unit-weight counterexample with root-fiber partition

\[
4+3+1.

Let `P` be the size-three root, `Q` the size-four root, and `S` the singleton root.

Write the size-three ear as

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+uw,P+wv\},
\]

with `w\notin V(C_P)` and `u,v\in V(C_P)`.  Since these are the only one-edge extensions of `P`, they are the only graph edges with an endpoint outside `V(C_P)`.  In particular `w` has degree exactly two in the non-isolated graph.

### Lemma 6.1 — both other roots use the full ear

The singleton cycle `C_S` cannot avoid `w`.  If it did, `uw` and `wv` would each have an endpoint outside `V(C_S)`, so both would extend the singleton root, contradiction.  Hence `C_S` uses both ear edges.

Now consider `C_Q`.  Because the degree of `w` is two, it either avoids `w` or uses both ear edges.

Suppose it avoids `w`.  Then `uw,wv` are two atoms of the size-four fiber of `Q`.

A size-four down-set is either a Boolean square or a three-atom star.

* It cannot be a Boolean square.  The archived Boolean-square lemma would make `uw,wv` graph bridges.  But `u,v\in V(C_P)`, and the two ear edges together with either `u-v` arc of `C_P` lie on a graph cycle, so neither ear edge is a bridge.
* Therefore it would have to be a three-spoke star.  Since two atoms `uw,wv` share the outside center `w`, the third atom would also be incident to `w`.  But `w\notin V(C_P)`, so that third edge would be a third one-edge extension of `P`, contradicting `|\mathcal F(P)|=3`.

Thus `C_Q` cannot avoid `w`; it uses both `uw,wv`.

We have proved

\[
\boxed{uw,wv\subseteq Q\cap S.}
\]

### Lemma 6.2 — the direct ear edge is absent

Set

\[
g=uv.
\]

Suppose `g\in E(G)`.  Since `Q` and `S` are forests containing the full ear, neither can contain `g`.  Suppress the path `u-w-v` to `g` in each root.  The two suppressed roots are distinct `R(G)` forests, because the suppression map is injective on all edges outside the ear.

Both suppressed forests avoid `uw,wv`.

But the only member of the original `4+3+1` family avoiding both ear edges is `P`: the other two members of the size-three fiber use one ear edge, while all four members of the `Q` fiber and the singleton `S` use both.  Hence both suppressed roots would have to equal `P`, contradiction.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{uv\notin E(G).}
\]

### Lemma 6.3 — ear suppression gives exactly `4+1+1`

Delete `uw,wv` and add the new simple edge

\[
g=uv,
\]

forming a graph `B`.

Any `A\in R(B)` with `g\notin A` is an `R(G)` forest avoiding both ear edges, hence

\[
A=P.
\]

Any `A\in R(B)` with `g\in A` expands `g` to `u-w-v` and therefore gives an `R(G)` forest containing the full ear.  These are exactly the four members of `\mathcal F(Q)` together with the singleton `S`.

Conversely suppressing the full ear in those five forests gives five distinct `R(B)` forests containing `g`.

Therefore

\[
|R(B)|=6.
\]

The four images of the `Q` fiber retain their inclusion structure and form a size-four fiber.  The suppressed image `S'` remains minimal: any proper `R(B)` subforest containing `g` would expand to a proper `R(G)` subforest of `S`, while a proper witness avoiding `g` would be `P\subset S`, contradicting minimality of `S`.  The same argument shows the image `Q'` of the size-four root is minimal and incomparable with both `P` and `S'`.

Hence

\[
\boxed{
B\text{ has root-fiber partition }4+1+1.
}
\]

Moreover the new edge `g` occurs in all four members of the size-four fiber and in the singleton `S'`, but not in `P`.  Thus

\[
\boxed{
\#\{A\in R(B):g\in A\}=5.
}

## 7. Elimination of `3+1+1+1+1+1`

### Theorem 7.1

There is no graph whose root-fiber partition is

\[
\boxed{3+1+1+1+1+1}.
\]

### Proof

Let the size-three root be `P` with ear

\[
\mathcal F(P)=\{P,P+uw,P+wv\},
\]

where `w\notin V(C_P)` and `u,v\in V(C_P)`.

As above, the two ear edges are the only graph edges incident with the outside vertex `w`, so every simple common cycle either avoids `w` or uses both ear edges.

Every singleton root must use the full ear.  If a singleton cycle avoided `w`, either ear edge would have an endpoint outside that singleton cycle and would extend the singleton root, contradiction.

Set

\[
g=uv.
\]

#### The direct edge is absent

Suppose `g\in E(G)`.  Suppress the full ear in each of the five singleton roots.  This produces five distinct `R(G)` forests avoiding both ear edges.  But the only original `R`-forest avoiding both is `P`: the two other members of the size-three fiber use exactly one ear edge, and all five singletons use both.  Contradiction.

Thus

\[
\boxed{uv\notin E(G).}
\]

#### Compression gives six minimal roots and a five-incidence edge

Delete `uw,wv` and add the new simple edge `g=uv`, forming `B`.

An `R(B)` forest avoiding `g` is exactly an `R(G)` forest avoiding both ear edges, hence it is `P`.

An `R(B)` forest containing `g` expands to an `R(G)` forest containing the full ear, hence it is one of the five singleton roots.  Conversely every singleton suppresses to such a forest.  Therefore

\[
|R(B)|=6.
\]

All six members are minimal.  For a suppressed singleton `S'`, a smaller `R(B)` witness containing `g` would expand to a smaller `R(G)` witness inside the original singleton `S`; a smaller witness avoiding `g` would be `P`, which would imply `P\subset S` after expansion and contradict minimality of `S`.  The root `P` is already minimal.

So `B` is an all-minimal six-root graph, and the new edge `g` belongs to the five suppressed singleton roots but not to `P`:

\[
\boxed{
\#\{A\in R(B):g\in A\}=5.
}
\]

By Theorem 4.1 the six minimal roots factor across the archived proper two-separator as a Cartesian product of two local path families.  Since both factors have size at least two and their product is six, their sizes are `2` and `3`.  Corollary 4.2 says every graph-edge incidence among the six roots is divisible by `2` or by `3`.

The value `5` is divisible by neither.  Contradiction.

Therefore the partition `3+1+1+1+1+1` is impossible.  QED.
