## 5. B2 — attack the negation first: (4.1) is decisively false

The counterexample is the finite regular rooted tree. The polynomial lower-bound phenomenon is consistent with the source's own discussion of regular trees and is **not claimed as novel**. What is new in the present route inventory is the exact implication for the repository's old induced-subgraph candidate: every induced adjacency spectrum can remain uniformly source-subcritical while the largest component is polynomial.

### 5.1 The host graph

Let `T_{d,h}` be the complete rooted `d`-ary tree of depth `h`: the root has `d` children, each non-root non-leaf vertex has one parent and `d` children, and every leaf is at distance `h` from the root.

Its vertex count is

`n_h = (d^(h+1)-1)/(d-1)`.                                  (5.1)

Its maximum degree is `d+1` for `h>=2`.

### 5.2 Exact adjacency spectral radius

**Lemma 5.1.**

`lambda(T_{d,h}) = 2 sqrt(d) cos(pi/(h+2)).`                 (5.2)

#### Proof

By automorphism symmetry, the Perron eigenvector is constant on each level. Let `x_j` be its value on level `j`, `0<=j<=h`, and put

`y_j = d^(j/2) x_j`.

The adjacency eigenvalue equations are

`lambda x_0 = d x_1`,

`lambda x_j = x_(j-1) + d x_(j+1)` for `1<=j<h`,

`lambda x_h = x_(h-1)`.

After the `y_j` change of variables these become

`lambda y_0 = sqrt(d) y_1`,

`lambda y_j = sqrt(d)(y_(j-1)+y_(j+1))`,

`lambda y_h = sqrt(d) y_(h-1)`.

Thus the radial Perron problem is exactly `sqrt(d)` times the adjacency eigenproblem of the path on `h+1` vertices. The top path eigenvalue is `2 cos(pi/(h+2))`, proving (5.2). `□`

### 5.3 Put the tree exactly at the source subcritical parameter

Fix `epsilon in (0,1)`. Choose an integer `d` so large that

`m_infty := (1-epsilon) sqrt(d)/2 > 1`.                     (5.3)

Equivalently, it is enough to take

`d > 4/(1-epsilon)^2`.

For each `h`, set

`p_h := (1-epsilon)/lambda(T_{d,h})`.                       (5.4)

This is exactly on the allowed source boundary `p=(1-epsilon)/lambda(G)`.

The downward offspring distribution seen from a vertex away from the bottom is `Bin(d,p_h)`, whose mean is

`m_h = d p_h`

`    = (1-epsilon) d / [2 sqrt(d) cos(pi/(h+2))]`

`    -> (1-epsilon) sqrt(d)/2 = m_infty > 1`.              (5.5)

So the ambient adjacency process is uniformly subcritical in the source sense, while outward branching is supercritical for a linear number of generations.

### 5.4 Every induced adjacency spectral radius is still source-subcritical

Let `H` be any induced subgraph of `T_{d,h}`. Its adjacency matrix is a principal submatrix of the tree adjacency matrix. Hence by the Rayleigh quotient/interlacing monotonicity,

`lambda(H) <= lambda(T_{d,h})`.

Therefore

`p_h lambda(H) <= 1-epsilon`.                              (5.6)

Consequently the old candidate (4.1) predicts at most

`log n_h / log(1/(1-epsilon)) = O_epsilon(log n_h)`.        (5.7)

The next proposition shows that the true largest component is polynomial in `n_h`.

### 5.5 Rigorous polynomial lower bound by many independent descendant trials

**Proposition 5.2.** Let `d` satisfy (5.3). Define

`alpha(d,epsilon) := log_d( (1-epsilon) sqrt(d)/2 ) > 0`.   (5.8)

Then for every `zeta>0`,

`P( |C_max((T_{d,h})_{p_h})| >= n_h^(alpha-zeta) ) -> 1`   (5.9)

as `h->infinity`.

#### Proof

Fix `zeta>0`. Pick `r` with

`1 < r < m_infty`

so close to `m_infty` that `log_d r > alpha-zeta/3`.

Choose a constant `p_0` satisfying

`r/d < p_0 < (1-epsilon)/(2 sqrt(d))`.                      (5.10)

Because `lambda(T_{d,h})<2sqrt(d)`, we have

`p_h > (1-epsilon)/(2sqrt(d)) > p_0`

for every `h`. Therefore a downward `Bin(d,p_h)` branching process stochastically dominates the fixed Galton--Watson process with offspring law `Bin(d,p_0)`.

Let `Z_L` denote generation size in that fixed process, and let `m_0=d p_0>r`. Since the offspring law is bounded, the standard supercritical Galton--Watson martingale theorem gives

`Z_L / m_0^L -> W` almost surely,

with `q:=P(W>0)>0`, and `W>0` exactly on survival. On `{W>0}`, because `r<m_0`, eventually

`Z_L >= r^L`.

Hence there are constants `L_0` and `c_0>0` such that for every `L>=L_0`,

`P(Z_L >= r^L) >= c_0`.                                   (5.11)

Now choose a small fixed `eta>0`, later small enough that

`(1-eta) log_d r > alpha-zeta`.                            (5.12)

At level

`k=floor(eta h)`

of `T_{d,h}` there are exactly `d^k` vertices. The descendant edge sets below these vertices are pairwise disjoint. For every such vertex, the percolation restricted to its descendant tree of depth

`L=h-k`

stochastically dominates the fixed Galton--Watson process above. Therefore, for all large `h`, each level-`k` vertex has probability at least `c_0` of being connected inside its descendant subtree to at least `r^L` vertices at level `h`, and these events are independent over the `d^k` roots.

Thus

`P(no level-k subtree succeeds) <= (1-c_0)^(d^k) -> 0`.    (5.13)

On success, the full percolated graph contains a connected component of at least `r^(h-k)` vertices. By (5.1),

`log n_h = h log d + O(1)`,

so

`r^(h-k) = n_h^((1-eta)log_d r+o(1))`.

Using (5.12) gives (5.9). `□`

### 5.6 B2 verdict

Equations (5.6)--(5.9) give the desired contradiction to the old intake formula:

- every induced `H` has `p lambda(H)<=1-epsilon`, so (4.1) is `O(log n)`;
- nevertheless `C_max` is at least `n^(alpha-o(1))` a.a.s.

Therefore **no universal answer based only on the collection of static induced-subgraph adjacency spectral radii in the form (4.1) can be correct**.

This does not refute the source question; it refutes a repository candidate answer.

## 6. B18 — change language: the missing variable is finite-horizon transient branching

The tree counterexample identifies exactly why the static induced-spectrum language fails.

### 6.1 The global finite Hashimoto spectral radius also cannot be the whole answer

On a finite tree every non-backtracking walk is simple and therefore has bounded length. The finite Hashimoto/non-backtracking matrix is nilpotent, so

`rho(B_T)=0`.                                              (6.1)

Yet before reaching the leaves, an outward directed edge in `T_{d,h}` has `d` non-backtracking continuations per step. At the source-subcritical `p_h`, this transient reproduction factor is

`p_h d -> (1-epsilon)sqrt(d)/2 > 1`.                       (6.2)

So replacing `lambda(H)` by the **global** finite-graph Hashimoto spectral radius would fail in the opposite direction: for a tree it reports zero asymptotic non-backtracking growth even though there is supercritical growth for `Theta(h)` generations.

This distinction is consistent with the older non-backtracking percolation literature, where infinite or locally tree-like asymptotics are central. G0010 requires a finite-size component bound, and transient amplification over a horizon comparable to `log n` can dominate even when the eventual finite-matrix spectral radius is zero.

### 6.2 A finite-horizon profile

For a vertex `v`, let

`N_k^NB(v)`

be the number of non-backtracking walks of length `k` starting at `v`. Define the transient growth profile

`beta_k(G) := max_v (N_k^NB(v))^(1/k)`.                    (6.3)

For the rooted `d`-ary tree, away from the bottom and for admissible horizons,

`beta_k >= d`.

But the old attempt already teaches that a maximum alone is not enough: complete bipartite examples and disjoint constructions require a multiplicity variable. One natural two-parameter refinement is

`M_G(k,a) := | { v : N_k^NB(v) >= a^k } |`.               (6.4)

The tree proof above uses exactly two facts:

1. `p a>1` for an `a` close to `d` over a long horizon;
2. there are many vertices with disjoint descendant neighborhoods realizing this growth, which amplifies a constant single-root success probability to an a.a.s. event.

Thus a plausible source-level state variable is not one more scalar eigenvalue. It is a **finite-horizon growth-plus-multiplicity profile**, or a dual functional encoding the same information.

No universal upper theorem in terms of (6.3)--(6.4) is proved here, so these definitions are exploratory, not a replacement target.

### 6.3 A standard logarithmic wedge, included only to locate the transition

There is one easy regime where no such transient supercritical growth is possible. Component exploration from a root is dominated after the first generation by a Galton--Watson process with offspring `Bin(Delta-1,p)`. Hence if for some fixed `eta>0`

`p(Delta-1) <= 1-eta`,                                    (6.5)

standard subcritical Galton--Watson exponential tails imply

`|C_max(G_p)| = O_eta(log n)` a.a.s.                       (6.6)

Under the source condition `p<=(1-epsilon)/lambda`, (6.5) follows from

`(1-epsilon)(Delta-1)/lambda <= 1-eta`.                   (6.7)

In particular, if `Delta <= (1+delta)lambda` and `delta` is small enough depending on fixed `epsilon`, then the source question has the familiar logarithmic answer in that restricted wedge.

This is standard branching-process folklore / a routine partial case, not a novelty claim and not metric movement. Its purpose here is structural: (6.7) marks the side of parameter space on which the tree mechanism is impossible.

**B18 outcome: the correct language must retain finite-horizon non-backtracking/transient branching and its multiplicity; neither induced adjacency spectra nor the global finite Hashimoto radius is sufficient.**

## 7. B24 — extremal-object search: a parameter-setting tree gadget

The previous section suggests asking how much transient branching can coexist with prescribed `(Delta,lambda)`. A simple disjoint construction gives a rigorous lower family and points to a candidate extremal branching factor.

### 7.1 Exact construction for fixed integer parameters

Fix integers `Delta,Lambda` satisfying

`sqrt(Delta) <= Lambda <= Delta`.                          (7.1)

(The inequalities are the natural feasible range for a graph with a vertex of degree `Delta`: the star around that vertex gives `lambda>=sqrt(Delta)`, while always `lambda<=Delta`.)

Choose an integer `b>=2` such that

`b+1 <= Delta`,

`2sqrt(b) <= Lambda`.                                     (7.2)

For every depth `h`, let

`G_h := T_{b,h} disjoint_union K_(Lambda+1) disjoint_union K_(1,Delta)`.  (7.3)

Then

`Delta(G_h)=Delta`,                                       (7.4)

because the star has degree `Delta`, while the clique has degree `Lambda<=Delta` and the tree degree is at most `b+1<=Delta`.

The spectral radius of a disjoint union is the maximum of the component spectral radii. By Lemma 5.1,

`lambda(T_{b,h}) < 2sqrt(b) <= Lambda`;

also

`lambda(K_(Lambda+1))=Lambda`,

`lambda(K_(1,Delta))=sqrt(Delta)<=Lambda`.

Therefore

`lambda(G_h)=Lambda` exactly.                              (7.5)

Set the source-boundary percolation parameter

`p=(1-epsilon)/Lambda`.                                   (7.6)

On the large tree component the downward offspring mean is

`mu_b = p b = (1-epsilon)b/Lambda`.                        (7.7)

Whenever `mu_b>1`, the proof of Proposition 5.2 applies verbatim (the fixed clique and star have negligible order as `h->infinity`) and gives, for every `zeta>0`,

`P( |C_max((G_h)_p)| >= |G_h|^(alpha_b-zeta) ) -> 1`,     (7.8)

where

`alpha_b = log_b( (1-epsilon)b/Lambda ) >0`.               (7.9)

Thus prescribed maximum degree and adjacency spectral radius can coexist with polynomial source-subcritical components whenever the permitted transient tree branching satisfies (7.7)>1.

### 7.2 Best regular-tree branching allowed by `(Delta,Lambda)`

Within this gadget family, the largest permissible branching factor is

`b_* = min{ Delta-1, floor(Lambda^2/4) }`.                 (7.10)

For `b>1`,

`alpha_b = 1 + log((1-epsilon)/Lambda)/log b`.

Since `log((1-epsilon)/Lambda)<0` in the nontrivial regime, `alpha_b` increases with `b`. Hence (7.10) maximizes the polynomial exponent among these regular-tree gadgets.

This suggests the lower-envelope exponent

`alpha_tree(Delta,Lambda,epsilon)`
`  := [ log_{b_*}( (1-epsilon)b_*/Lambda ) ]_+`,           (7.11)

where the positive part is understood as zero when the branching mean is at most one.

Equation (7.11) is **not** claimed as the answer to G0010. It is a rigorously realized lower-family exponent for fixed compatible integer parameter pairs, plus an extremal heuristic for the full problem.

Still, it matches the qualitative regimes unusually well:

- if `Lambda` is close to `Delta`, then `b_*` is about `Delta` and `(1-epsilon)b_*/Lambda<1`, so the polynomial tree mechanism turns off, agreeing with the logarithmic regular regime;
- if `Lambda` is of order `sqrt(Delta)` with large parameters, then `b_*` is of order `Delta` and (7.11) tends to exponent `1/2`, matching the source's square-root-scale behavior;
- if `Lambda=Delta^a` with `1/2<a<1` and the `Delta-1` constraint is active, then formally `alpha_tree` approaches `1-a`; for the complete-bipartite parameter relation `Delta=s^3`, `Lambda=s^2` this gives exponent `1/3`, the same power of `n` as the source's `s log s` example when `n~s^3` (the logarithmic multiplicity factor is not captured by the tree gadget).

That last parenthetical is important: the complete-bipartite example shows again that transient growth exponent alone is not the entire story. Multiplicity/geometry can add logarithmic factors.

### 7.3 Why `Lambda^2/4` appears naturally

The constraint `b<=Lambda^2/4` is not arbitrary. A depth-`h` complete `b`-ary tree has spectral radius approaching `2sqrt(b)`. Therefore sustained regular branching by factor `b` over a long tree-like horizon forces adjacency spectral radius at least approximately `2sqrt(b)`. This makes `Lambda^2/4` the natural regular-tree branching ceiling.

The important unanswered extremal question is whether **irregular** rooted trees / layered graphs can beat the regular-tree exponent (7.11) at fixed `(Delta,Lambda)`, and if so by how much. That is a concrete extremal problem rather than another broad restatement of G0010.

**B24 outcome: rigorous parameter-setting lower family plus a candidate extremal branching ceiling; no universal upper theorem yet.**

## 8. What failed, exactly

### Failed route 2 — replace adjacency spectrum by one global Hashimoto eigenvalue

### Incomplete route 3 — the tree lower exponent as the universal answer

The tree gadget gives (7.11), but it does not prove an upper bound. Complete bipartite graphs already show that multiplicity can create an additional logarithmic factor. Irregular layered trees may also realize transient growth not captured by the regular `b_*` model. Thus promoting (7.11) to a source answer would be unjustified.

### Incomplete route 4 — standard maximum-degree branching domination

The condition `p(Delta-1)<1` gives `O(log n)`, but this covers only the near-regular wedge (6.7). It says nothing in the hard parameter range where `(1-epsilon)(Delta-1)/lambda>1`.
