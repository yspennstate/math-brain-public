## 2. New idea before calculation

The previous B11 reduction says that Conjecture 10.3.1 is a boundary-gluing problem. Sheffield's original proof in the isotropic / discrete-Lipschitz regimes makes that statement literal: one samples the candidate Gibbs field in a large inner box and then interpolates it through a thin shell to an affine boundary condition of the same slope. The missing cost must be `o(volume)`.

The key question for this run is therefore not another bulk entropy identity. It is:

> **Where, exactly, does Sheffield's known isotropic boundary interpolation cease to be uniform when the coordinate potentials are fully anisotropic?**

Reading the proof chain in Chapter 5 isolates the issue. On a face whose outward normal is coordinate `k`, the tangential derivatives of the height mismatch are paid by the tangential coordinate potentials `V_i`, `i != k`, whereas the derivative through the thin shell contains a term of size `h/epsilon` and is paid by the **normal** potential `V_k`. In the isotropic proof the same Young function controls both sides, so the Orlicz--Sobolev estimate feeds exactly the integrability needed for the normal interpolation. In the anisotropic setting these are different functions.

Cianchi's fully anisotropic Sobolev inequality gives the optimal integrability produced by the tangential energies. It therefore tells us whether the normal potential is subcritical, critical or supercritical relative to the tangential trace regularity. This creates a sharp cross-direction obstruction that is invisible if one only says "use an anisotropic Sobolev inequality."

## 3. B22 — READ THE FAILURE: the direct Cianchi substitution is insufficient

### 3.1 What Sheffield's face interpolation needs

Consider a `(d-1)`-dimensional face with coordinates `x_i`, `i != k`. Let `h` be the difference between the sampled height function and the target affine plane on that face. In a shell of thickness `epsilon`, the most direct interpolation is schematically

\[
w(x_{\hat k},t)=u_{\rm aff}(x_{\hat k},t)+\theta(t)h(x_{\hat k}),
\]

where `theta` drops from one to zero across the shell. Tangential derivatives contain `partial_i h`, while the normal derivative contains a term comparable with `h/epsilon`. Thus a separable anisotropic energy asks simultaneously for

\[
\int_{\text{face}} V_i(\partial_i h),\qquad i\ne k,
\]

and

\[
\int_{\text{face}}V_k(h/\epsilon).
\tag{3.1}
\]

In the isotropic case all `V_i=V`, so Sheffield's Orlicz--Sobolev Lemma 5.3.1 supplies precisely the uniform-integrability step used in Lemmas 5.3.2--5.3.5. In a fully anisotropic SAP, the tangential energies need not imply the Orlicz integrability demanded by `V_k`.

### 3.2 Exact spike obstruction in dimension three

The obstruction already appears on a flat two-dimensional face. Let the tangential energies be linear,

\[
V_1(t)=V_2(t)=|t|,
\]

and let the normal direction have

\[
V_3(t)=|t|^p,
\qquad p\ge1.
\]

Inside a unit disk define, for `H -> infinity`,

\[
h_H(x)=H(1-H|x|)_+.
\tag{3.2}
\]

This is a triangular spike of height `H` and support radius `H^{-1}`. Direct polar integration gives

\[
\|h_H\|_{L^1}
=2\pi\int_0^{1/H}H(1-Hr)r\,dr
=\frac{\pi}{3H}\longrightarrow0,
\tag{3.3}
\]

while, on the support, `|nabla h_H|=H^2`, so

\[
\int |\nabla h_H|\,dx
=H^2\pi H^{-2}=\pi.
\tag{3.4}
\]

Thus the mismatch tends to zero in `L^1` while its total tangential energy remains uniformly bounded.

But the normal interpolation cost at fixed shell thickness `epsilon>0` is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int \left|\frac{h_H}{\epsilon}\right|^p dx
&=\epsilon^{-p}
2\pi\int_0^{1/H}H^p(1-Hr)^p r\,dr\\
&=\frac{2\pi}{(p+1)(p+2)}\,\epsilon^{-p}H^{p-2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.5}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{ll}
p<2:& \text{the normal cost tends to }0,\\
p=2:& \text{the normal cost stays bounded away from }0,\\
p>2:& \text{the normal cost diverges.}
\end{array}}
\tag{3.6}
\]

This is a complete analytic counterexample to the **direct anisotropic analogue of the small-mismatch face lemma** when the normal growth is quadratic or faster than the integrability generated by two tangential `L^1` derivatives.

It is **not** a counterexample to Sheffield Conjecture 10.3.1. It says that one specific deterministic gluing mechanism cannot be extended to all SAPs merely by replacing the isotropic Sobolev inequality with the phrase "fully anisotropic Sobolev inequality."

### 3.3 Why the threshold is exactly the anisotropic Sobolev threshold

Cianchi's fully anisotropic inequality treats a convex full-gradient integrand and identifies the optimal rearranged Sobolev target. For the separable power-law model on an `n=d-1` dimensional face,

\[
F_k(\xi_{\hat k})=\sum_{i\ne k}|\xi_i|^{p_i},
\]

write

\[
s_k:=\sum_{i\ne k}\frac1{p_i}.
\tag{3.7}
\]

When `s_k>1`, the anisotropic Sobolev target has power exponent

\[
q_k=\frac{d-1}{s_k-1}.
\tag{3.8}
\]

This recovers the ordinary exponent when all tangential `p_i` agree. If `s_k<=1`, the face is in the `L^infty`/supercritical Sobolev regime, so every finite normal power is below the basic target.

For a power normal cost `V_k(t)=|t|^{p_k}`, the direct facewise gluing has a compactness margin when

\[
\boxed{p_k<q_k.}
\tag{3.9}
\]

At equality one can lose strong compactness, and beyond equality anisotropic spike scaling destroys the required smallness. In the example `(p_1,p_2,p_3)=(1,1,p)` in `d=3`, `s_3=2`, hence `q_3=2`, exactly matching (3.6).

For general Young functions the natural replacement is that the normal Young function must be essentially/compactly dominated at infinity by the Cianchi target generated from the tangential integrand. I record that as the **cross-subcritical criterion**, not as a proved full-source theorem.

**B22 outcome:** a precise obstruction to the known proof technology. The old proof fails at a cross-direction Orlicz/Sobolev embedding, not at the DLR identity or existence of the bulk SFE limit. `C_FE` does not move.

## 4. B5 — DEFORM FROM ISOTROPY: a sharp continuation wall

A natural strategy for Conjecture 10.3.1 is to deform an isotropic potential, where Sheffield's theorem applies, into an anisotropic one and prove that the variational identity persists along the path.

The spike calculation shows why a continuation argument based on a **uniform Chapter-5 interpolation estimate** can fail before it reaches the target.

Take the three-dimensional family

\[
V_1(r)=V_2(r)=|r|,
\qquad
V_{3,t}(r)=|r|^{p(t)},
\qquad
p(t)=1+t(P-1),
\tag{4.1}
\]

for fixed `P>2` and `t in [0,1]`. At `t=0` the coordinate growth is isotropic linear growth. Along the path, the normal exponent crosses the tangential Sobolev exponent `2` at

\[
\boxed{t_c=\frac{1}{P-1}.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

For `t<t_c`, the normal exponent is cross-subcritical. At `t=t_c`, the spike (3.2) has a nonvanishing normal interpolation cost despite `L^1` mismatch tending to zero; for `t>t_c`, that cost diverges.

Consequently:

\[
\boxed{\text{no continuation proof whose only uniform input is Sheffield's deterministic face-interpolation estimate can cross }t_c.}
\tag{4.3}
\]

This family should be read as a path through coordinatewise convex/coercive SAPs. Once the growth exponent changes, it need not be a strictly dominated perturbation of the original isotropic potential in Sheffield's narrower "perturbed ISAP" sense. I therefore do not use (4.1) to claim failure of any theorem already proved in that narrower class.

The conclusion is about proof architecture, not about the truth of the conjecture. Another continuation mechanism could survive the threshold if it does not demand worst-case deterministic extension of every low-energy boundary trace.

**B5 outcome:** the naive isotropy-to-anisotropy homotopy has a real analytic barrier. The relevant parameter is not merely the size of anisotropy; it is the cross-Sobolev relation between normal growth and tangential trace integrability. No metric movement.

## 5. B30 — one face direction at a time: an obstruction profile

The preceding calculation naturally localizes by normal direction. For each `k`, define the tangential face integrand

\[
F_k(\xi_{\hat k})=\sum_{i\ne k}V_i(\xi_i).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Let `B_k` denote the optimal anisotropic Sobolev target generated from `F_k` in the sense of Cianchi. The direct Sheffield interpolation across a face normal to `e_k` is safe only when the normal potential `V_k` lies compactly below `B_k` at infinity. Thus an anisotropic model has a **directionwise gluing profile**:

\[
\mathcal G_k = \text{relation of }V_k\text{ to the Sobolev target generated by }\{V_i:i\ne k\}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

The useful point is that the obstruction need not be symmetric. For the three-dimensional power example

\[
(p_1,p_2,p_3)=(1,1,p),
\tag{5.3}
\]

we find:

- normal `3`: tangential exponents `(1,1)`, so `q_3=2`; this face is critical/supercritical when `p>=2`;
- normal `1`: tangential exponents `(1,p)`, so `s_1=1+1/p` and `q_1=2p`; the normal exponent is `p_1=1<2p`;
- normal `2`: identically `p_2=1<2p`.

Hence **one fast-growth coordinate breaks exactly the face perpendicular to that coordinate**; the other two faces remain cross-subcritical.

This is a stronger diagnosis than saying "anisotropic Sobolev is difficult": it identifies which direction is failing and the exact threshold.

### 5.1 A restricted positive local criterion

The same calculation gives a restricted positive analytic statement. In a separable power-law model, if every coordinate satisfies

\[
p_k<q_k
\qquad\text{whenever }s_k>1,
\tag{5.4}
\]

(and no restriction is needed from this mechanism when `s_k<=1`), then bounded tangential anisotropic Sobolev energy plus convergence of the mismatch in `L^1` yields strong convergence in the normal `L^{p_k}` norm by anisotropic Sobolev control and interpolation. Therefore the **local face interpolation step** has the same compactness mechanism as Sheffield's isotropic argument.

I do **not** promote this to a proof of Conjecture 10.3.1 even on the entire cross-subcritical class in this report: the lattice approximation, periodic/twisted boundary bookkeeping, finite-range perturbation terms, gauge conventions, and the complete free-energy resampling argument would all need to be checked end to end. The result here is a source-relevant analytic criterion and a matching obstruction outside it.

**B30 outcome:** the problem decomposes into a finite set of directional cross-Sobolev tests. This is useful partial structure, but it does not settle either full source conjecture.

## 6. What this says about the previous boundary-relative-entropy reduction

The previous report reduced the variational gap between same-slope Gibbs states to a boundary-shell information difference. Sheffield's construction tries to prove the corresponding leading boundary cost is state-independent by deterministic gluing of sampled inner configurations to a common affine outer boundary.

The spike shows why "the boundary has only order `L^{d-1}` sites" is still insufficient. A thin boundary shell can contain rare, high-amplitude traces whose **normal extension energy is volume-order or worse** when normal growth outruns the tangential Sobolev target. Thus the exact boundary-relative-entropy identity and the failure of deterministic anisotropic interpolation are consistent: the source difficulty really is concentrated in a boundary large-deviation / extension problem.

This also suggests that a successful proof may not need a deterministic extension estimate for *every* low-energy trace. For the variational principle, the trace is sampled from a finite-SFE Gibbs state. It may be enough to show that the bad supercritical traces are exponentially or entropy-negligibly rare.

That observation changes the next method rather than adding another Sobolev lemma.

## 7. New proposed object: probabilistic anisotropic extension energy

For a shell of thickness `epsilon L`, define schematically

\[
\mathcal E_{\epsilon,L}(h)
:=\inf_{w:\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\rm in}w=h,\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\rm out}w=0}
\sum_{e\subset \text{shell}}V_e(\nabla w(e)).
\tag{7.1}
\]

The isotropic proof effectively supplies a deterministic `o(L^d)` upper bound on this quantity for all traces satisfying the required Orlicz controls. The cross-supercritical spike proves that such a worst-case statement is too much to expect in the full anisotropic class.

A source-level replacement could instead aim for

\[
\boxed{
\forall\eta>0:\quad
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}\limsup_{L\to\infty}
\frac1{L^d}
\log \mu\!\left(\mathcal E_{\epsilon,L}(h)>\eta L^d\right)<0,
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

or any quantitatively adequate variant implying that the contribution of bad boundary traces to the Gibbs resampling/free-energy comparison is negligible. This is only a proposed sufficient route; (7.2) is **not proved**.

The advantage is conceptual: it spends the finite-SFE hypothesis probabilistically instead of trying to turn it into a deterministic Sobolev bound that the spike disproves.
