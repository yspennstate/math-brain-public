## 3. Preserved target-aware one-dimensional Bellman game

For an upper bound it suffices to give a two-agent adaptive adversary with announced exact MIVs `p_1=p_2=1`.

Present three initial goods valued `1` by both agents. If one agent receives all three, the other has `B=0`, best missed good `R=1`, total value `V=3`, and PROP1 factor `2/3`, already below every endpoint considered here. Otherwise the split is `2--1`; both agents have missed a unit good and every later value will lie in `[0,1]`, so `R_1=R_2=1` thereafter.

Fix

\[
\alpha=1-\delta,\qquad \beta=1+\delta,
\]

and define tilted safety

\[
z_i=2(B_i+R_i)-\alpha V_i.
\]

Then `z_i<=0` is exactly equivalent to PROP1 factor at most `alpha`. After the initial `2--1` split the sorted tilted state is

\[
(x,y)=(1+3\delta,3+3\delta),\qquad D=y-x=2.
\]

For a later good with values `(a,b)` to the current lower/higher tilted-safety agents, put

\[
C_\delta=\beta a+\alpha b.
\]

If the higher agent receives the good, the lower coordinate decreases by `alpha a` and the gap becomes

\[
D_H=D+\alpha a+\beta b.
\]

If the lower agent receives it, the lower-coordinate decrease after resorting is

\[
-\beta a+(C_\delta-D)_+,
\]

and the new gap is

\[
D_L=|D-C_\delta|.
\]

Thus, with `G_h^delta(D)` the largest decrease of the current lower tilted safety the adversary can force within at most `h` further goods,

\[
G_0^\delta(D)=0
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
G_h^\delta(D)=\sup_{0\le a,b\le1}\min\left\{
\alpha a+G_{h-1}^\delta(D+\alpha a+\beta b),
-\beta a+(C_\delta-D)_++G_{h-1}^\delta(|D-C_\delta|)
\right\}.
}
\]

The action square is compact and each finite-horizon branch function is continuous, so each finite-horizon supremum is attained. A Bellman lower bound therefore corresponds to an actual finite adaptive adversarial policy.

### Proposition 1 — one-step value

For every `0<alpha<=1`, `beta=2-alpha`, and `D>=0`,

\[
\boxed{G_1(D)=(\alpha-D)_+.}

**Proof.** With `G_0=0`, the high-recipient branch is `alpha a`. The low-recipient branch is

\[

\]

If there is no reflection this is `-beta a<=0`. If there is reflection it is `alpha b-D<=alpha-D`. Thus the min of the two branches is at most `(alpha-D)_+`. For `D<alpha`, action `(a,b)=(1,1)` has high value `alpha` and reflected low value `alpha-D`, attaining `alpha-D`; for `D>=alpha`, the zero-valued good attains zero. QED.

### Proposition 2 — exact two-step value

Define

\[
D_0=\alpha-\frac{\alpha^2}{2}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{2},
\qquad
A=\frac{\alpha^2}{2},
\qquad
D_1=2\alpha-\frac{\alpha^2}{2}
=\alpha+\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}.
\]

Then for every `D>=0`,

\[
\boxed{
G_2(D)=
\begin{cases}
\alpha-D, & 0\le D\le D_0,\\[2mm]
\alpha^2/2, & D_0\le D\le D_1,\\[2mm]
2\alpha-D, & D_1\le D\le2\alpha,\\[2mm]
0, & D\ge2\alpha.
\end{cases}}
\]

**Proof.** Put `f(D)=(alpha-D)_+`. For fixed action `(a,b)`, the high branch is

\[
H=\alpha a+f(D+\alpha a+\beta b)
=\max\{\alpha a,\alpha-D-\beta b\}.
\]

Let

\[
t=C_\delta-D=\beta a+\alpha b-D.
\]

The low branch is

\[
L=-\beta a+t_++f(|t|),
\]

hence exactly

\[
L=
\begin{cases}
-\beta a, & t\le-\alpha,\\
\alpha(1+b)-D, & -\alpha\le t\le0,\\
\alpha-\beta a, & 0\le t\le\alpha,\\
\alpha b-D, & t\ge\alpha.
\end{cases}
\]

The zero action pads the one-step value `f(D)`. Suppose a nonzero action attains a value `r>f(D)`. Since the second term in `H` is at most `f(D)`, we must have

\[
r\le\alpha a.
\]

In the two low-branch regions capable of improving on `f(D)`, the displayed formulas and their region conditions give

\[
r\le\alpha-\beta a.
\]

(The region `t>=alpha` itself has `L=alpha b-D<=f(D)` and cannot strictly improve on padding.) Therefore

\[
r\le \max_{0\le a\le1}\min\{\alpha a,\alpha-\beta a\}
=\frac{\alpha^2}{2},
\]

with the balancing point `a=alpha/2`, using `alpha+beta=2`.

For `D<=2alpha`, the same piecewise low-branch formula gives the universal bound

\[
L\le2\alpha-D.
\]

Indeed this is immediate in the first, second and fourth regions; in the third region `t>=0` implies `D<=beta a+alpha b<=beta a+alpha`, hence `alpha-beta a<=2alpha-D`. For `D>=2alpha`, each region instead gives `L<=0`, so no action can beat zero padding.

Consequently

\[
G_2(D)\le\max\left\{f(D),\min\left(\frac{\alpha^2}{2},2\alpha-D\right)\right\},
\]

which is exactly the displayed four-piece upper bound.

It remains to attain each piece. The first piece is attained by padding the one-step policy. For `D in [D_0,D_1]`, choose

\[
a=\frac\alpha2,
\qquad
b=\frac{D-D_0}{\alpha}.
\]

Then `0<=b<=1`, `C_delta=D`, so the low child reflects exactly at zero gap and both branches are at least `alpha^2/2`. For `D in [D_1,2alpha]`, choose

\[
b=1,
\qquad
a=\frac{D-\alpha}{\beta}.
\]

Again `C_delta=D`; the low branch is exactly `2alpha-D`, and the high branch is at least that amount precisely because `D>=D_1`. Finally zero padding attains the last piece. QED.

## 6. B25 — COUNT THE SAME THING TWICE: a reflection-credit charging identity

The fixed-action obstruction from the preceding run said that nonreflection dynamics alone have the wrong average sign for the adversary. The following identity pinpoints exactly where adversarial progress can enter.

Fix one action `(a,b)` with `s=a+b>0` and put

\[
\lambda=\frac{a}{a+b},
\qquad
c=\beta a+\alpha b,
\qquad
\kappa=\frac{2\delta ab}{a+b}.
\]

Define

\[
F=x+\lambda D.
\]

If the higher agent receives the item, then

\[
\Delta x=-\alpha a,
\qquad
\Delta D=\alpha a+\beta b,
\]

and direct substitution yields

\[
\boxed{\Delta F=\kappa.}
\]

If the lower agent receives it without reflection, then

\[
\Delta x=\beta a,
\qquad
\Delta D=-(\beta a+\alpha b),
\]

and again

\[
\boxed{\Delta F=\kappa.}
\]

If the lower agent receives it and reflection occurs (`D<c`), then

\[
\Delta x=D-\alpha b,
\qquad
\Delta D=c-2D,
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
\Delta F
=\kappa+(2\lambda-1)(c-D)
=\kappa-(1-2\lambda)(c-D).
}
\]

Thus for canonical actions `a<=b`, equivalently `lambda<=1/2`, every nonreflection move charges the same positive amount `kappa`, whereas a reflected low-recipient move earns a downward reflection credit

\[
(1-2\lambda)(c-D).
\]

For a varying action sequence `(lambda_t,c_t,kappa_t)`, let

\[
r_t=(1-2\lambda_t)(c_t-D_t)\,\mathbf 1_{\{\text{low-recipient reflection at }t\}}.
\]

The one-step identity is

\[
x_{t+1}-x_t+\lambda_t(D_{t+1}-D_t)=\kappa_t-r_t.
\]

Summing and applying discrete summation by parts gives the exact pathwise charging formula

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
x_T-x_0
={}&\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\kappa_t-r_t)
-\lambda_{T-1}D_T+\lambda_0D_0\\
&+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}D_t(\lambda_t-\lambda_{t-1}).
\end{aligned}}
\]

This counts the same lower-safety displacement in two ways. It identifies the only three channels capable of overcoming the positive nonreflection tax in canonical orientation:

1. accumulated reflection credit `sum r_t`;
2. a large terminal weighted gap `lambda_{T-1}D_T`;
3. downward variation of the action ratio through the final summation-by-parts term.

This identity explains why one fixed nonreflection action cannot solve the game and gives a concrete target for a future critical-ratio inequality.
