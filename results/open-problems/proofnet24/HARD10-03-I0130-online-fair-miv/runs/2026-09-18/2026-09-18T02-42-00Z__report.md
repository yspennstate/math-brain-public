## 3. Preserved target-aware one-dimensional game

For the upper bound it suffices to construct a two-agent adaptive adversary with exact announced MIVs

\[
p_1=p_2=1.
\]

Present three goods that both agents value at exactly `1`. If one agent receives all three, the other has bundle value `B=0`, best missed-good value `R=1`, and total value `V=3`, hence PROP1 factor

\[
\frac{2(B+R)}V=\frac23.
\]

That already lies below every endpoint considered here. Otherwise the allocation is `2--1`. Both agents have missed a unit good, and all later item values will lie in `[0,1]`, so `R_1=R_2=1` forever.

Fix a target

\[
\alpha=1-\delta,
\qquad 0\le\delta<1,
\]

and define each agent's tilted safety

\[
z_i=2(B_i+R_i)-\alpha V_i.
\]

Because `V_i>0`,

\[
z_i\le0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{2(B_i+R_i)}{V_i}\le\alpha.
\]

After the initial `2--1` split, the ordinary safeties are `(1,3)`, both total values equal `3`, and therefore the sorted tilted state is

\[
(x,y)=(1+3\delta,\,3+3\delta),
\qquad D=y-x=2.
\]

For a later good, write `(a,b) in [0,1]^2` for its values to the current lower- and higher-tilted-safety agents. Put

\[
\alpha=1-\delta,
\qquad \beta=1+\delta,
\qquad C_\delta=\beta a+\alpha b.
\]

If the higher agent receives the good, the lower tilted safety decreases by

\[
q_H=\alpha a
\]

and the gap becomes

\[
D_H=D+\alpha a+\beta b.
\]

If the lower agent receives the good, then the new gap is

\[
D_L=|D-C_\delta|
\]

and the decrease of the lower coordinate, after resorting, is the unified expression

\[
q_L=-\beta a+(C_\delta-D)_+.
\]

Indeed, if `D>=C_delta`, there is no reflection and the lower coordinate rises by `beta a`, giving decrease `-beta a`; if `D<C_delta`, the old higher agent becomes lower and the decrease is `alpha b-D`, which equals the displayed formula.

Let `G_h^delta(D)` be the largest decrease of the current lower tilted safety that the adaptive adversary can guarantee within at most `h` further goods from gap `D`. Then

\[
G_0^\delta(D)=0
\]

and for `h>=1`, with `f=G_{h-1}^delta`,

\[
\boxed{
G_h^\delta(D)=
\sup_{0\le a,b\le1}
\min\left\{
\alpha a+f(D+\alpha a+\beta b),
-\beta a+(C_\delta-D)_+ + f(|D-C_\delta|)
\right\}.
}
\]

For every finite `h` the action set is compact and the branch functions are continuous, so the supremum is attained. Thus a Bellman lower bound is realized by an actual finite adaptive adversarial policy; no limiting or randomized strategy is needed.

## 4. B7 — TAKE THE LIMIT: what the fixed-action mean-payoff calculation says

The previous run suggested studying a stationary/additive-eigenvalue limit. Before importing finite-state mean-payoff intuition, I asked whether a **single fixed action** `(a,b)` could itself create persistent negative tilted-safety drift away from the reflection surface.

Assume `a,b>0` and suppose the process stays in the nonreflection regime. Under the high-recipient branch,

\[
\Delta x_H=-\alpha a,
\qquad
\Delta D_H=\alpha a+\beta b.
\]

Under the low-recipient nonreflection branch,

\[
\Delta x_L=+\beta a,
\qquad
\Delta D_L=-(\beta a+\alpha b).
\]

If a long-run branch frequency `p` for the high-recipient branch balances the average gap increment to zero, then

\[
p(\alpha a+\beta b)
=(1-p)(\beta a+\alpha b),
\]

so

\[
p=\frac{\beta a+\alpha b}{2(a+b)}.
\]

The adversary's average **decrease** of the lower coordinate under that balancing frequency is

\[
p\alpha a-(1-p)\beta a.
\]

Substituting the exact value of `p` gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
p\alpha a-(1-p)\beta a
&=\frac{a}{2(a+b)}
\left[\alpha(\beta a+\alpha b)-\beta(\alpha a+\beta b)\right]\\
&=\frac{ab}{2(a+b)}(\alpha^2-\beta^2)\\
&=-\frac{2\delta ab}{a+b}<0.
\end{aligned}
\]

So a single fixed nonreflection action has the **wrong mean sign** for the adversary whenever `delta>0`: an allocation rule that merely balances the gap would, on average, make the lower tilted safety safer rather than drive it toward failure.

This is a useful structural obstruction. Any successful infinite-horizon/additive-eigenvalue strategy at positive `delta` must exploit at least one of:

1. state-dependent changes of `(a,b)`;
2. repeated crossings of the reflection surface `D=C_delta`;
3. a genuinely nonstationary finite-horizon mechanism.

This explains why the exact Bellman policies use many actions and why replacing them by one stationary move is not a legitimate compression.

## 6. B27 — SWAP THE QUANTIFIERS: one certified policy margin implies an interval of targets

The previous `9/10` theorem fixed one target `delta=1/10` and searched until the target was hit. The stronger quantifier direction is to prove that **one finite-horizon margin at one parameter controls a whole interval of nearby parameters**.

### 6.1 Shape lemma for every parameter

**Lemma 1.** For every `delta in [0,1)` and every finite horizon `h`, the function

\[
D\mapsto G_h^\delta(D)
\]

is nonincreasing and `1`-Lipschitz on `[0,infinity)`.

**Proof.** The statement is immediate for `h=0`. Suppose `f=G_{h-1}^delta` is nonincreasing and `1`-Lipschitz.

The high branch

\[
H(D)=\alpha a+f(D+\alpha a+\beta b)
\]

is nonincreasing and `1`-Lipschitz in `D`.

For the low branch define

\[
J_f(t)=t_+ + f(|t|).
\]

If `t<0`, then `J_f(t)=f(-t)`, which is nondecreasing and `1`-Lipschitz in `t`. If `t>=0`, then `J_f(t)=t+f(t)`; for `t_2>t_1>=0`, nonincrease and `1`-Lipschitzness of `f` give

\[
-(t_2-t_1)\le f(t_2)-f(t_1)\le0,
\]

and hence

\[
0\le J_f(t_2)-J_f(t_1)\le t_2-t_1.
\]

The formulas agree at zero, so `J_f` is globally nondecreasing and `1`-Lipschitz. The low branch can be written

\[
L(D)=-\beta a + J_f(C_\delta-D),
\]

and is therefore nonincreasing and `1`-Lipschitz in `D`. Pointwise minima and suprema of functions with these common monotonicity and Lipschitz bounds preserve the same properties. Induction closes. QED.

### 6.2 Parameter-Lipschitz theorem

**Theorem 2.** For any `delta,delta' in [0,1)`, every finite horizon `h`, and every `D>=0`,

\[
\boxed{
|G_h^\delta(D)-G_h^{\delta'}(D)|
\le 2h\,|\delta-\delta'|.
}
\]

**Proof.** Put

\[
\varepsilon=|\delta-\delta'|
\]

and let

\[
E_h=\sup_{D\ge0}|G_h^\delta(D)-G_h^{\delta'}(D)|.
\]

Clearly `E_0=0`. Fix the same action `(a,b)` in the two parameterized Bellman operators.

For the high branch, the direct term `(1-delta)a` changes by at most `epsilon`, while the child gap

\[
D+(1-\delta)a+(1+\delta)b
=D+a+b+\delta(b-a)
\]

changes by at most

\[
\varepsilon|b-a|\le\varepsilon.
\]

By Lemma 1, the continuation value is `1`-Lipschitz in its gap argument, and changing the value function itself costs at most `E_{h-1}`. Thus the high-branch values differ by at most

\[
E_{h-1}+2\varepsilon.
\]

For the low branch, use

\[
-\beta a+J_f(C_\delta-D),
\qquad
C_\delta=a+b+\delta(a-b).
\]

The direct term changes by at most `epsilon`, the argument `C_delta-D` changes by at most `epsilon |a-b|<=epsilon`, and `J_f` is `1`-Lipschitz. Replacing `f` by the other parameter's continuation value adds at most `E_{h-1}`. Therefore the low branches also differ by at most

\[
E_{h-1}+2\varepsilon.
\]

The operations `min` over the two recipient branches and `sup` over actions are nonexpansive under a uniform perturbation. Hence

\[
E_h\le E_{h-1}+2\varepsilon.
\]

Starting from `E_0=0` yields

\[
E_h\le2h\varepsilon.
\]

QED.

This theorem is continuous-action and source-valid. It does not depend on a mesh convergence limit.
