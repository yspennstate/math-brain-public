## 5.1 Pathwise comparison

Regularize

\[
K_\varepsilon=K+\varepsilon I,
\qquad
C_\varepsilon=M+B+\varepsilon I.
\]

Since `A>=M`,

\[
K_\varepsilon\succeq C_\varepsilon>0,
\qquad
K_\varepsilon^{-1}\preceq C_\varepsilon^{-1}.
\]

Define

\[
T_\varepsilon=\operatorname{Tr}(K_\varepsilon^{-1}A).
\]

Using `K_epsilon=A+B+epsilon I`,

\[
T_\varepsilon
=n-\operatorname{Tr}(K_\varepsilon^{-1}(B+\varepsilon I)).
\]

Inverse order and positivity of `B+epsilon I` imply

\[
T_\varepsilon
\ge
n-\operatorname{Tr}(C_\varepsilon^{-1}(B+\varepsilon I))
=
\operatorname{Tr}(C_\varepsilon^{-1}M).
\tag{5.3}
\]

Hilbert--Schmidt Cauchy, applied to `C_epsilon^{-1/2}M^{1/2}` and `C_epsilon^{1/2}M^{1/2}`, gives

\[
(\operatorname{Tr}M)^2
\le
\operatorname{Tr}(C_\varepsilon^{-1}M)
\operatorname{Tr}(C_\varepsilon M).
\tag{5.4}
\]

By the addition theorem, every diagonal entry of `M` equals `a`, so

\[
\operatorname{Tr}M=na.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Letting `epsilon` decrease to zero in (5.3)--(5.4) yields

\[
\boxed{
T_{\le L}
\ge
\frac{n^2a^2}{\operatorname{Tr}(M^2)+\operatorname{Tr}(BM)}.
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

The Gaussian kernel Gram matrix is invertible almost surely for distinct iid spherical inputs, so the left side has the intended unregularized meaning; the right side is obtained by monotone/continuous limiting from the regularized inequality.

## 5.2 Exact first moments of the denominator

Let

\[
m_\mu(x,y)

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\operatorname{Tr}(M^2)
=na^2+n(n-1)b.

\tag{5.7}

=

\[
\boxed{

Again, this uses orthogonality, not independence of the random matrices `B` and `M`.

# 6. New optimization theorem: the flat floor is second-order optimal

## `P23-T3-FLOOR-SECONDORDER-OPTIMALITY-46 = PROVED`

Let

\[
\lambda=\lambda_L,
\qquad
N=N_L,
\qquad
D=\sum_{s\le L}N_s,
\qquad
p=N/D.
\]

Also define

\[
c=(n-1)/D,
\qquad
\beta=\tau/(\lambda D).
\]

The floor certificate is

\[
\ell_{\rm floor}
=
\frac1{1+c+\beta}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

We upper-bound every certificate (5.10).

## 6.1 Case `a<=lambda D`

Weighted Cauchy gives

\[
a^2
=\left(\sum_{s\le L}N_s\mu_s\right)^2
\le
D\sum_{s\le L}N_s\mu_s^2
=Db.
\]

Thus

\[
\frac{(n-1)b}{a^2}\ge c.
\]

Also `a<=lambda D` implies

\[
\frac\tau a\ge\frac\tau{\lambda D}=\beta.
\]

Hence the denominator in (5.10) is at least `1+c+beta`, and

\[
\ell(\mu)\le\ell_{\rm floor}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

So any improvement must have `a>lambda D`: it must put extra mass into the larger lower-degree eigenvalues.

## 6.2 Case `a>lambda D`

Write

\[
a=\lambda D+\delta,
\qquad\delta>0.
\]

The top degree has the hard cap `mu_L<=lambda`. Let

\[
x=N\mu_L\le N\lambda
\]

be its contribution to `a`. Weighted Cauchy on the lower degrees gives

\[
b
\ge
\frac{x^2}{N}
+
\frac{(a-x)^2}{D-N}.
\tag{6.3}
\]

As a function of `x`, the right side is minimized unconstrained at `x=Na/D`. Since `a/D>lambda`, this unconstrained minimizer lies strictly above `Nlambda`. Hence over `x<=Nlambda` the minimum occurs at `x=Nlambda`, giving

\[
\boxed{
b
\ge
N\lambda^2
+
\frac{(a-N\lambda)^2}{D-N}.}
\tag{6.4}
\]

Set

\[
u=\frac\delta{\lambda D}>0,
\qquad
v=\frac{u}{1+u}\in(0,1).
\]

Then `a=lambda D(1+u)`. A direct simplification of (6.4) yields

\[
\boxed{
\frac{Db}{a^2}
\ge
1+\frac{p}{1-p}v^2.
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

Also

\[
\frac\tau a
=\frac\beta{1+u}
=\beta(1-v).
\tag{6.6}
\]

Therefore the denominator `rho(mu)` in (5.10) obeys

\[
\begin{aligned}
\rho(\mu)
&:=1+\frac{(n-1)b}{a^2}+\frac\tau a\\
&\ge
1+c+\beta
+
\frac{cp}{1-p}v^2
-
\beta v.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.7}
\]

Define

\[
A_0=\frac{cp}{1-p},
\qquad
R_0=1+c+\beta.
\]

Completing the square gives

\[
A_0v^2-\beta v
\ge
-\frac{\beta^2}{4A_0}.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\rho(\mu)
\ge
R_0-\Delta,
\qquad
\Delta
=
\frac{\beta^2(1-p)}{4cp}.
}
\tag{6.8}
\]

Whenever `R_0>Delta`, inversion gives

\[
\boxed{
\ell(\mu)\le\frac1{R_0-\Delta}.
}
\tag{6.9}
\]

Together with the feasible flat floor,

\[
\boxed{
0\le
\sup_\mu\ell(\mu)-\ell_{\rm floor}
\le
\frac{\Delta}{R_0(R_0-\Delta)}.
}
\tag{6.10}
\]

This proves the finite-dimensional optimization theorem.
