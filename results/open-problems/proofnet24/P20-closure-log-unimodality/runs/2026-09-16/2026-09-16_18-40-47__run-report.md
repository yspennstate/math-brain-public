## 3. New admissible input — exact definition

Define a probability measure `mu` by specifying its logarithmic pushforward. Let

\[
h(y)=
\begin{cases}
\frac1{24},& |y|\le 8,\\[2mm]
\frac1{36},& 8<|y|\le 14,\\[2mm]
0,& |y|>14.
\end{cases}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Then `log_* mu` has density `h`, equivalently

\[
\mu(dx)=rac{1}{24x}{\bf 1}_{[e^{-8},e^8]}(x)\,dx
+\frac{1}{36x}{\bf 1}_{[e^{-14},e^{-8})\cup(e^8,e^{14}]}(x)\,dx.
\tag{3.2}
\]

### 3.1 Exact normalization

The inner interval in logarithmic coordinates has length `16`; the two outer pieces have total length `12`. Hence

\[
16\cdot\frac1{24}+12\cdot\frac1{36}
=\frac23+\frac13=1.
\tag{3.3}
\]

This is checked with `fractions.Fraction` in the archived checker.

### 3.2 Multiplicative symmetry

The density `h` is even. Thus `Y~log_*mu` satisfies `Y =_d -Y`, and therefore `e^Y =_d e^{-Y}`. Hence

\[
\boxed{\mu=\mu^{-1}.}
\tag{3.4}
\]

### 3.3 Log-unimodality

The density in (3.1) is nondecreasing on `(-infinity,0]` and nonincreasing on `[0,infinity)`: it rises from `0` to `1/36`, then to `1/24`, and reverses symmetrically. Consequently `log_*mu` is unimodal with mode `0` in the source's sense, and

\[
\boxed{\mu\text{ is multiplicatively symmetric and log-unimodal with mode }1.}
\tag{3.5}
\]

Thus the input is admissible for exact Problem 4.3.

## 4. Exact transform of the two-level input

For `X>0` put

\[
F_X(z):=\int_{-X}^{X}\frac{du}{1-ze^u}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

With `t=e^u`, direct integration gives, on the principal branch for `z in C^+`,

\[
\boxed{
F_X(z)=2X-\log(1-ze^X)+\log(1-ze^{-X}).
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Indeed an antiderivative of `1/[t(1-zt)]` is `log t-log(1-zt)`. The checker separately certifies that all logarithm arguments used in the four final root boxes remain strictly off the principal branch cut.

Write

\[
M_\mu(z):=\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{1}{1-zx}\,d\mu(x).
\tag{4.3}
\]

Because

\[
h=\frac1{72}{\bf 1}_{[-8,8]}+\frac1{36}{\bf 1}_{[-14,14]},
\]

we have the exact formula

\[
\boxed{
M_\mu(z)=\frac{F_8(z)+2F_{14}(z)}{72}.

\tag{4.4}
\]

The usual positive-line transforms satisfy

\[
\psi_\mu=M_\mu-1,
\qquad
\boxed{\eta_\mu(z)=\frac{\psi_\mu(z)}{1+\psi_\mu(z)}=1-\frac1{M_\mu(z)}.}
\tag{4.5}
\]

All derivatives used below are obtained by differentiating (4.2)--(4.5) analytically; no finite differencing is used.

## 5. Self-convolution inverse equation and physical-branch proof

Let

\[
\rho:=\mu\boxtimes\mu.
\tag{5.1}
\]

For local inverse variable `w`, multiplicativity of the positive-line `Sigma` transform gives

\[
\eta_\rho^{-1}(w)
=\frac{\eta_\mu^{-1}(w)^2}{w}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Set `q=eta_mu^{-1}(w)`. Then the boundary inverse problem for `rho` is

\[
\boxed{
H(q):=\frac{q^2}{\eta_\mu(q)}=s,
\qquad
w=\eta_\mu(q).
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

The earlier P20 log-uniform reports used the same exact inverse identity; P19 independently passed that identity as a component audit. Here I also close the branch issue for the new input directly from general subordination theory rather than by assuming that an arbitrary algebraic root of (5.3) is physical.

### 5.1 Why an upper-half-plane simple root is the physical boundary branch

Put

\[
h_\mu(q):=\frac{\eta_\mu(q)}{q}.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Arizmendi--Hasebe--Kitagawa Proposition 3.3 proves for a nondegenerate positive-line law that

\[
h_\mu(\mathbb C^+)\subseteq\mathbb C^+,
\tag{5.5}
\]

and that, for `z in C^+`,

\[
f_z(v):=z h_\mu(z h_\mu(v))
\tag{5.6}
\]

is an analytic self-map of `C^+`; their fixed-point construction identifies the first subordination function as its unique interior fixed point in the nondegenerate case.

Now let `s>0` and suppose `q_0 in C^+` is a simple root of (5.3). Since

\[
H(q_0)=s
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q_0=s h_\mu(q_0),
\tag{5.7}
\]

and `H'(q_0) != 0`, the analytic implicit-function theorem gives a local analytic branch `q(z)` for `z` near `s` with

\[
H(q(z))=z,
\qquad q(s)=q_0.
\tag{5.8}
\]

Because `Im q_0>0`, after shrinking the neighborhood we have `q(z) in C^+` for `z in C^+` close to `s`. Equation (5.8) becomes

\[
q(z)=z h_\mu(q(z)),
\tag{5.9}
\]

so in particular

\[
f_z(q(z))
=z h_\mu(z h_\mu(q(z)))
=z h_\mu(q(z))
=q(z).
\tag{5.10}
\]

Thus `q(z)` is the interior fixed point singled out by the subordination theorem and therefore equals the physical first subordination function there. The second subordination function is `z h_mu(q(z))=q(z)` as well. Theorem 1.9 gives continuous boundary extension of the subordination functions, so `q_0` is the physical nontangential boundary value at `s`.

This argument is local and only needs the simple-root property and `Im q_0>0`, both certified below. It does **not** identify a root by a plot or by an unverified continuation choice.

## 6. Density and derivative formula in logarithmic coordinates

Let `x=e^y` and `s=e^{-y}=1/x`. For a positive-line law with transform

\[
M_\rho(s)=\int\frac{1}{1-st}\,d\rho(t),
\tag{6.1}
\]

Stieltjes inversion at an absolutely continuous point gives

\[
\boxed{
g(y):=e^y f_\rho(e^y)
=\frac1\pi\Im M_\rho(e^{-y}+i0).
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Since `eta=1-1/M`, if `w=eta_rho(s+i0)=eta_mu(q)`, then

\[
M_\rho(s+i0)=\frac1{1-w},
\qquad
\boxed{
g(y)=\frac1\pi\Im\frac1{1-w}.}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Differentiate the exact boundary equation

\[
H(q(y))=e^{-y}.
\tag{6.4}
\]

At a simple root,

\[
q_y=-\frac{e^{-y}}{H'(q)},
\qquad
w_y=\eta_\mu'(q)q_y,
\tag{6.5}
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
g'(y)=\frac1\pi\Im\frac{w_y}{(1-w)^2}.}
\tag{6.6}
\]

Every quantity in (6.6) is evaluated by analytic formulas (4.2)--(4.5), not by sampling a plotted density.

## 8. The new four-mode theorem

### Claim `P20-I0081-TWOLEVEL-FOURMODE-04 = PROVED`

For `mu` defined by (3.1)--(3.2), the logarithmic density of

\[
\rho=\mu\boxtimes\mu
\]

has at least four distinct modes. Hence `rho` is not log-unimodal and Problem 4.3 is false as stated.

### Proof

**Step 1: admissibility.** Sections 3.1--3.3 prove exactly that `mu` is a multiplicatively symmetric log-unimodal probability measure.

**Step 2: continuous output log-density.** The input is atomless and compactly supported in `(0,infinity)`. Arizmendi--Hasebe--Kitagawa Appendix A applies: the function

\[
x\mapsto x\frac{d\rho}{dx}(x)
\]

has a bounded continuous version on the positive axis relevant here. Therefore

\[
g(y)=e^y\frac{d\rho}{dx}(e^y)
\]

is continuous on the finite intervals used below. At the four certified points it is moreover analytic locally, because the physical subordination root is simple and the density is positive.

**Step 3: one positive-side mode in `(6,10)`.** By compactness and continuity, `g` attains a maximum on `[6,10]`. Since `g'(6)>0`, points immediately to the right of `6` have larger value than `g(6)`, so `6` is not a maximizer. Since `g'(10)<0`, points immediately to the left of `10` have larger value than `g(10)`, so `10` is not a maximizer. Hence an interval maximizer lies at some

\[
y_1\in(6,10).
\tag{8.1}
\]

Any interior maximizer of a closed interval is a local maximum of `g`; thus `y_1` is a log-density mode.

**Step 4: a second distinct positive-side mode in `(20,22)`.** The same argument using `g'(20)>0` and `g'(22)<0` gives a local maximum

\[
y_2\in(20,22).
\tag{8.2}
\]

The intervals are disjoint, so `y_1 != y_2`.

**Step 5: inversion symmetry creates two mirror modes.** Let `X,Y` be free positive variables with law `mu`. Then

\[
(X^{1/2}YX^{1/2})^{-1}
=X^{-1/2}Y^{-1}X^{-1/2}.
\tag{8.3}
\]

The variables `X^{-1},Y^{-1}` remain free and both have law `mu^{-1}=mu`; cyclicity of the trace gives the usual positive free-product law. Therefore

\[
\rho^{-1}=\mu^{-1}\boxtimes\mu^{-1}=\rho.
\tag{8.4}
\]

Equivalently `log_*rho` is symmetric about zero, so its continuous density satisfies

\[
g(-y)=g(y).
\tag{8.5}
\]

Thus `-y_1` and `-y_2` are two further local maxima. Since `y_1,y_2>0` and `y_1 != y_2`, the four points

\[
-y_2,-y_1,y_1,y_2
\]

are distinct. Therefore

\[
\boxed{N_{\rm modes}(\log_*\rho)\ge4.}
\tag{8.6}
\]

This contradicts the conclusion of Problem 4.3. QED.

No assertion of **exactly** four modes is made. There may be additional modes outside or between the certified intervals.
