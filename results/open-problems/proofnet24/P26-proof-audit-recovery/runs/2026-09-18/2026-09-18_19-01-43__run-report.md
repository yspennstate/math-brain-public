## 3. Exact owner theorem

Let `p = 5 (mod 24)` be prime and put

\[
V=\{x\in\mathbb F_p\setminus\{0,1\}:\chi(x)=\chi(x-1)=1\},
\qquad |V|=\frac{p-5}{4}.
\]

Let the anharmonic group `G=S_3` act on `V`, generated by

\[
r(x)=1-x,\qquad s(x)=x^{-1}.
\]

Fix `y in V`. In this congruence class the orbit `Gy` is free of size six. For a prescribed sign vector

\[
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_g)_{g\in G}\in\{\pm1\}^6,
\]

let `N_y(\varepsilon)` be the number of `x in F_p` satisfying

\[
\chi(x)=\chi(x-1)=1,
\qquad
\chi(x-g y)=\varepsilon_g\quad(g\in G).
\]

Claim 13 asserts the uniform bound

\[
\boxed{
\left|N_y(\varepsilon)-\frac p{256}\right|
\le \frac{769}{256}\sqrt p+8.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Writing `q=(p-5)/24` for the number of free `S_3` vertex orbits, and `k_{ij}` for the number of edges from one source-orbit vertex to the six vertices of a distinct target orbit, the consequence is

\[
\boxed{
\#\{i\ne j:k_{ij}=k\}
=\frac{\binom6k}{64}q+O(\sqrt p),
\qquad k=0,\ldots,6.
}
\tag{3.2}
\]

The claim is explicitly local/block pseudorandomness. It does not assert a global operator-norm theorem or solve Randomstrasse Conjecture 27.

## 4. #2 ATTACK THE NEGATION FIRST — boundary, freeness, and root tests

I attacked the assumptions that could make (3.1) silently false.

### 4.1 Small-prime boundary

At `p=5`, the localized set `V` is empty, so there is no `y` to quantify over and the theorem is vacuous. This is not a hidden strict-inequality failure of the kind found in P04 Claim 26 in the preceding audit cycle.

At `p=29`, `|V|=6`, hence there is exactly one free `S_3` orbit. Formula (3.1) still makes sense, while (3.2) has no distinct source orbit to count. Its `O(sqrt p)` formulation remains valid. The new exact checker explicitly covers both boundaries.

### 4.2 Freeness of the anharmonic action

For `p=5 (mod 24)`, we have `p=5 (mod 8)`, so

\[
\left(\frac2p\right)=-1.
\]

The three transpositions of the anharmonic `S_3` action have fixed-point candidates represented by `1/2`, `-1`, and `2` (besides excluded `0,1` under conjugacy). None lies in `V`: the relevant membership tests force a factor of `chi(2)=-1`.

Also `p=2 (mod 3)`, so the order-three fixed-point equation

\[
x^2-x+1=0
\]

has no roots in `F_p`. Hence every `y in V` has orbit size six, and `0`, `1`, and the six points `g y` are eight distinct roots. This removes the main squarefreeness failure mode in the character-sum argument.

### 4.3 Indicator and root correction

Away from those eight roots the exact prescribed-pattern indicator is

\[
2^{-8}(1+\chi(x))(1+\chi(x-1))
\prod_{g\in G}(1+\varepsilon_g\chi(x-g y)).
\tag{4.1}
\]

At a root, `chi(0)=0`, so (4.1) is not literally the desired `0/1` indicator. There are exactly eight such points, hence deleting them costs at most eight in absolute count. No larger exceptional set is hidden.

### 4.4 Character-sum obstruction search

After expanding (4.1), the empty subset gives `p`. Every one-factor term is a complete nontrivial linear-character sum and equals zero. Every term using `r>=2` of the eight distinct linear factors is the quadratic character of a squarefree polynomial of degree `r`, hence the standard Weil bound gives

\[
\left|\sum_x\chi\!\left(\prod_{a\in S}(x-a)\right)\right|
\le(r-1)\sqrt p.
\]

Because the polynomial is squarefree, no even-cardinality subset becomes a perfect square and escapes the Weil bound. Summing the worst-case Fourier coefficients gives

\[
\sum_{r=2}^8\binom8r(r-1)=769.
\]

Together with the eight-root correction this is exactly (3.1). I found no counterexample to the claim.
