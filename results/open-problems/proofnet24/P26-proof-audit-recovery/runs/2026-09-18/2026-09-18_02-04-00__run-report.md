# 2. Independent reconstruction of the finite-convolution inputs

The first possible hidden gap is whether a bounded density of the `q`-fold convolution is strong enough to supply the one-dimensional / planar microscopic inputs used later. It is.

Let `X_1,...,X_q` be iid copies of a real entry `X`. If all `X_i` lie in an interval of radius `r` around some center, then their sum lies in an interval of radius `qr`. Since the density of the sum is bounded by `M`,

\[
Q_X(r)^q\le 2Mqr,
\]

hence

\[
\boxed{Q_X(r)\le(2Mqr)^{1/q}.}
\]

In the complex plane the same argument with a disk gives

\[
Q_X(r)^q\le \pi M q^2r^2,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{Q_X(r)\le(\pi Mq^2r^2)^{1/q}.}
\]

The Fourier-power input is also correctly normalized. Bobkov--Goetze, arXiv:2407.20744, Proposition 5.3 gives the required density-to-characteristic-function power integrability. Applied to the `q`-fold sum and rescaled back to one entry, it yields the package's real and complex bounds, in particular the correct `r^{-1/2}` real and `r^{-1}` planar decay at powers `r >= 2q`.

These two consequences are exactly the fixed microscopic regularity needed later: concentration control and integrable characteristic-function powers. No fourth moment is being smuggled into this step.

# 3. Projection densities and the lower extreme

I independently checked the normalization of the Brascamp--Lieb/Fourier projection-density bounds used in the sparse-kernel lower extreme.

For a complex `d`-dimensional projection with coefficient matrix `L`, the low-leverage density ceiling has the form

\[
\|f_{LX}\|_\infty
\le
\frac{(2eqM)^d}{\det_{\mathbb C}(LL^*)},
\]

while the real analogue is

\[
\|f_{LX}\|_\infty
\le
\frac{(\sqrt{2eq}\,M)^d}{\sqrt{\det(LL^*)}}.
\]

The fixed `2q`-basis versions likewise have the expected determinant exponents: `1/q` in the complex case and `1/(2q)` in the real case. The determinant powers agree with real dimension, so there is no missing square root in passing between the real and complex formulations.

The recursive extraction of `2q` disjoint `k`-bases from a useful normal is legitimate. The sparse-kernel avoidance input prevents concentration on supports of size `o(m/log m)`; after at most `(2q-1)k` deletions, Cauchy--Binet still supplies the next basis. The loss from determinant thresholds, support nets, and choices is at most

\[
\exp\{O(k_m\log m)\}=e^{o(m)}.

The candidate row-subset count is

\[
\binom{N_m}{m+k_m-1}=\exp\{h(\gamma)m+o(m)\}.

Combining the projection-density estimate, the negative-second-moment reduction, and the candidate entropy therefore gives the lower-extreme exponent on the claimed scale. The negative second moment is used in the safe direction: if the least singular value is at most `t`, then some relevant column distance is at most `sqrt(m)t`.

\[

For the real cleaned law, the variance lower bound follows directly from the bounded density of the `q`-fold sum: choosing a small fixed interval forces a fixed mass outside that interval, producing a positive variance lower bound depending only on `q` and the cleaned density ceiling. # 9. NEW-METHOD #27 — swap the quantifiers

Three quantifier swaps are dangerous and were checked explicitly.

First, the theorem is

\[
\exists q<\infty,\ M<\infty\ \text{fixed for the triangular family}
\]

with the ceiling uniform in `m`. It is **not** the weaker assertion that for every `m` there merely exist `q_m,M_m`. Allowing either to drift can destroy the uniform Fourier and local-limit constants.

Second, in the upper proof the correct order is

\[
\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists\beta<\gamma\ \exists\lambda
\]

with `beta` chosen sufficiently close to `gamma`, followed by `lambda` between `h(gamma)-epsilon` and `h(beta)`. There is no claim that one fixed `beta<gamma` works simultaneously for all `epsilon`.

Third, the theorem permits a sequence `k_m` satisfying `k_m log m=o(m)` and therefore genuinely strengthens the fixed-`r` regime. The proof losses are uniform only because every combinatorial/net/determinant overhead is `e^{O(k_m log m)}=e^{o(m)}`. Swapping this to an arbitrary `k_m=o(m)` would be unjustified.

These checks remove the main ways the theorem could look stronger on paper than the proof actually supports.
