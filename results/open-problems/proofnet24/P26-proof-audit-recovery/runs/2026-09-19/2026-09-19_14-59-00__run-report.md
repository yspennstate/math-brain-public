## 6. Method #1 — CHANGE THE CATEGORY

The most informative change of category is from split-reliability polynomials to a discrete derivative of ordinary reliability with respect to one distinguished edge. The bridge identity makes

\[
\Delta_{st}R(H;p)
\]

the probability mass carried by states in which `st` is pivotal for all-terminal connectivity, up to the standard edge-occupation factor. In reliability language this is a Birnbaum-style component-importance functional for a marked edge.

This translation explains why the earlier max-minus-two and max-minus-three arguments worked and why max-minus-four does not follow automatically. Classical reliability-improving transformations order the scalar `R(G;p)`. The new target orders a derivative/difference of two scalar reliabilities with the same marked edge present versus absent. A theorem in the first category need not survive differentiation into the second.

Outcome of #1: useful reclassification, but no theorem claim. The missing object is a marked-edge importance monotonicity theorem, not another ordinary UMRG comparison.

## 7. Method #12 — FIND WHAT IS CONSERVED

The exact terminal-edge swap used in the finite `T_{9,32}` proof has a genuine conservation law: a successful split state never uses the terminal edge `st`, so replacing `st` by a missing edge preserves the selected edge set and its cardinality. That is why P26 could strengthen the finite reduction coefficientwise.

What is *not* conserved by ordinary swing/Kelmans surgery is the subset of connected states for which the distinguished edge is pivotal. A surgery may increase total reliability on `G` and also increase total reliability on `G-st`, while changing those two quantities by different amounts. The pivotal-state mass is the difference of the two gains.

Thus the conservation analysis separates two transformations that superficially look similar:

- terminal-edge swap: preserves successful split states and edge count, hence coefficientwise injection is available;
- reliability-improving swing surgery: preserves/controls enough structure to order total reliability, but the existing theorem does not preserve the `st`-pivotal subset.

Outcome of #12: the useful invariant is the successful split-state set under terminal-edge swap; the missing invariant is a surgery-compatible marked-pivotal-state structure.

## 8. Method #20 — FIND WHY THE ALGORITHM FAILS

Suppose a terminal-preserving surgery `S` is used to move an arbitrary three-edge defect toward the terminal-centered star and commutes with deletion of `st`. To prove marked-edge improvement one needs

\[
\boxed{
\big(R(SG;p)-R(G;p)\big)
\ge
\big(R(S(G-st);p)-R(G-st;p)\big).
}
\tag{GAIN}
\]

The ordinary swing-surgery theorem proves that each parenthesized gain has the desired nonnegative sign. It does not compare the two gains to one another. That precise comparison is the point where the obvious algorithm fails.

Equivalently, an injection establishing ordinary reliability improvement may map connected states that do not use or need `st` in a way that overwhelms its action on `st`-pivotal states. Without a marked injection, `(GAIN)` does not follow from two scalar inequalities.

Outcome of #20: the failure is not a missing finite case or another lemma about total reliability. The proof architecture lacks a coupling/injection that carries the distinguished-edge pivotal event through the surgery.
