## 3. Refutation-first stress tests before proving

Before accepting the proof route I tried to break the theorem at the places where the open-set geometry and compactness are easiest to misuse.

1. **Strict open-transform threshold.** For the one-dimensional open set `K=(-1,1)` and `v=1`,
   \[
   \mathcal T_1K=((K-1)\cap(K+1))+(-2,2)=\varnothing,
   \]
   because the intersection is empty. A proof that silently replaced the open transform by the interior of the closed transform would therefore be wrong. The source explicitly distinguishes this threshold behavior, and the audited argument below preserves the strict-open convention.

2. **One density versus one density per direction.** Controlling each directional variation with a different density would not close the iteration. I tried to break the argument by retaining only scalar inequalities `inf_rho V_{u_i}(rho)<=b_i`. The obstruction is real: separate minimizers need not coincide. The common-density step must swap the quantifiers using convex separation. Section 7 below rederives that step.

3. **Degenerate anisotropy.** The finite mixture
   \[
   H(\xi)=\sum_i\alpha_i|u_i\cdot\xi|

The proof must regularize and then take the limit with fixed test densities. Section 8 checks this explicitly.

4. **Endpoint equality.** Nonemptiness cannot be manufactured by an equality at a closed fiber threshold. The retained-height calculation must supply the source's strict/open geometry without silently changing the transform definition. The exact L=3 calculation is checked below and by the archived checker.

## 6. Anisotropic Cheeger costs on the horizontal sections

For nonnegative coefficients `alpha_i`, define the finite anisotropy

\[
H_\alpha(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_i|u_i\cdot\xi|
\tag{6.1}
\]

and for an admissible probability density `eta` define

\[
E_{H_\alpha}(\eta)
=\sum_i\alpha_iV_{u_i}(\eta).
\tag{6.2}
\]

For a bounded open convex domain `D`, let

\[
h_{H_\alpha}(D)
:=\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal P(D)}E_{H_\alpha}(\eta).
\tag{6.3}
\]

Let `D_r` be the horizontal section of `B^*` at absolute height `r`. Vertical symmetry and convexity imply that the sections are nested as `r` decreases and satisfy Minkowski interpolation:

\[
(1-\theta)D_r+\theta D_s
\subseteq
D_{(1-\theta)r+\theta s}.
\tag{6.4}
\]

The analytic input checked in Section 8 is that `h_H` is convex under Minkowski interpolation of convex domains. Together with domain monotonicity, this makes

\[
g(r):=h_{H_\alpha}(D_r)
\tag{6.5}
\]

convex and nondecreasing in `r>=0` over the vertical support.

Write

\[
q(s)=\int R^*(y,s)\,dy.
\]

Slicing and lower semicontinuity give

\[
\int E_{H_\alpha}(R^*_s)\,ds
\le
E_{H_\alpha}^{\rm horizontal}(R^*)
\le\sum_i\alpha_i b_i.
\tag{6.6}
\]

Whenever `q(s)>0`, the normalized slice is supported in `D_|s|`, hence

\[
q(s)g(|s|)\le E_{H_\alpha}(R^*_s).
\tag{6.7}
\]

Integrating and applying Jensen to the probability density `q` gives

\[
g(M)\le\int q(s)g(|s|)\,ds
\le\sum_i\alpha_i b_i.
\tag{6.8}
\]

Because `M>=1` and `g` is nondecreasing,

\[
\boxed{
h_{H_\alpha}(D_1)\le\sum_i\alpha_i b_i
\qquad\text{for every }\alpha_i\ge0.
}
\tag{6.9}
\]

In particular the height-one section is nonempty; otherwise its Cheeger cost cannot be finite in this construction.

## 7. NEW-METHOD #27 — swap the quantifiers by convex separation

Equation (6.9) says that **for every nonnegative weighted combination of directions there exists an admissible density with small weighted energy**. Proposition 4.1 needs the stronger-looking statement that **there exists one density that works for every direction in the finite list**.

Define the upward variation set

\[
\mathcal S=
\left\{z\in\mathbb R^N:
\exists\eta\in\mathcal P(D_1),\
V_{u_i}(\eta)\le z_i\ \forall i
\right\}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

This set is convex because variation is convex under mixtures of densities, and it is coordinatewise upward closed. The inclusion of the coordinate basis among the `u_i`, with finite target budgets, gives a uniform full-BV bound for any sequence whose variation vectors remain in a bounded coordinate box. On the fixed bounded support, BV compactness gives an `L^1`-convergent subsequence; lower semicontinuity of each directional variation shows that the relevant lower boundary of `S` is closed.

Suppose the target vector

\[
b=(b_1,\ldots,b_N)
\]

were not in `S`. Strict finite-dimensional separation of `b` from the closed upward convex set `S` gives a nonzero normal `alpha`. Upward closure forces

\[
\alpha_i\ge0\quad\text{for all }i;
\tag{7.2}
\]

otherwise one could send a coordinate of an element of `S` to `+infinity` and violate separation. The separating inequality would then imply

\[
\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal P(D_1)}
\sum_i\alpha_iV_{u_i}(\eta)
>
\sum_i\alpha_i b_i,
\tag{7.3}
\]

contradicting (6.9). Therefore `b in S`, i.e. there is **one** density on `D_1=T_vK` satisfying every finite directional budget simultaneously. This proves Proposition 4.1.

This is the essential quantifier swap; using separately optimized densities would not be enough.

## 8. Audit of the deep analytic input: Cheeger convexity

The finite-direction proof still depends on one theorem-sized analytic bridge: Minkowski convexity of the anisotropic Cheeger value for the finite mixture (6.1). I audited this rather than treating it as a black-box title citation.

### 8.1 Smooth strongly convex anisotropy

For a smooth strongly convex Finsler norm `F`, Wang–Xia (2011) prove a Brunn–Minkowski inequality for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue `lambda_{p,F}` of the Finsler `p`-Laplacian. In the inverse-power form used by Guo–Fang–Lu,

\[
\lambda_{p,F}((1-\theta)K+\theta L)^{-1/p}
\ge
(1-\theta)\lambda_{p,F}(K)^{-1/p}
+
\theta\lambda_{p,F}(L)^{-1/p}.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Since `x -> x^{-p}` is convex on the positive half-line, (8.1) implies the weaker linear upper convexity needed here:

\[
\lambda_{p,F}((1-\theta)K+\theta L)
\le
(1-\theta)\lambda_{p,F}(K)+\theta\lambda_{p,F}(L).
\tag{8.2}
\]

Guo–Fang–Lu Appendix A also supplies a direct eigenfunction route for the regularized norms, using the log-concavity/regularity framework of Mosconi–Riey–Squassina (2024). I found no sign or normalization conflict in the hypotheses used for that regularized setting.

### 8.2 The `p downarrow 1` limit

Let `lambda_{p,F}(K)` be normalized with `int |u|^p=1`. Put `w=|u|^p`, so `int w=1`. By the chain rule and Hölder,

\[
E_F(w)
=p\int |u|^{p-1}F(\nabla u)
\le
p\left(\int F(\nabla u)^p\right)^{1/p}.
\tag{8.3}
\]

Taking an eigenfunction/minimizer gives

\[
h_F(K)\le p\lambda_{p,F}(K)^{1/p}.
\tag{8.4}
\]

Hence

\[
\liminf_{p\downarrow1}\lambda_{p,F}(K)\ge h_F(K).
\tag{8.5}
\]

Conversely, fix a smooth compactly supported probability density whose F-energy is within `epsilon` of `h_F(K)`. Insert an appropriate positive power of that fixed test density into the `p`-Rayleigh quotient and let `p downarrow1`; dominated convergence gives

\[
\limsup_{p\downarrow1}\lambda_{p,F}(K)\le h_F(K)+\epsilon.
\tag{8.6}
\]

Then `epsilon downarrow0` gives

\[
\lambda_{p,F}(K)\longrightarrow h_F(K).
\tag{8.7}
\]

Passing (8.2) to the limit proves Minkowski convexity for smooth strongly convex `F`.

### 8.3 Degenerate finite mixtures

The actual anisotropy (6.1) is not necessarily smooth or strictly convex. Regularize by

\[
H_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\xi)
=
\sum_i\alpha_i
\sqrt{(u_i\cdot\xi)^2+\epsilon^2\|\xi\|_2^2}.
\tag{8.8}
\]

For nonzero `alpha` this is a smooth strongly convex norm away from the origin and

\[
H_\alpha(\xi)
\le H_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\xi)
\le H_\alpha(\xi)
+\epsilon\Big(\sum_i\alpha_i\Big)\|\xi\|_2.
\tag{8.9}
\]

Apply the smooth convexity theorem at fixed `epsilon`. To send `epsilon downarrow0`, use fixed BV/smooth near-minimizers for the limiting `H_alpha` problem in the upper-bound direction and lower semicontinuity in the lower-bound direction. No uniform estimate for the `epsilon`-dependent eigenfunctions is needed. Thus Minkowski convexity survives the degenerate finite-mixture limit.

This closes the analytic dependency required in Section 6.

\[

\tag{9.4}

\mathbb E\left[\left.\left|\sum_i u_iT_i\right|\right|S\right]

\tag{9.7}

\[
V_u(\rho_C)
\le

\tag{9.8}
