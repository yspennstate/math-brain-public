## 2. Pure quadratic forms and range-compatible affine terms have exactly the same best constant

### Theorem 2.1 (exact translation equivalence)

Fix any real symmetric `A`. Let `C_pure(A)` be the least `C` such that, for every independent product law with coordinate densities bounded by `1` and every bounded interval `I`,

`P{X^T A X in I} <= C |I|`.

Let `C_range-aff(A)` be the corresponding least constant when `X^TAX` is replaced by

`X^T A X + b^T X + c`,

uniformly over all `c in R` and all `b in Range(A)`. Then

`C_range-aff(A) = C_pure(A)`.

This equality is valid whether `A` is definite, indefinite, or singular.

### Proof

The inequality `C_pure(A)<=C_range-aff(A)` is immediate by taking `b=0,c=0`.

Conversely, if `b in Range(A)`, choose `a` with `2Aa=b`. Then

`x^T A x + b^T x + c = (x+a)^T A (x+a) + c - a^T A a`.

If `X` has independent coordinate densities `f_i<=1`, then `Y=X+a` has independent coordinate densities `g_i(y)=f_i(y-a_i)<=1`. Adding the scalar `c-a^TAa` only translates the output interval. Hence the pure bound with constant `C_pure(A)` applies and gives `C_range-aff(A)<=C_pure(A)`.

No inverse of `A` is used; singular matrices are allowed. QED.

### Consequence

Any coordinatewise recentering of the input may generate a linear term, but exactly the linear terms generated by translating a pure quadratic form lie in `Range(A)`. Thus **absolute location of the product law costs no constant at all**. What remains after recentering is relative fragment separation/diameter, not a hidden translation parameter.

## 3. Exact component-box mixture for finite fragments

The account04/B14 reduction allows us to work with finite unions. Write, disjointly up to endpoints,

`E_i = disjoint_union_{alpha=1}^{m_i} I_{i,alpha}`,

where

`I_{i,alpha} = c_{i,alpha} + ell_{i,alpha}[-1/2,1/2]`,

`ell_{i,alpha}>0`, and `sum_alpha ell_{i,alpha}=1`.

Let `K=(K_1,...,K_n)` be the component-label vector. Under the product indicator law on `E_1 x ... x E_n`,

`P{K=k} = w_k := product_i ell_{i,k_i}`,                    (3.1)

and `sum_k w_k=1`.

Conditioned on `K=k`, put

`D_k = diag(ell_{1,k_1},...,ell_{n,k_n})`,

`c_k=(c_{1,k_1},...,c_{n,k_n})`,

and let `U_i` be independent uniform variables on `[-1/2,1/2]`. Then

`X = c_k + D_k U`,

and therefore

`Q = U^T B_k U + b_k^T U + d_k`,                            (3.2)

with

`B_k=D_k A D_k`,

`b_k=2D_k A c_k`,

`d_k=c_k^T A c_k`.

Because every `D_k` is invertible,

`Range(B_k)=D_k Range(A)`

and

`b_k=2B_k D_k^{-1}c_k in Range(B_k)`.                       (3.3)

Thus all arbitrary component locations enter the normalized unit-box problem only through a **range-compatible linear term**, which Theorem 2.1 identifies as genuine translation rather than new curvature.

For every bounded interval `I`, the exact mixture identity is

`P{Q in I} = sum_k w_k P{U^T B_k U+b_k^T U+d_k in I}`.       (3.4)

This is an exact localization identity, not an approximation and not a numerical check.

### Important obstruction to a naive fragment-by-fragment proof

The original spectral hypothesis is **not** preserved by the congruence `B_k=D_k A D_k`. For example, with `A=I_3` and one selected component of length `epsilon` in each coordinate, `B_k=epsilon^2 I_3`, so none of its eigenvalues is large when `epsilon<1`, even though the original `A` has three eigenvalues equal to one.

Therefore one may not condition on a tiny fragment box and simply reapply the owner target to `B_k`. A correct proof must exploit the weights `w_k` in (3.4) together with the scale loss in `B_k`. This is the exact **weighted-congruence debt** created by finite-fragment localization.

## 4. A translation-uniform target-level branch: definite physical coordinate pairs

The next lemma pays that debt completely whenever two physical coordinates already carry a definite quadratic block.

### Lemma 4.1 (two-dimensional definite shell)

Let `B` be a real symmetric `2 x 2` matrix with

`Delta=det(B)>0`.

Then `B` is positive or negative definite. For every `ell in R^2`, every `c in R`, and every bounded interval `I`,

`Leb_2{x in R^2 : x^T B x + 2 ell^T x + c in I}`

`    <= pi |I| / sqrt(Delta)`.                              (4.1)

The bound is independent of the linear term and hence of translations.

### Proof

Assume first that `B` is positive definite. Completing the square gives

`x^T Bx+2ell^Tx+c = (x+B^{-1}ell)^T B(x+B^{-1}ell) + c-ell^T B^{-1}ell`.

With `y=B^{1/2}(x+B^{-1}ell)`, Lebesgue measure transforms by

`dx = dy/sqrt(det B)`.

If `J` is any interval of length `L`, then

`Leb_2{y: |y|^2 in J} <= pi L`,                              (4.2)

because the area of `{r_1^2<=|y|^2<=r_2^2}` is `pi(r_2^2-r_1^2)` and truncating an interval at zero only decreases that difference. Hence (4.1).

If `B` is negative definite, multiply the polynomial and output interval by `-1`; `-B` is positive definite and has the same determinant. QED.

### Theorem 4.2 (definite physical-pair branch)

Let `A` be any real symmetric `n x n` matrix. Suppose some physical coordinate pair `J={i,j}` has

`Delta_J = det(A_{J,J}) > 0`.                               (4.3)

For independent real `X_k` with densities `0<=f_k<=1`, the law of `Q=X^TAX` has an a.e. density satisfying

`||p_Q||_infinity <= pi / sqrt(Delta_J)`.                    (4.4)

This conclusion permits arbitrary additional eigenvalues, arbitrary global sign pattern, arbitrary cross-couplings to the other coordinates, arbitrary translations of every input, disconnected/unbounded supports, and no moment assumptions.

### Proof

Condition on all coordinates outside `J`. For each fixed outside value `z`, the conditional polynomial in `x=(x_i,x_j)` has the form

`q_z(x)=x^T Bx+2ell(z)^T x+c(z)`,

where `B=A_{J,J}` and the outside cross-couplings appear only in `ell(z)` and `c(z)`. Independence gives conditional joint density

`f_i(x_i) f_j(x_j) <= 1`.

Therefore Lemma 4.1 yields, uniformly in `z`,

`P{q_z(X_i,X_j) in I | X_{J^c}=z} <= pi |I|/sqrt(Delta_J)`.

Averaging over `z` preserves this bound. The standard interval-domination criterion then gives absolute continuity and (4.4). QED.

If `Delta_J>=eta^2`, the explicit bound is `pi/eta`. In particular a definite physical pair with determinant at least one gives the sharp-form estimate `||p_Q||_infinity<=pi` for this branch.
