## Main new theorem: gradient-shell/cofactor projection bound

### Theorem

Let `X=(X_1,X_2,X_3)` have independent real coordinates with probability densities `f_i` satisfying `0<=f_i<=1`. Let `B` be any invertible real symmetric `3 x 3` matrix, with **arbitrary signature**, and let

`q(X)=X^T B X + ell^T X + c`.

For row sums

`R_i := sum_{j=1}^3 |B_{ij}|`,

the law of `q(X)` is absolutely continuous and has an essentially bounded density satisfying

`||p_q||_infinity <= 16 |det B|^{-1/2} sum_{i=1}^3 sqrt(R_i).`        (1)

Equivalently, if `0<s_1<=s_2<=s_3` are the singular values of `B`, then

`||p_q||_infinity <= 48 * 3^(1/4) / sqrt(s_1 s_2).`                  (2)

In particular, in dimension three, if all three eigenvalues of `B` have absolute value at least one, then

`||p_q||_infinity <= 48 * 3^(1/4) < 63.172.`                         (3)

The result covers both genuinely rotated signatures `(+,+,-)` and `(+,-,-)`, including matrices with all three diagonal entries zero.

### Proof

Because `B` is invertible, complete the square:

`x_0 = -(1/2) B^{-1} ell`,

`q(X) = Y^T B Y + c'`, where `Y=X-x_0`.

Translation preserves independence and the one-dimensional density caps, so it suffices to treat `q(y)=y^TBy`.

Write

`M(y)=||By||_infinity`.

Outside the single point `y=0`, partition `R^3` measurably into sets `E_i`, `i=1,2,3`, by assigning `y` to the least index `i` for which

`|(By)_i| = M(y)`.

For each integer `k`, define the dyadic shell

`E_{i,k} = E_i intersect { 2^k <= M(y) < 2^(k+1) }`.

Fix an interval `I` in the target line and fix the two coordinates `y_{-i}`. Along the remaining coordinate `y_i`,

`d/dy_i (y^T B y) = 2(By)_i`.

On `E_{i,k}` this derivative has absolute value at least `2^(k+1)`. As a function of `y_i`, the quadratic has at most two monotonicity branches. On each branch the inverse-image length of `I` is at most `|I|/2^(k+1)`. Hence the total admissible `y_i`-length is at most

`2 * |I|/2^(k+1) = 2^{-k}|I|`.

Since the density of `Y_i` is at most one, for every fixed `y_{-i}`,

`P(q(Y) in I, Y in E_{i,k} | Y_{-i}=y_{-i}) <= 2^{-k}|I|`.          (4)

We now use the third physical direction to control how much `y_{-i}` can occur on the shell. Put `C=B^{-1}`, and write its columns as `c_1,c_2,c_3`. If `y in E_{i,k}`, then

`||By||_infinity < r_k := 2^(k+1)`,

so

`y in C[-r_k,r_k]^3`.

Project this parallelepiped onto the two coordinates different from `i`. If `pi_i` denotes deletion of the `i`th coordinate, the projection is the planar zonotope generated by the three vectors `r_k pi_i c_j`. Its exact area is

`4 r_k^2 S_i`,

where

`S_i := sum_{1<=p<q<=3} |det(pi_i c_p, pi_i c_q)|`.

Since `r_k^2=4*4^k`, the area is

`16 S_i 4^k`.

The joint density of the other two coordinates is a product bounded by one and has total mass one. Therefore its probability mass on this projection is bounded by

`min(1, 16 S_i 4^k)`.                                               (5)

Combining (4) and (5),

`P(q(Y) in I, Y in E_{i,k}) <= |I| min(2^{-k}, 16 S_i 2^k).`         (6)

For every `a>0`, the elementary dyadic estimate

`sum_{k in Z} min(2^{-k}, a 2^k) < 4 sqrt(a)`                       (7)

follows by splitting at the integer where `2^{-k}` and `a2^k` cross and summing the two geometric tails. Applying (7) to `a=16S_i` and summing over `i` gives

`P(q(Y) in I) <= 16 |I| sum_i sqrt(S_i).`                            (8)

The exceptional point `Y=0` has probability zero because the law is absolutely continuous in `R^3`.

It remains to rewrite `S_i` in terms of the original matrix. Each determinant in `S_i` is a `2 x 2` minor of `C=B^{-1}` with row set equal to the complement of `{i}`. Jacobi's complementary-minor identity gives, for the column-pair whose complement is `{r}`,

`|det C[ {1,2,3}\{i}, {1,2,3}\{r} ]| = |B_{ri}|/|det B|`.

Because `B` is symmetric,

`S_i = (sum_r |B_{ir}|)/|det B| = R_i/|det B|`.                    (9)

Substitution of (9) into (8) proves (1). Since interval domination `mu(I)<=C|I|` for every interval implies `mu<=C Lebesgue`, the pushforward law has a Radon-Nikodym density bounded by the same constant almost everywhere.

For (2), `R_i <= sqrt(3)||row_i(B)||_2 <= sqrt(3)s_3`. Thus

`sum_i sqrt(R_i) <= 3 * 3^(1/4) sqrt(s_3) = 3^(5/4)sqrt(s_3)`.

As `|det B|=s_1s_2s_3`, (1) becomes

`||p_q||_infinity <= 16*3^(5/4)/sqrt(s_1s_2) = 48*3^(1/4)/sqrt(s_1s_2)`.

This proves (2) and (3). QED.

## Exact explanation of how the third direction removes the rank-two logarithm

The proof exposes the dimensional mechanism directly.

On a shell where `||By||_infinity` is comparable to `r`, one physical coordinate has derivative magnitude comparable to `r`, so one-dimensional transversality costs `O(1/r)`.

For an invertible quadratic in three physical coordinates, the allowed projection of the shell onto the *other two independent coordinates* has area `O(r^2)`. Therefore the small-shell contribution is `O(r)`, and the dyadic sum over `r -> 0` converges.

In the two-dimensional opposite-sign saddle, the analogous projection has only length `O(r)`. Multiplying by the `O(1/r)` transversality cost leaves `O(1)` per small dyadic scale, and summing those scales gives precisely the familiar logarithmic divergence. The third direction contributes the one extra power of `r` that changes logarithmic divergence into summability.

This keeps the opposite-sign rank-two logarithmic example intact rather than incorrectly claiming the rank-two density is bounded.
