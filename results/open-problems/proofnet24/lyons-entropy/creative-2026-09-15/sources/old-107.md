## 1. Definitions and the precise finite theorem

Let \(E\) be a labeled set with \(n\ge2\) elements, let \(0\le r\le n-2\), and write
\[
\mathcal L_k=\{A\subseteq E:|A|=k\}.
\]
All functions below are real valued. A function \(f:2^E\to\mathbb R\) is supermodular when
\[
f(A\cup B)+f(A\cap B)\ge f(A)+f(B)
\quad(A,B\subseteq E).
\tag{1}
\]
For \(S\subseteq E\) and distinct \(i,j\notin S\), define
\[
D_{S;ij}
 =\delta_S+\delta_{S\cup\{i,j\}}
  -\delta_{S\cup\{i\}}-\delta_{S\cup\{j\}},
\tag{2}
\]
where \(\delta_A\) is the coordinate vector at the subset \(A\). We use unordered pairs \(\{i,j\}\), so each elementary diamond occurs once. The pairing of a signed measure \(b\) and a function \(f\) is
\[
\langle b,f\rangle=\sum_{A\subseteq E}b(A)f(A).
\]
Thus
\[
\langle D_{S;ij},f\rangle
 =\Delta_i\Delta_j f(S).
\tag{3}
\]

Let \(\mu_0,\mu_u,\mu_v,\mu_2\) be arbitrary probability measures, respectively supported on
\[
\mathcal L_r,\quad\mathcal L_{r+1},\quad
\mathcal L_{r+1},\quad\mathcal L_{r+2}.
\]
Set
\[
b=\mu_0+\mu_2-\mu_u-\mu_v,\qquad
q_f=\langle b,f\rangle.
\tag{4}
\]
The subscripts \(u,v\) are only names at this stage: no determinantal assumption is used.

An **unlabeled elementary-diamond coupling** is a probability distribution on
\[
(S,\{i,j\}),\qquad S\in\mathcal L_r,\quad i\ne j,\quad i,j\notin S,
\]
such that, with \(T=S\cup\{i,j\}\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{law}(S)&=\mu_0,\\
\operatorname{law}(T)&=\mu_2,\\
\mathbb E\bigl[\delta_{S\cup\{i\}}+\delta_{S\cup\{j\}}\bigr]
 &=\mu_u+\mu_v.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]
The last equality is an equality of measures of total mass two. It imposes no prescribed law on an individually ordered middle vertex.

**Theorem 1.** The following are equivalent.

1. \(q_f\ge0\) for every supermodular \(f:2^E\to\mathbb R\).
2. \(b\) belongs to the nonnegative cone generated by all elementary diamond vectors (2), with bases of any rank.
3. There are numbers \(x_{S;ij}\ge0\), indexed only by \(|S|=r\), such that
   \[
   b=\sum_{\substack{|S|=r\\\{i,j\}\subseteq E\setminus S}}
       x_{S;ij}D_{S;ij},\qquad
   \sum_{S;ij}x_{S;ij}=1.
   \tag{6}
   \]
4. There is an unlabeled elementary-diamond coupling (5).

This theorem includes \(r=0\), \(r=n-2\), and the overlapping endpoint case \(n=2,r=0\). It does not assert that any of these equivalent properties automatically holds for arbitrary four graded laws.

## 2. Complete proof of the finite cone criterion

### 2.1 Elementary inequalities characterize supermodularity

Condition (1) implies \(\Delta_i\Delta_j f(S)\ge0\) by taking
\(A=S\cup\{i\}\) and \(B=S\cup\{j\}\).

Conversely, suppose all elementary second differences are nonnegative. By adding one coordinate at a time,
\[
\Delta_i f(X)\le\Delta_i f(Y)
\quad\text{whenever }X\subseteq Y,\ i\notin Y.
\tag{7}
\]
Indeed, each step from \(X\) to \(Y\) changes \(\Delta_i f\) by an elementary second difference.

For arbitrary \(A,B\), enumerate \(A\setminus B=\{i_1,\ldots,i_m\}\) and put \(C=A\cap B\). Apply (7) to the successive increments along the two chains starting at \(C\) and \(B\). Summation gives
\[
f(A)-f(C)\le f(A\cup B)-f(B),
\]
which is (1). Hence the supermodular cone is exactly
\[
\mathrm{SM}
 =\{f:\langle D_{S;ij},f\rangle\ge0
       \text{ for every elementary diamond}\}.
\tag{8}
\]

### 2.2 The diamond cone is closed

Let
\[
\mathcal C=\operatorname{cone}\{D_{S;ij}\}.
\]
There are only
\[
m=\binom n2\,2^{n-2}
\]
generators. A direct closedness argument avoids any implicit topological assumption in duality.

Define
\[
h(A)=\binom{|A|}{2}.
\]
For every elementary diamond,
\[
\langle D_{S;ij},h\rangle
 =\binom{|S|}{2}+\binom{|S|+2}{2}
   -2\binom{|S|+1}{2}=1.
\tag{9}
\]
Suppose \(c_\ell\in\mathcal C\) converges to \(c\), and choose representations
\(c_\ell=\sum_{d=1}^m a_{\ell d}D_d\) with \(a_{\ell d}\ge0\). Then
\[
\sum_{d=1}^m a_{\ell d}=\langle c_\ell,h\rangle.
\]
The right side is bounded. Thus all coefficient vectors lie in a bounded subset of the nonnegative orthant. A subsequence converges to \(a\ge0\), and continuity gives
\(c=\sum_d a_dD_d\in\mathcal C\). Therefore \(\mathcal C\) is closed.

### 2.3 Finite cone duality, with the sign checked

If \(b\in\mathcal C\), (8) immediately gives \(\langle b,f\rangle\ge0\) for every \(f\in\mathrm{SM}\).

For the converse, suppose \(b\notin\mathcal C\). Since \(\mathcal C\) is a closed convex cone in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, let \(z\) be a point of \(\mathcal C\) closest to \(b\), and put \(f=z-b\). The minimizing property gives
\[
\langle f,c-z\rangle\ge0
\quad(c\in\mathcal C).
\]
Using \(c=0\) and \(c=2z\) yields \(\langle f,z\rangle=0\). Consequently
\[
\langle f,c\rangle\ge0\quad(c\in\mathcal C),
\qquad
\langle f,b\rangle=-\|f\|^2<0.
\tag{10}
\]
In particular, \(\langle D_{S;ij},f\rangle\ge0\) for every diamond, so \(f\) is supermodular by (8). It violates statement 1 of Theorem 1. This proves the equivalence of statements 1 and 2, and is the required finite Farkas separation argument.

### 2.4 Grading forces every positive coefficient to have base rank \(r\)

Suppose
\[
b=\sum_{S;ij}a_{S;ij}D_{S;ij},\qquad a_{S;ij}\ge0.
\tag{11}
\]
From (9) and the four prescribed cardinalities,
\[
\sum_{S;ij}a_{S;ij}
 =\langle b,h\rangle
 =\binom r2+\binom{r+2}{2}-2\binom{r+1}{2}
 =1.
\tag{12}
\]
There is at least one positive coefficient.

Let \(k\) be the smallest base cardinality among the positive coefficients. If \(k<r\), look at coordinates indexed by \(k\)-element sets. Every contribution there is a nonnegative bottom contribution from a base of size \(k\). Negative middle contributions would require a positive coefficient with base size \(k-1\), and positive top contributions would require base size \(k-2\); neither exists by minimality. At least one bottom contribution is strictly positive. But \(b\) is identically zero at level \(k<r\), a contradiction.

Let \(\ell\) be the largest base cardinality among positive coefficients. If \(\ell>r\), look at level \(\ell+2>r+2\). Every contribution there is a nonnegative top contribution from a base of size \(\ell\). A negative middle contribution would require base size \(\ell+1\), and a bottom contribution would require base size \(\ell+2\); neither exists by maximality. At least one top contribution is strictly positive, contradicting that \(b\) vanishes on that level.

Thus \(k\ge r\) and \(\ell\le r\), so all positive coefficients have base size exactly \(r\). Together with (12), this proves statement 3.

This argument examines the smallest and largest occupied base levels. It does not assume that arbitrary intermediate levels have no cancellations.

At \(r=0\), the lower-level exclusion is vacuous and the upper-level argument is unchanged. At \(r=n-2\), the upper-level exclusion is vacuous and the lower-level argument is unchanged. For \(n=2,r=0\), there is a single possible diamond, and (12) gives its weight one.

### 2.5 The coefficients are exactly the desired coupling

Once all bases have size \(r\), equality of coordinates in (6) gives
\[
\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq E\setminus S}x_{S;ij}=\mu_0(S)
\quad(S\in\mathcal L_r),
\tag{13}
\]
\[
\sum_{\substack{S\subseteq T\\|S|=r}}x_{S;T\setminus S}=\mu_2(T)
\quad(T\in\mathcal L_{r+2}),
\tag{14}
\]
and
\[
\sum_{\substack{S;ij:\\
M=S\cup\{i\}\ \mathrm{or}\ M=S\cup\{j\}}}
x_{S;ij}=\mu_u(M)+\mu_v(M)
\quad(M\in\mathcal L_{r+1}).
\tag{15}
\]
The two middle vertices are distinct, so each relevant atom is counted once in (15). Equations (12)--(15) say precisely that the weights form the probability distribution in (5).

Conversely, a distribution (5) gives (6) by taking expected signed diamond vectors. Also, directly,
\[
q_f=\mathbb E\bigl[\Delta_i\Delta_j f(S)\bigr]\ge0
\]
for every supermodular \(f\). This completes the proof.

## 3. Exact finite LP certificates, including global extension

Let \(D_r\) be the matrix whose columns are \(D_{S;ij}\) with \(|S|=r\), restricted to the three levels \(r,r+1,r+2\). Put
\[
N=\binom nr+\binom n{r+1}+\binom n{r+2},
\qquad
M=\binom nr\binom{n-r}{2}.
\]
The primal feasibility problem is exactly
\[
D_r x=b,\qquad x\ge0.
\tag{16}
\]
The total mass condition \(\sum x=1\) follows from (16), so it need not be imposed independently.

### 3.1 A three-level separating table is enough

A dual infeasibility certificate for (16) is a real table \(\phi\) on the three levels satisfying
\[
\phi(S)+\phi(S\cup\{i,j\})
-\phi(S\cup\{i\})-\phi(S\cup\{j\})\ge0
\quad(|S|=r),
\tag{17}
\]
but
\[
\langle b,\phi\rangle<0.
\tag{18}
\]
This is already an exact LP certificate. To turn it into a literal supermodular set function on *all* subsets, no unspecified extension theorem is needed.

Define the rank barrier
\[
\rho_r(k)=(r-k)_+^2+(k-r-2)_+^2,
\qquad t_+=\max(t,0).
\tag{19}
\]
It vanishes on \(k=r,r+1,r+2\). Its elementary second difference, at a base of size \(k\), is
\[
\rho_r(k)+\rho_r(k+2)-2\rho_r(k+1)
=
\begin{cases}
0,&k=r,\\
1,&k=r-1\text{ or }k=r+1,\\
2,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\tag{20}
\]
with only valid Boolean-lattice base sizes considered.

Let \(\widehat\phi\) equal \(\phi\) on the three prescribed levels and zero elsewhere. Put
\[
L=\max|\phi|,\qquad
f(A)=\widehat\phi(A)+4L\,\rho_r(|A|).
\tag{21}
\]
For a base of size \(r\), the second difference is the one in (17). At every other base, the second difference of \(\widehat\phi\) is at least \(-4L\), while the barrier contributes at least \(4L\), by (20). Hence every elementary second difference of \(f\) is nonnegative: \(f\) is globally supermodular. Moreover,
\[
\langle b,f\rangle=\langle b,\phi\rangle<0,
\tag{22}
\]
because \(b\) is supported where the barrier vanishes.

Thus every local dual certificate has an explicit global extension, even at \(r=0\) or \(r=n-2\). More generally, the restriction of the global supermodular cone to these three ranks is precisely the cone defined by (17).

### 3.2 Rational certificates and finite support

If the four probability tables are rational, a feasible primal has a rational solution. To see this directly, start from any feasible solution. If its positive-support columns are dependent, choose a nonzero relation \(\sum_j z_jD_j=0\). Pairing with \(h\) gives \(\sum_jz_j=0\), so the relation has both positive and negative entries. Subtract
\[
t z,\qquad t=\min_{z_j>0}\frac{x_j}{z_j},
\]
from the current coefficient vector. This preserves nonnegativity and the represented vector and makes at least one positive entry zero. Repeat until the positive-support columns are linearly independent. Their coefficients are then the unique solution of an independent rational linear system, so are rational.

This also gives a support bound of \(\operatorname{rank}D_r\). Every diamond annihilates the \(n+1\) modular functions
\[
1,\quad \mathbf 1_{\{i\in A\}}\quad(i\in E).
\]
Their restrictions to the three levels are linearly independent: comparison of equal-sized sets differing in one element forces all coordinate coefficients equal, and comparison of adjacent levels then forces that common coefficient and the constant to vanish. Therefore
\[
\#\{\text{positive coupling atoms}\}
\le\operatorname{rank}D_r\le N-n-1.
\tag{23}
\]

An infeasible rational instance likewise has a rational, and after scaling an integer-valued, global supermodular witness. Here is an elementary rational-approximation proof. If a separating supermodular \(f\) has \(\langle b,f\rangle=-\eta<0\), set \(g=f+(\eta/2)h\). Every elementary second difference of \(g\) is at least \(\eta/2\), while \(\langle b,g\rangle=-\eta/2\). Approximate the finitely many entries of \(g\) by rationals with uniform error less than \(\eta/16\). Elementary second differences change by less than \(\eta/4\), and the objective changes by less than \(\eta/4\), since \(\|b\|_1=4\). The rational approximation remains supermodular and has negative objective. Clear denominators to obtain an integer table. Rationality of \(b\) then makes the negative objective an exactly checkable rational number.

An exact primal record should provide the four probability tables and a finite list of nonnegative rational weights indexed by \((S,\{i,j\})\). Verification consists of the input cardinalities and masses, (13)--(15), and rational arithmetic. An exact dual record may provide a rational table only on the three levels, verify (17)--(18), and specify the extension (19)--(21). If the intended claim concerns a determinantal instance, the common frame and its exact orthonormality and determinant formulas must also be verified.

\]

### 3.3 Why a labeled dual is different

For a prescribed ordered pair of middle laws, use ordered additions \((i,j)\) and require separately
\[
\operatorname{law}(S\cup\{i\})=\mu_u,\qquad
\operatorname{law}(S\cup\{j\})=\mu_v.
\tag{24}
\]
This is a different LP, with \(2M\) columns and a separate row for each middle label. At \(n=6,r=2\), there are 180 labeled columns and 70 marginal rows before removing dependencies.

A general labeled dual consists of four separate tables \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) and inequalities
\[
\alpha(S)+\beta(S\cup\{i\})+\gamma(S\cup\{j\})
+\delta(S\cup\{i,j\})\ge0.
\tag{25}
\]
Its objective pairs these four tables with the four individual laws. Supermodular separation is the special pattern
\[
\alpha=f|_{\mathcal L_r},\quad
\beta=\gamma=-f|_{\mathcal L_{r+1}},\quad
\delta=f|_{\mathcal L_{r+2}}.
\tag{26}
\]
A negative labeled dual objective with unrelated \(\beta,\gamma\) need not yield a supermodular witness. Checking nonnegativity on 180 labeled diamonds is therefore not by itself the same certificate as checking (17).

## 4. Grading, modular moments, and determinantal compatibility

Since both a modular function and its negative are supermodular, Theorem 1 requires
\[
\langle b,1\rangle=0,\qquad
\sum_{A\ni i}b(A)=0\quad(i\in E).
\tag{27}
\]
The first identity follows from the four masses. The coordinate identities are additional conditions for arbitrary graded laws. They are automatic for compatible projection laws because the four projection matrices satisfy
\[
P_{W\oplus U}+P_W
-P_{W\oplus[u]}-P_{W\oplus[v]}=0,
\tag{28}
\]
and a DPP's one-site inclusion probabilities are the diagonal entries of its kernel. These moment identities alone are not sufficient.

The following exact examples concern arbitrary graded laws. Neither is a replacement for the lost determinantal example.

### Example A: aggregate feasibility does not prescribe labels

Let \(E=\{1,2,3,4\}\), \(r=0\), and set
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_0&=\delta_\varnothing,\\
\mu_2&=\tfrac12\delta_{\{1,2\}}+\tfrac12\delta_{\{3,4\}},\\
\mu_u&=\tfrac12\delta_{\{1\}}+\tfrac12\delta_{\{2\}},\\
\mu_v&=\tfrac12\delta_{\{3\}}+\tfrac12\delta_{\{4\}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{29}
\]
Put weight \(1/2\) on each of the two unlabeled diamonds with added pairs \(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{3,4\}\). This is a valid primal certificate, and
\[
q_f=\tfrac12\Delta_1\Delta_2f(\varnothing)
    +\tfrac12\Delta_3\Delta_4f(\varnothing)\ge0.
\tag{30}
\]

There is no coupling with the individually prescribed labels. A middle vertex with law \(\mu_u\) lies in \(\{1,2\}\) with probability one. In any diamond it must lie in the top set, but the top meets \(\{1,2\}\) with probability only \(1/2\). This is already a two-law inclusion obstruction. The example illustrates the logical distinction and makes no compatible-frame claim.

### Example B: even balanced modular moments do not force positivity

\mu_u=\delta_{\{1,2\}},\quad
\mu_v=\delta_{\{3,4\}},\quad
\mu_2=\delta_{\{2,3,4\}}.
\tag{31}
\]
Each coordinate occurs once on each side of (4), so (27) holds. But the globally supermodular function

\tag{32}
\]
has

q_f=0+0-1-0=-1.
\tag{33}
\]
Its elementary second differences are zero or one. ## 5. Projection laws and invariance under the update-plane basis

For a \(d\)-dimensional subspace \(H\subseteq\mathbb C^E\), choose an \(n\times d\) matrix \(Q_H\) with orthonormal columns spanning \(H\). Write \(\mu_H\) for its projection DPP:
\[
\mu_H(A)=
\begin{cases}
|\det (Q_H)_A|^2,&|A|=d,\\
0,&|A|\ne d.
\end{cases}
\tag{34}
\]
The determinant of the empty \(0\times0\) matrix is one. Cauchy--Binet gives total mass
\(\det(Q_H^*Q_H)=1\). Replacing \(Q_H\) by \(Q_H R\), \(R\) unitary, multiplies each maximal minor by \(\det R\), whose modulus is one. Thus (34) depends only on the subspace.

Now fix an orthonormal frame \(w_1,\ldots,w_r,u,v\), let
\[
W=\operatorname{span}(w_1,\ldots,w_r),\qquad
U=\operatorname{span}(u,v)\subseteq W^\perp,
\]
and define
\[
\mu_0=\mu_W,\quad
\mu_u=\mu_{W\oplus[u]},\quad
\mu_v=\mu_{W\oplus[v]},\quad
\mu_2=\mu_{W\oplus U}.
\tag{35}
\]
Theorem 1 applies directly to these four graded probability laws.

For \(A\in\mathcal L_{r+1}\), use the fixed increasing order of its coordinate rows and set
\[
a_A=\det[w_1\ \cdots\ w_r\ u]_A,\qquad
c_A=\det[w_1\ \cdots\ w_r\ v]_A.
\tag{36}
\]
If another orthonormal basis of \(U\) is obtained by
\[
[\widetilde u\ \widetilde v]=[u\ v]R,\qquad R\in U(2),
\]
linearity in the last column gives the row of middle amplitudes
\[
(\widetilde a_A,\widetilde c_A)=(a_A,c_A)R.
\]
Therefore, for every \(A\),
\[
\mu_{\widetilde u}(A)+\mu_{\widetilde v}(A)
=|a_A|^2+|c_A|^2
=\mu_u(A)+\mu_v(A).
\tag{37}
\]
The bottom and top subspaces are unchanged, so their laws are unchanged. Alternatively, the top minor is multiplied by \(\det R\), again of modulus one. Hence the entire signed measure \(b\), and \(q_f\) for every real table \(f\), are invariant under the orthonormal basis of \(U\).

Only the column basis of the fixed subspaces is being changed. The coordinate ground set and the values of \(f\) are not rotated.

For the mixed-derivative convention in the surrounding campaign, this alternating expectation agrees with
\[
D^2F_f(P_W)[uu^*,vv^*],\qquad F_f(K)=\mathbb E_{P_K}f.
\tag{38}
\]
Indeed, writing the Boolean Möbius expansion
\(f(A)=\sum_{B\subseteq A}c_B\) gives
\(F_f(K)=\sum_Bc_B\det K_B\). Each determinant is affine in each rank-one update parameter. Thus the coefficient of \(st\) in
\(F_f(P_W+suu^*+tvv^*)\) is exactly the four-corner difference (4), and exactly the mixed derivative in (38). There is no factor of two.

## 6. Real-frame rotation and the labeled-coupling corollary

**Theorem 2.** Suppose \(W\) has a real orthonormal basis and \(u,v\in\mathbb R^E\) are orthonormal and perpendicular to \(W\). Regard these real subspaces inside their complexifications. Then
\[
u'=\frac{u+iv}{\sqrt2},\qquad
v'=\frac{u-iv}{\sqrt2}
\tag{39}
\]
are an orthonormal complex basis of the same complex plane \(U\), and
\[
\mu_{u'}=\mu_{v'}=\frac{\mu_u+\mu_v}{2}.
\tag{40}
\]

**Proof.** The change-of-basis matrix is
\[
R=\frac1{\sqrt2}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
i&-i
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad R^*R=I,\qquad\det R=-i.
\tag{41}
\]
Using a real basis of \(W\), both amplitudes \(a_A,c_A\) in (36) are real. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{u'}(A)
 &=\left|\frac{a_A+ic_A}{\sqrt2}\right|^2
  =\frac{a_A^2+c_A^2}{2},\\
\mu_{v'}(A)
 &=\left|\frac{a_A-ic_A}{\sqrt2}\right|^2
  =\frac{a_A^2+c_A^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{42}
\]
The other cardinalities have probability zero. This proves (40). The top determinant acquires the phase \(-i\); its probability is unchanged.

There is no orientation ambiguity. A change of the real basis of \(W\) multiplies both middle amplitudes by the same sign. More generally, an arbitrary complex orthonormal basis change within \(W\) multiplies both amplitudes by the same unit complex number, so the equality remains true. Reordering the selected coordinate rows also multiplies both by the same sign. Formula (34) is invariant under all these choices. \(\square\)

**Corollary 3.** For the real-frame data of Theorem 2, the following are equivalent:

1. \(q_f\ge0\) for every supermodular \(f\).
2. The original four laws have an unlabeled elementary-diamond coupling.
3. The four laws associated with \(W,u',v'\) have a labeled elementary-diamond coupling with their individually prescribed middle marginals.
4. Some complex orthonormal basis of \(U\) has such a labeled coupling.

The equivalence of 1 and 2 is Theorem 1. To prove 2 implies 3, draw an unlabeled diamond and independently toss a fair coin to decide which of its two middle sets gets label \(u'\). Give the other middle set label \(v'\). Each labeled marginal is
\[
\frac12\mathbb E[
\delta_{S\cup\{i\}}+\delta_{S\cup\{j\}}]
=\frac{\mu_u+\mu_v}{2},
\]
which is exactly (40). Bottom and top are unchanged. Statement 3 implies 4. Finally, forgetting the labels in 4 gives an unlabeled coupling with the same aggregate middle law by (37), and therefore gives 2.

The existence of the unlabeled coupling is still a substantive hypothesis. The rotation does not create it. The corollary permits complex orthonormal bases; (39) does not justify a claim restricted to real orthogonal changes of basis.

For arbitrary graded laws with \(\mu_u=\mu_v\), the same fair-swap argument proves that unlabeled and individually prescribed labeled feasibility are equivalent, without any determinantal hypothesis.

## 7. Endpoint ranks and the limitation for general complex frames

### 7.1 Explicit endpoint couplings for compatible projection laws

At \(r=0\), the bottom is empty and the top is the rank-two projection law on \(U\). Define
\[
\lambda_{ij}=|u_iv_j-u_jv_i|^2,\qquad i<j.
\tag{43}
\]
Cauchy--Binet gives \(\sum_{i<j}\lambda_{ij}=1\). Orthonormality gives, for each coordinate \(i\),
\[
\sum_{j\ne i}|u_iv_j-u_jv_i|^2
=|u_i|^2+|v_i|^2.
\tag{44}
\]
Thus weight \(\lambda_{ij}\) on the diamond
\((\varnothing,\{i\},\{j\},\{i,j\})\) has the correct top law and aggregate middle law, even for complex \(u,v\).

At \(r=n-2\), necessarily \(W=U^\perp\). Complementary maximal minors of a unitary matrix have equal magnitudes; completing an orthonormal frame to a unitary matrix therefore gives
\[
\mu_H(A)=\mu_{H^\perp}(E\setminus A).
\tag{45}
\]
Consequently, weight \(\lambda_{ij}\) on the diamond with bottom \(E\setminus\{i,j\}\) and top \(E\) is a valid unlabeled coupling. The two middle laws are complements of the rank-one laws on \([v]\) and \([u]\), and (44) verifies their aggregate. This gives explicit endpoint constructions in addition to the endpoint validity of the general cone proof.

### 7.2 The displayed rotation does not equalize arbitrary complex amplitudes

For general complex \(a_A,c_A\), the same rotation gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{u'}(A)
 &=\frac{|a_A|^2+|c_A|^2}{2}
   +\operatorname{Im}(a_A\overline{c_A}),\\
\mu_{v'}(A)
 &=\frac{|a_A|^2+|c_A|^2}{2}
   -\operatorname{Im}(a_A\overline{c_A}).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46}
\]
The cross terms need not vanish simultaneously. Thus reality, or an appropriate common-phase property, is essential to the proof of (40).

In fact, there need not exist *any* orthonormal basis of a general complex plane that equalizes the two middle laws. Here is a small exact example, unrelated to the lost six-site witness.

Let \(n=4,r=0\), let \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}\), and put
\[
u=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt6},
              \frac1{\sqrt6},\frac1{\sqrt6}\right)^T,
\qquad
v=\frac1{\sqrt3}(0,1,\omega,\omega^2)^T.
\tag{47}
\]
The vectors are orthonormal because \(1+\omega+\omega^2=0\), and
\[
(P_U)_{kk}=\frac12\quad(k=1,2,3,4).
\tag{48}
\]
Suppose an orthonormal basis \(x,y\) of \(U\) had equal rank-one laws. Since their coordinate squared magnitudes sum to the diagonal in (48), both would have squared magnitude \(1/4\) at every coordinate. Write
\[
x=a u+c v,\qquad |a|^2+|c|^2=1.
\]
The first coordinate gives \(|a|^2=1/2\), hence \(|c|^2=1/2\). The second and third coordinates then give
\[
\operatorname{Re}(a\overline c)=0,\qquad
\operatorname{Re}(a\overline c\,\overline\omega)=0.
\tag{49}
\]
Since \(\omega\) is nonreal, these two equations imply \(a\overline c=0\), contradicting \(|a||c|=1/2\). No equalizing basis exists.

This is a counterexample to general complex basis equalization, not to supermodular mixed positivity. Its rank is \(r=0\), so (43)--(44) already provide an unlabeled coupling. Nor does the failure of equalization assert the absence of every possible labeled coupling: equal middle laws are sufficient for the fair-swap construction, not necessary for an arbitrary labeled coupling.
