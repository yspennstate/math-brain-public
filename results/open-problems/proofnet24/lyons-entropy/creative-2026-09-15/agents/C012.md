## 1. Main theorem and precise scope

Let \(E=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\), \(\zeta=e^{\pi i/3}\), and
\[
f_k(j)=6^{-1/2}\zeta^{jk}\qquad(0\leq j,k\leq5).
\]
For arbitrary real \(\theta,\phi\), let \(G=G(\theta,\phi)\) act as the identity off coordinates 0 and 3 and act there by
\[
G_{\{0,3\}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&e^{i\phi}\sin\theta\\
-e^{-i\phi}\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{1}
\]
Set
\[
W=\operatorname{span}(Gf_0,Gf_1),\quad
u=Gf_2,\quad v=Gf_3,\quad
Z=\operatorname{span}(Gf_4,Gf_5).
\]
These spaces and vectors are orthogonal, with dimensions \(2,1,1,2\). For a projection subspace \(V\), write \(\mu_V\) for its projection DPP law.

**Theorem 1 (global complex row-rotation family).** For every \(\theta,\phi\), the four laws
\[
\mu_W,\quad\mu_{W+[u]},\quad\mu_{W+[v]},\quad\mu_{W+[u,v]}
\]
have an unlabeled elementary-diamond coupling. Its 90 nonnegative weights are the closed formulas in Sections 3–4, generated from two integer tables with denominators 36 and 72. No optimization is required to evaluate those formulas.

In particular, for every supermodular set function \(f\),
\[
\mathbb E_{\mu_W}f+\mathbb E_{\mu_{W+[u,v]}}f
-\mathbb E_{\mu_{W+[u]}}f-\mathbb E_{\mu_{W+[v]}}f\geq0.
\tag{2}
\]
Theorem 2 below gives the corresponding entropy-concavity consequence for two oppositely moving eigenvalues.

This covers every complex unitary operation on the antipodal coordinate pair, up to coordinate phases that do not change any DPP law. Indeed, left multiplication by diagonal phases makes the diagonal entries of an arbitrary two-by-two unitary real and nonnegative and puts it in form (1). Coordinate permutations carry the theorem to either of the other antipodal pairs.

The coupling is **unlabeled**: it specifies the sum of the two middle laws. It does not specify them separately after an ordering of the two added sites.

\[

## 3. Two integer transport certificates generate ten vertices

Define three actions on a diamond table:

- \(\mathsf X\): apply \(\sigma=(1\ 2)(4\ 5)\) to every coordinate;
- \(\mathsf Y\): interchange \(A\) and \(B\);
- \(\mathsf Z\): apply \(\tau=(1\ 5)(2\ 4)\) to every coordinate.

They commute and are involutions. The coefficient tables show that their induced actions on contrasts are exactly
\[
(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,z),\qquad
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,z),\qquad
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,-z),
\tag{7}

## 4. Closed formulas for the global coupling

Let
\[
\mathcal P=\operatorname{conv}\bigl(\{(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)\}
\cup\{(0,0,2),(0,0,-2)\}\bigr).
\]
The physical ellipsoid in (5) lies in \(\mathcal P\). The following constructive proof also gives the coupling formula.

If \(|z|\leq1\), then \(|x|,|y|\leq1\), and use
\[
\boxed{\quad
X(x,y,z)=\sum_{s\in\{-1,1\}^3}
\frac{(1+s_xx)(1+s_yy)(1+s_zz)}8\,C_s.
\quad}
\tag{11}
\]
The scalar weights are the usual product interpolation in the cube. They are nonnegative, sum to one, and have coordinate mean \((x,y,z)\).

If \(1<|z|\leq\sqrt3\), put
\[
\epsilon=\operatorname{sign}(z),\qquad \lambda=2-|z|.
\]
Weighted Cauchy–Schwarz gives
\[
|x|+|z|\leq2\sqrt{x^2+z^2/3}\leq2,
\qquad
|y|+|z|\leq2\sqrt{y^2+z^2/3}\leq2.
\tag{12}
\]
Consequently \(0<\lambda<1\) and \(|x|,|y|\leq\lambda\). Use
\[
\boxed{\quad
X(x,y,z)=\sum_{s_x,s_y=\pm1}
\frac{\lambda}{4}
\left(1+\frac{s_xx}{\lambda}\right)
\left(1+\frac{s_yy}{\lambda}\right)C_{(s_x,s_y,\epsilon)}
+(1-\lambda)T_\epsilon.
\quad}
\tag{13}
\]
Again all coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one. Their coordinate mean is
\[
\lambda\left(\frac{x}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda},\epsilon\right)
+(1-\lambda)(0,0,2\epsilon)=(x,y,z).
\]
The formulas agree on \(|z|=1\). The implementation additionally defines the natural limit at the two tips of \(\mathcal P\); physical points always have \(|z|\leq\sqrt3<2\), so there is no singular denominator in (13).

Since \(b\) is affine, (10)–(13) prove \(DX(x,y,z)=b(x,y,z)\). The weights are nonnegative and have total mass one. This proves Theorem 1. Pairing each diamond column with a supermodular function proves (2) immediately. ∎

The family is genuinely two-dimensional: from
\[
a_{01}=\frac{2-x+z}{36},\quad
a_{02}=\frac{2+x+z}{36},\quad
g_{012}=\frac{5-3y+2z}{72}
\]
one recovers all three contrast coordinates. Their physical domain is a full ellipsoid surface. It contains C004's Fourier point at \((x,y,z)=(1,0,0)\).

A specific nonsymmetric example is
\[
\theta=\arctan(2/3),\quad\phi=\pi/3,
\qquad(x,y,z)=(5/13,6/13,18/13).
\]
Here \(a_{01}=1/12\) while \(a_{12}=1/36\), so cyclic symmetry is broken. All 15 bottom pair probabilities are positive. A rank-two projection with every pair minor positive cannot split over nontrivial disjoint coordinate blocks: a rank-one block with two sites would give a zero pair minor, a rank-zero block would give zero probabilities involving its sites, and two one-site rank-one blocks cannot cover six positive coordinates. Thus this is a non-block example. The extension reaches well beyond a small perturbation neighborhood of the Fourier flag.

## 6. Entropy consequence for opposite spectral slopes

**Theorem 2.** Fix any frame in Theorem 1. Let
\[
K(t)=P_W+\lambda(t)uu^*+\mu(t)vv^*,\qquad 0\leq t\leq1,
\]
where \(\lambda,\mu\) are affine, valued in \([0,1]\), and their slopes \(\alpha,\beta\) satisfy \(\alpha\beta\leq0\). Then \(t\mapsto H(\mathbb P_{K(t)})\) is concave.

**Proof.** Initially suppose \(0<\lambda,\mu<1\). The determinantal law is the spectral mixture
\[
p=(1-\lambda)(1-\mu)\mu_W
+\lambda(1-\mu)\mu_{W+[u]}
+(1-\lambda)\mu\mu_{W+[v]}
+\lambda\mu\mu_{W+[u,v]}.
\]
Thus \(p''=2\alpha\beta b\), and
\[
H''=-\sum_{S:p_S>0}\frac{(p'_S)^2}{p_S}
-2\alpha\beta\sum_{S:p_S>0}b_S\log p_S.
\tag{17}
\]
For a DPP atom law, every elementary square satisfies
\[
p_{A\cup\{i\}}p_{A\cup\{j\}}\geq p_Ap_{A\cup\{i,j\}}.
\tag{18}
\]
For \(0<K<I\), write \(p_S=\det L_S/\det(I+L)\), \(L=K(I-K)^{-1}>0\). Taking the two-by-two Schur complement over \(A\) proves (18): the difference of the two determinant products is \((\det L_A)^2\) times the squared absolute off-diagonal entry of that Schur complement. Approximation by \(\varepsilon I+(1-2\varepsilon)K\) proves (18) for boundary kernels as well.

If a coupling weight \(X_{A,B}>0\), all four atoms at its diamond have positive \(p\): its bottom and top marginals are positive, and both aggregate middle marginals are positive, with strictly positive spectral mixture coefficients. Consequently (18) gives
\[
\sum_S b_S\log p_S
=\sum_{A,B}X_{A,B}
\log\frac{p_Ap_{E\setminus B}}{p_{A\cup\{i\}}p_{A\cup\{j\}}}
\leq0.
\tag{19}
\]
Atoms outside the support have all four projection-mixture coefficients zero and cause no term in (17). Since \(\alpha\beta\leq0\), both contributions in (17) are nonpositive. This proves concavity in the open spectral square. Replacing \(\lambda,\mu\) by \(\varepsilon+(1-2\varepsilon)\lambda,\varepsilon+(1-2\varepsilon)\mu\) preserves the slope condition; continuity of finite Shannon entropy gives the closed-square statement. ∎

This is a restricted Lyons theorem for a specific global family of fixed eigenbases, two eigenvalues fixed at one, two at zero, and two moving in opposite directions. It does not assert joint concavity for unrestricted slopes or arbitrary Hermitian directions.

## 7. Exact obstruction: no globally affine positive lift

The nonlinear correction in (16) has a structural reason.

**Theorem 3.** There are no 90 affine functions
\[
L_{A,B}(x,y,z)=c_{A,B}+h_{A,B}x+k_{A,B}y+l_{A,B}z
\]
that are nonnegative at every point of
\(x^2+y^2+z^2/3=1\) and satisfy \(DL=b(x,y,z)\) everywhere on that surface.

**Proof.** Each of the eight bottom pairs containing exactly one of 0 and 3 has a probability of the form
\[
a_A(x,y,z)=\frac{2+s x+t z}{36},\qquad s,t\in\{-1,1\}.
\tag{20}
\]
It is nonnegative on the ellipsoid and vanishes at its unique tangent point
\((-s/2,0,-3t/2)\). The same is true of the corresponding complement-top marginal \(a_B\).

An affine function nonnegative on an ellipsoid surface is also nonnegative on its filled ellipsoid. If it vanishes at one of these tangent points, it is a nonnegative multiple of the unique supporting affine function there. This follows directly from Cauchy–Schwarz after scaling \((x,y,z)\) to \((x,y,z/\sqrt3)\): for \(c+u\cdot r\geq0\) on the unit sphere, \(c\geq\|u\|\), and equality at one point forces \(u\) to be a fixed multiple of the opposite unit normal.

Every summand in a nonnegative decomposition of (20) must vanish at that point. Therefore, if a diamond's bottom \(A\) or complement-top \(B\) contains exactly one of 0 and 3, its affine weight is a multiple of such a marginal and has **zero coefficient of \(y\)**.

Now compare middle triples 012 and 123. Among the nine diamonds incident to either triple, the only one whose bottom and complement-top both avoid exactly-one membership in \(\{0,3\}\) is the common diamond
\[
(A,B)=(12,45),\qquad J=\{0,3\}.
\]
Hence both aggregate middle \(y\)-coefficients would have to equal the \(y\)-coefficient of that single common weight. But the exact laws are
\[
g_{012}=\frac{5-3y+2z}{72},\qquad
g_{123}=\frac{5+3y-2z}{72},
\]
whose \(y\)-coefficients are \(-1/24\) and \(+1/24\). Contradiction. ∎

This rules out every fixed affine correction of the signed inverse over the full complex family. It does not rule out nonlinear corrections; (11)–(13) provide them explicitly. It also does not rule out affine formulas on a restricted arc or a small neighborhood.
