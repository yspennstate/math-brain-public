    R−k=log(1+r)−½log(1+a s²/b).                 (1)

It follows by writing c=b+a s². In particular, with s fixed below one,

    R−rk → log 2,   S+rD → 2S,

    U22−(R−rk)²−(S+rD)² → 4/b−log²2−4S².

`4−log²2>0`. Meanwhile U11 has order 1/a, whereas the first row of T
and the off-diagonal Schur entry grow at most logarithmically in 1/a.
Thus the complete two-by-two Schur remainder is positive sufficiently

## 2. Two new boundary theorems

**Theorem 1 (a curved boundary domain).** Throughout

    0≤r,s<1,       1−r²≤(1−s²)²/256,                       (2)

the stronger one-count Schur inequality has the quantitative gap

    U−TTᵀ ≥ ½ diag((1−r²)^−1,1).                          (3)

Interchanging r,s interchanges the two coordinates in (3). The domain permits
s to approach one arbitrarily closely, provided r approaches it sufficiently
faster. It is not contained in the previously proved square r,s≤3/5.

**Theorem 2 (a broader bounded strip).** The same inequality (3) holds on

    999/1000≤r<1,       0≤s≤9/10,                         (4)

and on its reflected domain. Neither theorem asserts the unrestricted square.

Here is the common proof mechanism, with the constant Fisher margin retained.
Assume r≥s and write M=U−TTᵀ. Since k is increasing in s,

    −½log(1−r²) ≤ k ≤ atanh(r²) ≤ R.

The lower endpoint and upper endpoint correspond to s=0 and s=r. Indeed
`∂k/∂s=s(1−r²)/[(1−s²)(1−r²s²)]≥0`. Therefore

    0≤T21=R−rk≤R+(r/2)log(1−r²)≤r.                       (5)

For the last inequality the expression on the right before ≤r vanishes at
r=0 and has derivative `1+½log(1−r²)≤1`; integration proves the claim.
This cancellation is essential: separately bounding R and rk would create
a false logarithmic divergence in the second Schur diagonal.
Also D≤S, |S−sk|≤R, and

    U22=4/b−2a/(bc),        1/b=cosh²S≥1+S².

Consequently the following bounds hold simultaneously on the complete
ordered triangle r≥s:

    M11 ≥ 2/a−(R+sS)²−R²,
    M22 ≥ 3−2a/b²,
    |M12| ≤ 2/b+sS+(3+2S)R.                              (6)

They bound the full integrated Schur remainder, not its isolated homogeneous
coefficients or a sign-changing resolvent density. For example the cross
bound follows from `|U12|≤2/b+2R`, `|T11 T21|≤R+sS`, and
`|T12 T22|≤2RS`.

### Proof of Theorem 1

Let P=diag(sqrt(a),1), and N=PMP. Condition (2) implies r≥s and R≥2S:

    cosh R=1/sqrt(a)≥16/b,
    cosh(2S)=(2−b)/b≤2/b.

Thus (6) yields

    N11≥2−(13/4)aR²,
    N22≥3−1/128,
    |N12|≤1/8+(7/2)sqrt(a)R+sqrt(a)R².                  (7)

Put a0=1/256 and r0=sqrt(255/256). Since a≤a0, r≥r0. At this
endpoint `3<R0<7/2`: the ratio `(1+r0)/(1−r0)` lies between 768 and
1024; `e<3` gives the lower bound, while `log 2<7/10` gives the upper
bound. The latter follows by summing the exponential series through degree
four at 7/10; the resulting rational number exceeds 2. These are exact
elementary bounds.

The functions aR², sqrt(a)R, and sqrt(a)R² decrease for r≥r0. Their
derivative signs are respectively those of `1−rR`, `1−rR`, and `2−rR`;
here `rR≥r0 R0>2`. Therefore

    aR²≤49/1024,
    sqrt(a)R≤7/32,
    sqrt(a)R²≤49/64.

Substitution into (7) gives

    N11≥7555/4096>9/5,
    N22≥383/128>29/10,
    N12²≤(53/32)²=2809/1024<11/4.

Both diagonal entries of N−I/2 are positive, and their product is at least
`(13/10)(12/5)=78/25>11/4`. The two-by-two principal-minor criterion
proves N≥I/2. Congruence by P^−1 proves (3). ∎

## 4. A sign-bearing local replica continuation at Shannon exponent one

The near-boundary proof can be carried into a genuine neighborhood of the
replica exponent, rather than treating an unsigned replica identity as a sign
proof. The following elementary lemma gives an explicit radius once the
strict Shannon curvature certificate is known.

Let p_j(t)>0 be any twice differentiable normalized finite law, and write

    Z(q,t)=sum_j p_j(t)^q,
    C_q= [∂²_t Z(q,t)]/[q(q−1)]  (q≠1).

At the base t=0, normalization gives the exact compensated representation

    C_q=sum_j p_j^(q−2)(p'_j)²
          +sum_j p''_j [p_j^(q−1)−1]/(q−1),
    C_1=FI+sum_j p''_j log p_j=−H''.                     (9)

Let L=max_j|log p_j|. Taylor's theorem applied to exp((q−1)log p_j),
including its integral remainder, yields for |q−1|≤eta

    |C_q−C_1|≤eta exp(eta L)
                 [L FI+(L²/2)sum_j|p''_j|].             (10)

This estimate includes the acceleration term and controls both signs of
q−1. Suppose the affine velocity belongs to a finite real tangent space
with norm |v| and a proven lower bound C_1(v)≥lambda |v|², lambda>0.
Define the finite, explicit coefficient

    B=max_(|v|=1)[L FI(v)+(L²/2)sum_j|p''_j(v)|].

Then with the zero-denominator cases omitted,

    eta=min(1/2,1/L,lambda/(2eB)),

equation (10) proves C_q(v)≥(lambda/2)|v|² whenever |q−1|≤eta.
In particular the sign of ∂²_t Z is the sign of q−1 on this entire
exponent interval for every nonzero velocity in the tangent space.

Apply this lemma to the **actual measured** DPP law at (8), on the
four-dimensional tangent space obtained by applying the fixed A-block
Hadamard to the root's four real diagonal velocities. The strict Q
certificate supplies lambda>0, because measured negative curvature is at
least the touching-envelope form Q. The invertible conversion
`(a0,b0,e,f)↦(a0+re,b0+sf,e,f)` preserves positive definiteness.
All atoms are positive because K is strict. Thus every base in our new
domains has this genuinely sign-certified local replica neighborhood.
On any compact subset of either domain its radius can be chosen uniformly,
by continuity and the strict lower bound. This is a local second-derivative
statement. It does not claim global Rényi concavity away from q=1, nor is
(9) alone offered as a proof of Shannon concavity.
