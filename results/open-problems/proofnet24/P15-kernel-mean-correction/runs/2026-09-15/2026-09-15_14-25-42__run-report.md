\[

\[

=
\overline\Delta_{n-1}^{\rm rel},
\tag{8.1}

where

\[

## 8.2 LOO variance

Use inherited `P15-LOO-EFRON-GAIN-21`.

For its direct-evaluation term

\[
A_j
=
 d_{S^{-j}}(Z_j)-d_{S^{-j}}(Z_j'),
\]

the fit is independent of both evaluation observations. By `(5.4)` and

\[
(a-b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2,

\le

\[
EB_{ij}^2
\le

\tag{8.3}

\[
\overline\Delta_{n-1}^{\rm rel}
\le
\widehat\Delta_{\rm loo}^{\rm rel}
+
\sqrt{
\frac{
2\left[
4(C_{n-1}^{\rm rel})^2
+(n-1)^2(R_{n-1}^{\rm rel})^2
\right]

\tag{8.5}

By `(5.5)`, Jensen and Cauchy--Schwarz,

\[
\begin{aligned}

-
\overline\Delta_{n-1}^{\rm rel}

\Delta_n^{\rm rel}(S)
-
\Delta_{n-1}^{\rm rel}(S^{-I})

\\
&\le

\[
\begin{aligned}

-

&\le
E_Z
|
 d_S^{\rm rel}(Z)
-d_{S^{(j)}}^{\rm rel}(Z)

\boxed{

\le

# 9. `P15-MEANREL-INVERSEMOM-COR-23` — ordinary prediction-stability corollary

The weighted theorem is sharpest because it preserves the actual correlation between output magnitude and predictor instability.

A simpler sufficient condition follows from a conditional inverse-square moment.

Assume

\[
\boxed{
M_{-2}
=
\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_x
E[\|Y\|^{-2}\mid X=x]
<\infty.
}
\tag{9.1}

Define ordinary unweighted fresh-input scales

\[
(C_s^0)^2
=
E\|c_T(X)\|^2,
\tag{9.2}
\]

\[
(R_s^0)^2
=
E P_{T,T'}(X)^2,
\tag{9.3}
\]

\[
(D_n^0)^2
=
E P_{S,S^{-I}}(X)^2.
\tag{9.4}
\]

Because the fresh response is conditionally independent of the training sample given \(X\),

\[
(C_s^{\rm rel})^2
\le
M_{-2}(C_s^0)^2,
\]

\[
(R_s^{\rm rel})^2
\le
M_{-2}(R_s^0)^2,
\]

\[
(D_n^{\rm rel})^2
\le
M_{-2}(D_n^0)^2.
\]

Therefore `(7.2)` implies

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_n^{\rm rel}(S)
\le{}&
\widehat\Delta_{\rm loo}^{\rm rel}(S)
\\
&+
\sqrt{M_{-2}}
\Bigg[
D_n^0
+
\sqrt{\frac{n(R_n^0)^2}{\delta}}
\\
&\qquad\qquad
+
\sqrt{
\frac{
2\left[
4(C_{n-1}^0)^2
+(n-1)^2(R_{n-1}^0)^2
\right]
}{n\delta}
}
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
}

# 13. `P15-MEANREL-INFINITEVAR-CE-23` — denominator obstruction

The denominator issue is not cosmetic.

Consider scalar positive output

\[
Y\sim f(y)=2y\mathbf1_{(0,1)}(y).
\]

Then

\[
E[Y^{-1}]=2<\infty,
\]

but

\[
E[Y^{-2}]=\infty.
\]

Compare two predictor pairs:

\[
m=0,\qquad c=0,
\]

and

\[
m'=0,\qquad c'=\varepsilon
\]

for fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1\).

The prediction perturbation is uniformly tiny:

\[
\|c'-c\|=\varepsilon.
\]

The relative paired-loss difference is

\[
D_\varepsilon(Y)
=
\frac{|Y-\varepsilon|-Y}{Y}.
\]

For \(0<Y<\varepsilon\),

\[
D_\varepsilon(Y)
=
\frac{\varepsilon}{Y}-2.
\]

Its absolute first moment is finite because near zero

\[
|D_\varepsilon(y)|f(y)
\asymp
\frac\varepsilon y\cdot2y
=O(\varepsilon),
\]

but its second moment diverges:

\[
E[D_\varepsilon(Y)^2]
\ge
\int_0^{\varepsilon/4}
\frac{\varepsilon^2}{4y^2}
(2y)\,dy
=\infty.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{arbitrarily small unweighted prediction perturbation does not imply finite-variance stability of the mean-relative loss.}
}
\]

\[
1<p<2,

\[
E[Y^{-p}]
=
\frac2{2-p}<\infty,
\]

so the truncation theorem `(11.3)` still applies under the corresponding uniform predictor bounds.

This demonstrates that the inverse-\(p\) route is genuinely stronger than demanding inverse square integrability.
