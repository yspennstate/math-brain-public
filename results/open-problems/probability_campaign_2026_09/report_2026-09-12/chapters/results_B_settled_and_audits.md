## The settled problems, the E, F and G series, and what the audits found

This chapter carries the mathematics of the results the campaign settled, together with the audit
findings that qualify them. It is in three parts. Part 1 is the thirteen problems written up on
8 September in the memo's own numbering, each with the problem as its source poses it, the argument at
the length the mathematics needs, the machine-checked part where there is one, and the numerical
check. Part 2 is the E, F and G series: the results labelled proved or refuted, the restricted cases,
the two explicitly known rows, and the partials whose remaining obligation is named. Part 3 is the
audits, which is where the corrections live.

Two standing qualifications apply to everything below and are not repeated in each section. The first
is the memo's own: for all of these the mathematics is settled and the prior art is not. A handful of
web searches turned up nothing, which is a bounded search and not a clearance. The second is the
9 September audit's count: zero newly certified full solutions, where certification means full source
correctness, independent verification and a verified check that the problem was open. Seventeen ledger
rows carry a full-answer claim. A claim is not a certificate.

Where a section says a step was re-derived, that means it was re-derived for this report from the
statement of the problem, not read off the memo. Those re-derivations are recorded in a sentence at
the end of each section, and the two places where my reading differs from the record are stated
neutrally where they arise.

### Part 1. The thirteen problems settled on 8 September

The numbering is the memo's own. Twelve sections cover thirteen problems, because Agresti and Veraar's
Problems 4 and 5 fall to one construction and are treated together; the partial that came out of the
same session, P0189, closes the part.

### 1. Aldous and Fill, Open Problem 7.27: refuted by the twin-split cycle

*Reversible Markov Chains and Random Walks on Graphs*, Chapter 7, dated 31 January 1994, page 259:

> Is it true that, over arc-transitive graphs excluding the $n$-cycles, tau* = $O(n \log n)$,
> tau$_2$ = $O(n)$ and tau*$/(2$tau$_0) = 1 + o(1)$?

The sentence immediately before it is what makes the question answerable rather than merely hard:

> Example 7.14 shows that one can invent vertex-transitive graphs which mimic the $n$-cycle, but it
> is not clear whether such arc-transitive graphs exist.

The counterexample is $C_m[\bar K_2]$ for odd $m \ge 5$: the $m$-cycle with every vertex split into
two twins that are not joined to each other. Vertices are pairs $(i,a)$ with $i \in \mathbb{Z}_m$ and
$a \in \{0,1\}$, and $(i,a) \sim (j,b)$ exactly when $j = i \pm 1$. So $n = 2m$, the graph is
4-regular, connected, and it is not a cycle.

Their Example 7.14 is the ladder $\mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ with generators $(1,0)$,
$(-1,0)$, $(0,1)$, and the whole content of that example is that its rungs are distinguishable from
its cycle edges: the mean hitting time $E_v T_w$ depends on which kind of edge joins $v$ to $w$, so no
automorphism carries a rung to a cycle edge and the graph is vertex-transitive but not
arc-transitive. Deleting the rungs removes exactly that obstruction and changes nothing else about the
geometry. The twin-swap maps are automorphisms because adjacency depends only on the base coordinate:
for each $i$ independently one may exchange $(i,0)$ and $(i,1)$.

For arc-transitivity, the group generated by the rotation $(i,a) \mapsto (i+1,a)$, the reflection
$(i,a) \mapsto (-i,a)$ and the single fibre swap at $i=0$ contains the full twin-swap group
$(\mathbb{Z}_2)^m$ together with the dihedral group of the base cycle, so it has order $2^m \cdot 2m$,
and it acts transitively on the $8m$ arcs. Verified mechanically at $m = 5, 7, 9$: the orbit of one
arc has size $40$, $56$, $72$ against $8m = 40$, $56$, $72$, and the group orders come out
$320$, $1792$, $9216$ against $2^m \cdot 2m$.

For the hitting times, write $g(j)$ for the mean hitting time from a vertex to a vertex at cycle
distance $j \ge 1$, and $t$ for the twin hitting time (distance $0$, different fibre element). The
walk's projection to the base coordinate is the simple random walk on $C_m$ slowed by nothing at all:
from $(i,a)$ the walk moves to one of the four neighbours, two in each adjacent fibre, so the base
coordinate performs the simple walk on $C_m$. But the target is one specific vertex of its fibre, and
on arrival in the target fibre the walk lands on the right twin with probability $1/2$. The claim is

$$g(j) = j(m-j) + m, \qquad t = 1 + g(1) = 2m .$$

To see that these solve the hitting system it is enough to check the harmonic equations. For
$1 \le j \le m-1$ the one-step equation is $g(j) = 1 + \tfrac12 g(j-1) + \tfrac12 g(j+1)$ with
$g(0)$ interpreted as the twin value $t$ at $j=0$; equivalently $2g(j) = 2 + g(j-1) + g(j+1)$, which
$j(m-j)+m$ satisfies identically, and at $j=0$ the value $g(0) = m$ is the average of the two
fibre-mates' values, half of $t = 2m$ and half of $0$ for the target itself. Both identities were
checked symbolically over all odd $m$ in a range and all $j$, and they hold as polynomial identities
in $j$ rather than merely on the admissible range. Uniqueness of the solution of $(I-P)h = 1$ off the
target state for a finite irreducible chain does the rest.

Solving the hitting system numerically by linear algebra at $m = 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 21$ reproduces
$g(j) = j(m-j)+m$ for every $j$ and every $m$ tested, and the twin value $2m$ exactly.

Now the three quantities. The maximal mean hitting time is $\max_j g(j) = \lfloor m^2/4\rfloor + m$,
which is quadratic in $n = 2m$ and so not $O(n \log n)$. The average hitting time, which by the
random target lemma equals $\sum_{k \ge 2} 1/\lambda_k$ and is independent of the starting state, is

$$\text{tau}_0 = \frac{1}{m}\Big(t + \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} g(j)\Big) = \frac{m^2-1}{6} + m ,$$

using the classical cycle sum $6\sum_{j<m} j(m-j) = m(m^2-1)$. Computed values agree exactly:
$9$, $15$, $22\tfrac13$, $31$, $52\tfrac13$, $94\tfrac13$ at the six sizes against
$(m^2-1)/6+m$.

The relaxation time is the cycle's own. The adjacency matrix factors as
$A(C_m[\bar K_2]) = A(C_m) \otimes J_2$ with $J_2$ the all-ones $2 \times 2$ matrix, whose eigenvalues
are $2$ and $0$; so the eigenvalues of $A$ are $4\cos(2\pi k/m)$ together with $m$ copies of $0$, the
transition matrix $A/4$ has eigenvalues $\cos(2\pi k/m)$ and $0$, and

$$\text{tau}_2 = \frac{1}{1-\cos(2\pi/m)} \sim \frac{m^2}{2\pi^2} = \frac{n^2}{8\pi^2},$$

again quadratic, not $O(n)$. Measured against the formula at all six sizes: $1.44721$, $2.65597$,
$4.27432$, $6.29935$, $11.56677$, $22.50874$, exact to the printed digits.

For the third claim, $\lfloor m^2/4 \rfloor + m$ over $2\,\text{tau}_0 \sim m^2/3$ tends to
$3/4$, and twice that quantity over $2\,\text{tau}_0$ tends to $3/2$. Either way the ratio does not
tend to $1$, so the third claim fails with the other two.

The machine-checked part is `P0208.lean`, which in the campaign's Lean tree carries nine theorems: the
harmonic identity `g_harmonic`, the twin value `twin_hitting`, the fibre-average identity
`g_zero_is_fibre_average`, the maximum $4j(m-j) \le m^2$ with its attainment at odd $m$ (`hit_le`,
`hit_max_odd`, `tau_star_odd`), the classical cycle sum `cycle_sum`, the closed form for tau$_0$
(`tau0_formula`), and the two asymptotic identities `ratio_three_halves` and `tau_star_quadratic`.
The `#print axioms` lines report `propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound` and nothing else, with
no `sorry` and no `native_decide`. The memo records that Lean caught two of its author's own algebra
slips, in the ratio and growth statements, before they reached the report. Not formalised:
uniqueness of the solution of $(I-P)h = 1$, and the tensor eigenvalue computation. Both are standard
and both were checked numerically.

I checked this independently. Every hitting-time formula, the twin value, tau$_0$ and tau$_2$ reproduce
exactly at $m = 5,7,9,11,15,21$, the harmonic identity holds as a polynomial identity, and
arc-transitivity comes out mechanically at $m = 5,7,9$ with the predicted group order. One difference
of reading. The quantity the memo tabulates as tau* is $2\lfloor m^2/4\rfloor + 2m$, which is twice
the maximal mean hitting time I compute ($11$ at $m=5$ against the memo's $22$), and the limit $3/2$
for tau*$/(2\,$tau$_0)$ follows from that convention; under the reading tau*
$= \max_{i,j} E_i T_j$ the same graph gives $3/4$. Both readings are quadratic and neither is
$1 + o(1)$, so the refutation of all three claims is unaffected, but a paper quoting the constant
needs the convention fixed against the source. The 10 September publishability audit flags the same
area from the other side, listing a graph definition question among the reasons P0208 is a weaker
first publication choice.

### 2. Aldous and Janson, Open Problem 14: the drawn length is linear, not $n \log^4 n$

*The Critical Beta-splitting Random Tree II*, arXiv:2303.02529, pages 54-55:

> Prove that $L_n$ grows roughly like $n \log^4 n$

where $L_n = E[\text{drawn length of } \mathrm{DTCS}(n)]$ and drawn length is defined by their
recursion (73),

$$l(\chi_m) = l(\chi_i) + l(\chi_{m-i}) + 2 + |h(\chi_{m-i}) - h(\chi_i)| ,$$

$h$ being the height of a subtree. The proposed rate is wrong: the drawn length is $\Theta(n)$.

There are two steps, and the first is deterministic and holds for every full binary tree, not only for a random
one. A full binary tree with $k$ leaves has height at most $k-1$, so the increment
$2 + |h_R - h_L|$ at an internal node is at most $2 + \max(h_L,h_R) \le n_L + n_R$ where $n_L, n_R$
are the leaf counts of the two subtrees. Summing the recursion over internal nodes therefore gives

$$\mathrm{drawn}(T) \;\le\; \sum_{\text{leaves } v} \mathrm{depth}(v),$$

and in fact the recursion telescopes exactly to
$\mathrm{drawn}(T) = \sum_{\text{internal } u} h(T_u) + h(T)$, which is the cleaner statement of the
same content. The second step is the authors' own Theorem 3: the expected depth of a uniformly chosen
leaf of $\mathrm{DTCS}(n)$ is asymptotically $(1/2\zeta(2))\log^2 n = 3\pi^{-2}\log^2 n$. Combining,

$$E[\mathrm{drawn}] \;\le\; n \cdot E[\text{leaf depth}] \;\sim\; \frac{3}{\pi^2}\, n \log^2 n
\;=\; o(n \log^4 n).$$

Where the heuristic broke. One factor of $m$. The authors' own text states that the mean number of
subclades of size $m$ in $\mathrm{DTCS}(n)$ equals $(n/m)\,a(n,m)$, with the fixed-$m$ limit
$a(m) = 6h_{m-1}/(\pi^2(m-1))$, so the clade count is of order $n \log m / m^2$. Their heuristic (75)
carries $m^{-1}\log m$ where $m^{-2}\log m$ belongs, which is what happens if $n\,a(n,m)$ is used as
the clade count instead of $(n/m)\,a(n,m)$. With the wrong count the sum to be estimated is
$\sum \log^3 m / m$, which diverges like $\log^4 n$ and reproduces the conjectured rate exactly; with
the right count it is $\sum \log^3 m / m^2$, which converges and gives $\Theta(n)$. The one-line check
that catches it is the hard constraint $\sum_m \mathrm{count}(m) = n-1$: a count of order
$n \log m / m$ is impossible, because it would sum to $n \log^2 n$.

The simulation, with the sampler validated first. The split of a clade of size $m$ into parts
$\{i, m-i\}$ has probability proportional to $1/(i(m-i))$, normalised by
$\sum_{i<m} 1/(i(m-i)) = (2/m)H_{m-1}$. Sampling that recursion and evaluating the drawn-length
recursion gives $L_n/n = 4.81$, $4.82$, $4.88$, $4.84$ at $n = 10^3$, $4\times10^3$, $1.6\times10^4$,
$6.4\times10^4$ -- flat across a 64-fold range of $n$ over which $\log^4 n$ grows by a factor of
$6.6$. The memo's own run reports $4.71$ through $4.86$ over a 400-fold range. Before believing
either, the sampler was checked against the paper's own formulas: clade counts match $(n/m)a(m)$ to
four figures, the mean leaf depth matches $3\pi^{-2}\log^2 n$ in shape and constant, and
$E[h(T_m)]/\log^2 m$ is flat near $0.8$, consistent with the authors' still-open Open Problem 4.

The machine-checked part is `P0340.lean`, which formalises full binary trees as an inductive type and
proves `height_succ_le_leaves` (height $+\,1 \le$ leaves), `sumDepth_shift`, the inequality
`drawn_le_sumDepth`, and the exact decomposition `drawn_eq_sumHeights_add_height`. Standard axioms
only, no `sorry`.

A reading trap the memo records and it is worth keeping. The PDF text extractor renders $\log^2$
as "log2", so Theorem 3 reads $3\pi^{-2}\log 2\,n$ and looks like a logarithm to base two. It is log
squared; the authors' own heuristic on page 13 derives the integral as $(3/\pi^2)\log^2 n$
explicitly. Read as base two, the mean leaf depth becomes linear in $\log n$ and the refutation
evaporates.

I checked this independently. The deterministic inequality was checked exhaustively over all $2056$ full
binary trees with at most nine leaves: drawn length never exceeds the sum of leaf depths, and
height $+\,1$ never exceeds the leaf count. The simulation reproduces a flat $L_n/n$ near $4.8$.
Agrees.

### 3. Hasebe, Ueda and Wang, Problem 4.7: answered, no

*Log-unimodality for free positive multiplicative Brownian motion*, arXiv:2009.13848:

> if nu is a probability measure supported on [alpha, beta] such that $0 < $ alpha $ < $ beta, does
> there exist a number $D \ge 0$ (depending on nu) such that sigma$_t \boxtimes$ nu is log-unimodal
> for all $t \ge D$?

No. The two-atom measure nu $= (\delta_{1/10} + \delta_{10})/2$, supported on $[1/10,10]$, is
log-unimodal for no $t \ge 5\pi$.

Their Lemma 4.1(3) says sigma$_t \boxtimes$ nu is log-unimodal if and only if for
every $R \in (0,\pi)$ the equation

$$\frac{\sin R}{R} \int \frac{r\xi}{1 + r^2\xi^2 - 2r\xi\cos R}\, d\text{nu}(\xi) = \frac{1}{t}$$

has at most two solutions in $r$. Writing $r = e^u$ and the atoms as $e^{\pm a}$ with $a = \log 10$,
the integral is exactly

$$F_R(u) = \tfrac12\big(K_R(u-a) + K_R(u+a)\big), \qquad
K_R(s) = \frac{1}{2(\cosh s - \cos R)},$$

because $1 + e^{2(u\mp a)} - 2e^{u \mp a}\cos R = 2e^{u\mp a}(\cosh(u \mp a) - \cos R)$ and the
factor $e^{u\mp a}$ cancels against the numerator.

The choice of $R$. Take the unique $R \in [\pi/2,\pi)$ with $R/\sin R = t/10$. Since $R/\sin R$
equals $\pi/2$ at $R = \pi/2$ and increases to infinity as $R \uparrow \pi$, such an $R$ exists
exactly when $t \ge 5\pi$. For that $R$ the right-hand side of the criterion, $R/(t \sin R)$, is
exactly $1/10$.

Two uniform bounds give four roots. With $\cos R \le 0$ on $[\pi/2,\pi)$,

$$F_R(0) = \frac{1}{2(\cosh a - \cos R)} \le \frac{1}{2\cosh a} = \frac{10}{101} < \frac{1}{10},$$

while at the atoms $F_R(\pm a) \ge \tfrac12 K_R(0) = 1/(4(1-\cos R)) \ge 1/8 > 1/10$, and
$F_R \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$. So $F_R - 1/10$ is negative at $-\infty$, positive at $-a$, negative at
$0$, positive at $a$, negative at $+\infty$: four sign changes, hence at least four roots, hence not
log-unimodal. Both bounds hold uniformly over the whole admissible range of $R$, which is what makes
this a proof rather than a numerical observation: maximising $F_R(0)$ and minimising $F_R(a)$ over
$R \in [\pi/2,\pi)$ gives $0.099010$ and $0.129901$, straddling $1/10$ with room.

On scope, their Theorem 4.4 gives log-unimodality for large $t$ under a hypothesis that this
measure fails: $\beta^4 - 3\alpha^4 = 9999.9997$ is not less than $2\alpha^3\beta = 0.02$. That is
the point of the example. Their Theorem 4.5 and Example 4.8 counterexamples have support
accumulating at $0$ or unbounded, as they say themselves, which is exactly why Problem 4.7 asks about
the case bounded away from zero. The root structure also matches the mechanism of their own
Theorem 4.4 proof: their $\Theta_R$ is monotone outside $[1/\beta,1/\alpha]$, so two roots sit
outside that interval and the two extra ones sit inside, in the middle region that the dropped
hypothesis exists to control.

Control, both ways, and an instrument trap. On three cases where Theorem 4.4 does apply
($\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 1.1, 1.2, 1.3$) the same root counter returns at most two roots at
$t = 1.01D$ and $t = 10D$, agreeing with their proved theorem. The trap is worth recording: a uniform
grid scan over $R$ reports only two roots for the candidate at $t = 10^5$, because the relevant $R$
sits within $3 \times 10^{-4}$ of $\pi$ and falls outside a grid capped at $\pi - 10^{-3}$. Solve for
$R$, do not scan for it.

I checked this independently. The exact form of $F_R$, the two uniform bounds ($0.099010$ and $0.129901$),
and the existence threshold $t \ge 5\pi$ all reproduce. Solving for $R^*$ and counting sign changes
of $F_{R^*} - 1/t$ on $[-8,8]$ gives four at $t = 5\pi + 10^{-6}$, $t = 20$, $t = 200$ and
$t = 10^5$, so the failure is not a small-$t$ artefact and the grid trap does not bite when $R$ is
solved for. Agrees.

### 4. Agresti and Veraar, Problems 4 and 5: both answered, no

*Nonlinear parabolic stochastic evolution equations in critical spaces*, journal version 2025,
page 130.

> Problem 4. In the variational setting of Theorem 6.4, is there a constant $C_T$ such that for
> all $u_0 \in L^2(\Omega;H)$ one has
> $E \sup_{t \le T} \|u(t)\|_H^2 \le C_T(1 + E\|u_0\|_H^2)$?

> Problem 5. If $u_0^n \to u_0$ in $L^2(\Omega;H)$, does
> $u^n \to u$ in $L^2(\Omega; L^2(0,T;V) \cap C([0,T];H))$?

Both fail, and they fail on the model example the authors name as the one they cannot settle:
Allen-Cahn on the one-dimensional torus with one-dimensional quadratic noise,
$du = (\Delta u + u - u^3)\,dt + \gamma u^2\,dW$, at the critical $\gamma^2 = 2$. Their words: "For
$\gamma^2 < 2$, Section 6.3.2 gives an $L^2(\Omega)$-bound. But for $\gamma^2 = 2$ we do not know
whether it holds."

The reduction to a Bessel process. With deterministic constant initial data $u_0 \equiv a > 0$
the solution stays spatially constant, because $\Delta u = 0$ on constants and the noise is
one-dimensional, and it solves the scalar equation

$$dU = (U - U^3)\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,U^2\,dW .$$

Put $Y = 1/U$. Ito's formula with $\mu = U-U^3$ and $\sigma = \sqrt2 U^2$ gives
$dY = -U^{-2}dU + U^{-3}\sigma^2 dt = (-Y + 3/Y)\,dt - \sqrt{2}\,dW$. Put $Y = e^{-t}V$: then
$dV = 3e^{2t}V^{-1}dt - \sqrt{2}e^{t}dW$. Change time by $s = (e^{2t}-1)/2$, so that $ds = e^{2t}dt$
and the driving martingale has quadratic variation $ds$: then $dV = (3/V)\,ds - \sqrt2\,d\tilde W$.
Finally $R = V/\sqrt2$ satisfies

$$dR = \frac{3}{2R}\,ds - d\tilde W ,$$

which is a Bessel process of dimension $\delta$ with $(\delta-1)/2 = 3/2$, that is $\mathrm{BES}(4)$,
started from $R_0 = 1/(\sqrt2 a)$, and

$$U_t = \frac{e^t}{\sqrt2\, R_s}, \qquad s = \frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}.$$

The reverse direction confirms it. From $U = e^t/(\sqrt2 R)$ one has $\partial_t U = U$,
$\partial_R U = -U/R$, $\partial_R^2 U = 2U/R^2$, so
$dU = U\,dt - \tfrac12 e^{2t}(U/R^2)\,dt - (U/R)\,d\beta$, and substituting
$1/R = \sqrt2\, U e^{-t}$ turns the drift correction into exactly $-U^3\,dt$ and the martingale term
into $\sqrt2\,U^2\,dW$.

Why the expectation diverges. From the representation, $\sup_{t\le T} U_t^2 \ge 1/(2\inf_{s \le
S} R_s^2)$ with $S = (e^{2T}-1)/2$. A $\mathrm{BES}(4)$ is the norm of a four-dimensional Brownian
motion, and the chance that a four-dimensional Brownian motion enters a ball of radius $x$ by a fixed
time is comparable to the ball's Newtonian capacity, of order $x^2$. Over the infinite horizon the
probability is exactly $(x/R_0)^2$, because $r \mapsto r^{-2}$ is harmonic for
$\mathrm{BES}(4)$; the finite-horizon probability is a positive fraction of it, since a
$\mathrm{BES}(4)$ conditioned to hit small levels is a $\mathrm{BES}(0)$, which reaches zero in
finite time almost surely. So $P(\sup U^2 > y) \asymp 1/y$: tail index one, and
$E\sup_{t\le T}\|u(t)\|_H^2 = \infty$ for every $T > 0$ while $E\|u_0\|_H^2 = a^2$ is finite.
Problem 4 is refuted.

Problem 5 follows from the same example with no extra work. Take $u_0^n \equiv 1/n \to 0$ on one
prescribed Brownian space; the limit solution is identically zero, and convergence in
$L^2(\Omega; C([0,T];H))$ would require $E\sup\|u^n\|^2 \to 0$, which is $\infty$ for every $n$.

One instrument note. A first simulation appeared to contradict the $x^2$ scaling, with $P/x^2$ falling
through $1.53$, $1.24$, $0.84$, $0.24$. That was the step size: at 3000 steps the path moves $0.033$
per step while the balls being probed had radius $0.025$ and $0.0125$, so the discretised path stepped
over them. Refining the steps at fixed $x$ makes $P/x^2$ climb toward a positive constant.

Not Lean-checked, and the memo says why: there is no finite arithmetic core here, the content
being Ito calculus and a capacity estimate. The 10 September review adds that the Lean components
supplied for this result certify conditional algebraic and measure-theoretic statements and not the
stochastic construction, so the package must not be described as a full Lean proof.

I checked this independently. The chain of transformations was redone symbolically: $Y = 1/U$ gives drift
$-Y + 3/Y$ and diffusion $-\sqrt2$ exactly; the time change gives $\mathrm{BES}(4)$ with
$\delta = 4$ solved from $(\delta-1)/2 = 3/2$; and the reverse Ito computation returns drift
$U - U^3$ and diffusion $\sqrt2 U^2$ after substituting $R = e^t/(\sqrt2 U)$. A small-ball simulation
of $\mathrm{BES}(4)$ reproduces the step-size artefact and then the scaling: at $x = 0.2$ the ratio
$P/x^2$ climbs $1.48$, $1.57$, $1.66$ as the step count goes $3\times10^3$, $3\times10^4$,
$3\times10^5$, and at $x = 0.1$ it climbs $1.32$, $1.50$, $1.71$, in both cases toward a positive
finite-horizon constant rather than to zero. Agrees.

### 5. Aldous and Fill, Open Problem 4.30: answered, no

Same book, page 137. Writing $S_t = \int_0^t g(X_u)\,du$ for a standardized $g$ along a stationary
reversible chain, sigma$^2$ for the asymptotic variance rate of their Proposition 4.29, and tau$_2$
for the relaxation time:

> Is there some explicit function psi$(b,s) \to 0$ as $s \to \infty$, not depending on the chain,
> such that for standardized $g$ and continuous-time chains,
> $\sup_x |P(S_t/(\text{sigma}\sqrt t) \le x) - P(Z \le x)| \le$ psi$(\|g\|_\infty, t/$tau$_2)$?

No. Take two independent reversible chains on a four-state product space. $Y$ is a two-state chain
with rates $0 \to 1$ equal to eps and $1 \to 0$ equal to $1 - $eps, so its stationary mass at $1$ is
eps and its spectral gap is exactly $1$; $Z$ is a fair sign flipping at rate $R/2$, with gap $R$.
With eps $= s^{-2}$ and $R = s^4$, set

$$A = 2\text{eps} + \sqrt{(1-\text{eps})(1-4\text{eps})}, \qquad
B = \frac{2-A}{1-\text{eps}}, \qquad g = A Z + B (Y - \text{eps}).$$

Then exactly and at every $s$: $Eg = 0$; $Eg^2 = A^2 + B^2\,$eps$(1-$eps$) = 1$;
$\|g\|_\infty = A + B(1-$eps$) = 2$; and tau$_2 = 1$, since the product chain's gap is the smaller of
$1$ and $R$. Take $t = s$, so $t/$tau$_2 \to \infty$ with the sup norm pinned at $2$.

Why it stays non-normal, and it is not because the functional vanishes. The normalized functional
has standard deviation close to $1$: the asymptotic variance rate
sigma$^2 = 2A^2/R + 2B^2\,$eps$(1-$eps$)$ has converged on the time scale $t = s$, because $Y$'s
relaxation time is $1 \ll t$. The obstruction is shape. The expected number of visits of $Y$ to its
rare state during $[0,t]$ is eps$\,t = 1/s \to 0$, so $S_t$ is compound Poisson with vanishing
intensity: with probability $1 - O(1/s)$ no visit occurs, $S_t$ sits at the deterministic value
$-B\,$eps$\,t$ plus negligible fast noise from $Z$, and the entire variance is carried by the
vanishingly rare single excursion. That is as far from Gaussian as a distribution with the right
variance can be.

The arithmetic is clean enough to do in closed form. With eps $= s^{-2}$, $R = s^4$, $t = s$ and
$B \to 1$, the deterministic location in normalized units is

$$\frac{-B\,\text{eps}\,t}{\text{sigma}\sqrt t}
\;\approx\; \frac{-s^{-1}}{\sqrt{2s^{-2}\cdot s}} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2s}},$$

which is $-0.129$, $-0.0707$, $-0.0408$, $-0.0224$ at $s = 30, 100, 300, 1000$, while the mass at
that point, $(1-$eps$)e^{-\text{eps}\,t}$, is $0.966$, $0.990$, $0.997$, $0.999$. So the distribution
function jumps from about $0$ to about $0.999$ at a point where $\Phi$ is $0.449$, $0.472$, $0.484$,

### 7. Pollett, Section 2 Conjecture (ii): answered no, and probably known

For a lambda-transient irreducible countable transient class with almost sure absorption, must every
finite strictly positive limit of $t^{\alpha} e^{\lambda t} p_t(i,j)$ factor as $A x_i m_j$?

No. Take the nearest-neighbour continuous-time walk on $\mathbb{Z}$ with rate $1$ to each side, killed
at rate kappa everywhere and at an extra rate $v$ at the origin. The class is lambda-transient with
lambda $=$ kappa, and

$$t^{3/2} e^{\text{kappa}\, t}\, p_t(i,j) \;\longrightarrow\;
\frac{(|i| + 2/v)(|j| + 2/v) + ij}{4\sqrt{\pi}} .$$

The $ij$ term is the obstruction. Writing $u_i = |i| + 2/v$ and $w_i = i$, the limit kernel is
$u u^{\mathsf T} + w w^{\mathsf T}$, a rank-two form, and its $2\times2$ minor on rows and columns
$\{0,1\}$ is

$$\frac{(u_0 w_1 - u_1 w_0)^2}{(4\sqrt\pi)^2} = \frac{(2/v)^2}{16\pi} > 0$$

for every $v$, so no rank-one factorisation $A x_i m_j$ can exist. The reason in one sentence: the
chain has two ends, the limit kernel carries one degree of freedom per end, and $A x_i m_j$ can encode
only one. Any conjecture of this shape needs a one-end hypothesis.

The Feynman-Kac semigroup was solved directly -- the free-walk generator minus a point
mass $v$ at the origin, exponentiated on a lattice wide enough that the boundary is invisible -- at
$t = 100, 400, 1600, 6400$ with $v = 2$. Against the claimed limits $0.141047$, $0.282095$,
$0.705237$, $0.423142$ for $(i,j) = (0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$, $(1,-1)$, the computed values converge
monotonically to $0.141010$, $0.282004$, $0.705018$, $0.422932$, and the minor formed from the
measured entries is $0.019889$ against the predicted $0.019894$.

Caveat, and it is the campaign's own. Unlike the other results in this part, this row's ledger
entry already carries a specific prior-art warning: Foley and McDonald published a discrete two-end
construction from which the same continuous-time obstruction follows. That kind of warning usually
proves right -- a two-ended killed walk is the obvious discrete analogue and the rank-two structure is
identical -- so this should be treated as confirmed mathematics that is probably known. The
10 September comparison lists the same identification for the neighbouring row P0288 and says plainly
that a rederivation should not be sold as a new answer.

### 8. Burdzy and Scheutzow, Problem 7.5: yes, by a classical technique

*Forward Brownian motion*, arXiv:1302.6958, Problem 7.5, page 26:

> Is it true that for any process $\{X_t, t \ge 0\}$ whose law is equivalent to BM, we can find some
> random piece which we can put in front of $X$ such that the new process is Brownian motion?

Yes, and under absolute continuity rather than equivalence, which is weaker than the problem assumes.

The argument rests on a filling-scheme lemma: if theta is an invertible ergodic measure-preserving
transformation of a standard Borel space $(E,P)$ and $Q = fP$, then there is an almost surely finite
integer $N \ge 0$ with $\mathrm{law}(\text{theta}^N Z) = Q$ for $Z \sim P$. Apply it with
$E = C_0(\mathbb{R})$ under two-sided Wiener measure and $(\text{theta}\,w)(t) = w(t+1) - w(1)$,
lifting the one-sided density of $X$'s law to two-sided paths. The lemma returns a finite $N$ for
which $\{Z(N+t) - Z(N),\, t \ge 0\}$ has $X$'s law, while $\{Z(t) - Z(0),\, t\ge 0\}$ is a Brownian
motion whose first $N$ units of time are exactly the prefix.

The filling construction was implemented on a finite ergodic system,
$\mathbb{Z}/n$ with the $+1$ shift, for $n = 5, 7, 12, 60$ with random densities: total accepted mass
$1.000000000000$ every time, the law of theta$^N Z$ matching $fP$ to about $10^{-16}$, and $E[N]$
finite. The negative control is the informative one. With a non-ergodic theta -- the shift by $2$ on a
cycle of even length, with $f$ supported on the odd class -- exactly half the mass is ever accepted and
half survives forever. Ergodicity is load-bearing in the lemma rather than decorative, and the
construction fails in exactly the way it should without it. The $L^1$ ergodic-theorem step in the
infinite-dimensional setting was read rather than verified; it is standard, but that step is checked by
eye only.

Novelty is the weak point, and the campaign's own proof file volunteers it: the lemma "is a
filling construction related to classical work of Chacon-Ornstein, Rost, and Thorisson", and
Thorisson's shift-coupling theory is precisely about reaching an absolutely continuous law by a random
shift along an ergodic flow. The nearest relative was later named explicitly: Last, Morters and
Thorisson, *Unbiased shifts of Brownian motion*, arXiv:1112.5373, which studies random times $T$,
measurable functions of $B$, for which $(B_{T+t} - B_T)$ is a Brownian motion independent of $B_T$,
characterising them through allocation rules that balance local times. Separately, Burdzy maintains a
live open-problems page which does not list Problem 7.5; its nearest entry is a different
concatenation question, listed as unsolved. That page is a curated selection rather than a complete
ledger, so its silence is weak evidence either way, but it is the author's own current record. The
10 September comparison rates this row's risk of being a classical shift-coupling consequence as
strong.

I checked this independently. I reimplemented the filling scheme on $\mathbb{Z}/n$ for $n = 5, 7, 12, 60$: the
accepted mass is $1.000000000000$ with unmatched residue at most $1.5\times10^{-16}$ and $E[N]$
between $0.70$ and $1.71$; the non-ergodic control accepts exactly $0.500000$ and leaves $0.500000$
unmatched forever. Agrees, including the direction of the control.

### 9. Salez, Problem 2.4: the literal statement falls

*Modern aspects of Markov chains: entropy, curvature and the cutoff phenomenon*, arXiv:2508.21055,
Problem 2.4, page 27:

> Prove that cutoff is equivalent to the product condition for random walks on Cayley graphs of
> Abelian groups.

Salez presents this as a workshop prediction reiterated as [52, Conjecture 1] and says that confirming
it "would represent a substantial progress" on two of his other problems. No degree bound appears in
the question as printed.

This row has an unusual history inside the campaign, recorded because the lesson transfers. It was
first published as "flagged, not verified" on the assumption that the ledger's fields were the whole
argument, while a complete written proof was sitting in the campaign's `proofs/` directory. The
correction is the reason Part 3 records "read `proofs/` first" as a standing instruction.

The construction is $G_d = (\mathbb{Z}/2)^d \times (\mathbb{Z}/m_d)$ with
$m_d = 1 + \lceil 2^d \log d / 4\rceil$, and generating set

$$S_d = \{(e_i, k) : i \le d,\ k \ne 0\} \;\cup\; \{(x,0) : x \text{ in the cube},\ x \ne 0\}.$$

The two families are disjoint, the set is inverse-closed and generating, and it excludes the identity,
so this is a simple, connected, undirected, vertex-transitive, unweighted Cayley graph on an abelian
group, of degree $D_d = d(m_d-1) + 2^d - 1$.

The mechanism is a reset. The second family of generators adds a uniform nonzero cube element,
which is a reset: its contribution to the projected generator is
$Q_d = (2^d \Pi_d - I)/(2^d-1)$ with $\Pi_d$ the projection onto the uniform measure on the cube. A
reset commutes with the cube semigroup, so the projected total variation obeys the exact identity
$q_d(t) = e^{-\beta_d t} c_d(\alpha_d t)$ with $\alpha_d = d(m_d-1)/D_d$ and $\beta_d = 2^d/D_d$,
where $c_d$ is the cube's own total-variation profile. The size of $m_d$ is chosen so that
$\beta_d T_d \to 1$, where $T_d$ is the cube's mixing scale: the survival factor $e^{-s}$ therefore
bites exactly on the scale where the cube mixes, and the limiting profile is $e^{-s}$ for
$0 < s < 1$ and $0$ beyond. Continuous decay, so no cutoff.

As for the numbers, $\alpha_d \to 1$ and $\beta_d \sim 4/(d\log d)$, so
$\beta_d T_d = 2^d \log d / (4(m_d-1)) \to 1$ by the definition of $m_d$. The character computation
puts the slowest sector at the first cube coordinate, giving a spectral gap
$\gamma_d = 2\alpha_d/d + \beta_d \sim 2/d$, so
$\gamma_d\, t_{\mathrm{mix}} \sim \tfrac12 \log d \to \infty$ and the product condition holds. The
profile then gives $t(1/4)/T_d \to 1$ and $t(3/4)/T_d \to \log(4/3) = 0.288$, so the ratio
$t(3/4)/t(1/4)$ tends to $\log(4/3)$ rather than to $1$: no cutoff.

Two caveats decide what it is worth. First, the degree is unbounded, of order $\sqrt{|G|}$ times a
logarithm. Problem 2.4 as printed carries no degree bound, so the literal statement falls; whether the
workshop question and [52, Conjecture 1] carry one is what determines the significance, and the
10 September comparison read the modern sources and found no standing bounded-degree restriction
attached to this question, while noting that the old workshop's ratio is printed backwards in its
Conjecture 7 so that the modern formulations govern. Second, the reset mechanism is classical and the
proof file names the Pak-style counterexamples itself; the work is making one live inside an
unweighted abelian Cayley graph. A scoped independent review by another agent is on file, checking the
full character spectrum, the coupling and the mixing-time brackets, and found no gap in scope; it also
records one arithmetic point worth keeping, that the actual global jump rate is
$\theta_d = (2^d-1)/D_d$ and substituting $\theta_d$ for $\beta_d$ in the profile identity is wrong.

The owner retired this line on 10 September because a more general manuscript of his own, *Sparse
unweighted Cayley realizations and instability of cutoff*, covers the result and the sparse-degree
follow-up. So this section is history rather than a live campaign result, and the older padding lemma
should not be revived as a new open target. It is included here because the mechanism is the
transferable part.

I checked this independently. At $d = 8, 12, 20, 40, 80$: $\alpha_d = 0.808, 0.882, 0.937, 0.974, 0.989$
rising to $1$; $\beta_d$ against $4/(d\log d)$ agrees to within twenty per cent at $d = 8$ and to
three figures by $d = 40$; $\beta_d T_d = 0.9932, 0.9998, 1.000000, 1.000000, 1.000000$; and

### 10. Lyons and Peres, Question 17.18: answered, no

*Probability on Trees and Networks*, version 19 August 2026, Question 17.18, page 575: must a
consistent equivariant flow rule admitting a stationary measure *equivalent* to Galton-Watson measure
induce an ergodic Markov chain? Their Proposition 17.17 proves it when all child weights are strictly
positive, and Exercise 17.22 gives a non-ergodic example whose stationary measure is only absolutely
continuous, so equivalence is the point of the question.

No. Take Galton-Watson with $P(D = 2) = 3/5$ and $P(D = 3) = 2/5$, so there are no leaves and the mean
is $12/5$, and define an isomorphism-invariant colour in $\{0,1\}$ on the tree by AND at two-child
vertices and OR at three-child vertices. Both rules preserve unanimity, so every vertex has at least
one child of its own colour; let the flow rule choose uniformly among the children whose colour
matches the vertex's. Then Galton-Watson measure is stationary, the root colour is invariant along the
chain and takes each value with probability $1/2$ -- a non-constant invariant function, hence no
ergodicity -- and the stationary measure is Galton-Watson measure itself, so trivially equivalent to
it. The rule gives weight zero to wrong-coloured children, which places it outside Proposition 17.17
exactly as it must.

That the colour exists is the technical content. Truncate at level $n$, colour the boundary
Bernoulli$(1/2)$, propagate upward, and let $q_n$ be the probability that the root is $1$. The
one-step map is

$$F(p) = \tfrac35 p^2 + \tfrac25\big(1 - (1-p)^3\big),$$

and $F(1/2) = 3/20 + 7/20 = 1/2$, so the marginal is preserved. The root value is a read-once
function of the boundary bits, hence multilinear in them, so $q_n$ is a bounded martingale of mean
$1/2$. Write $r_n = E[q_n^2]$, which is the probability that two independent colourings of the same
tree both give root $1$. At an AND vertex with two children the contribution is $r^2$; at an OR vertex
with three children, both colourings give $1$ unless one of them gives $0$, so the contribution is
$1 - \tfrac18 - \tfrac18 + r^3 = \tfrac34 + r^3$, using that a pair of colourings with marginals
$1/2$ and $P(11) = r$ also has $P(00) = r$. Hence $r_0 = 1/4$ and

$$r_{n+1} = \tfrac{3}{10} + \tfrac35 r_n^2 + \tfrac25 r_n^3 ,$$

whose increment factors exactly as

$$r_{n+1} - r_n = \Big(r_n - \tfrac12\Big)\Big(\tfrac25 r_n^2 + \tfrac45 r_n - \tfrac35\Big).$$

On $[1/4, 1/2]$ the quadratic factor is negative, taking the values $-0.375$ at $1/4$ and $-0.1$ at
$1/2$, and so is $r_n - 1/2$, so $r_n$ increases; the competing root of the quadratic,
$(\sqrt{10}-2)/2 = 0.5811$, lies above $1/2$, so $r_n$ rises to $1/2$ and no further. Then
$E[q_n(1-q_n)] = 1/2 - r_n \to 0$, the martingale limit is $\{0,1\}$-valued, and the colour is a
genuine two-valued function of the uncoloured tree.

The step the proof leaves implicit, checked in the write-up: that Galton-Watson measure really is
stationary. This is not obvious, because the flow's choice depends on a tail function of the tree. It
holds because, conditioned on the children's colours, the child subtrees are independent, each
distributed as Galton-Watson conditioned on its own colour; every admissible child carries the root's
colour $c$, so the chosen subtree is exactly $\mathrm{GW}(\cdot \mid \text{colour} = c)$, the uniform
choice adding no bias because those children are exchangeable given the colour vector, and mixing over
$c$ returns Galton-Watson measure.

Prior art is unchecked, and the tree-automaton and factor-of-iid literature is where to look, since an
AND/OR read-once formula on a Galton-Watson tree with a fixed point at $1/2$ is a natural object. The
10 September comparison rates the construction a strong alternative and says the flow-rule application
needs a dedicated citation review.

I checked this independently. $F(1/2) = 1/2$ exactly in rational arithmetic; the recursion's first exact terms
are $1/4$, $11/32$, $6343/16384$; the increment factorisation is an exact polynomial identity; the
quadratic's roots are $-1 \pm \sqrt{10}/2$ with values $-0.375$ and $-0.1$ at $1/4$ and $1/2$; and the
iteration increases monotonically to $0.4999999999999991$ after four thousand steps, passing $0.4747$
at $n = 10$ and $0.4999986$ at $n = 100$. Agrees.

### 11. Hasebe and Ueda, Problem 3.3: the constant is 1

*Large time unimodality for classical and free Brownian motions with initial distributions*,
ALEA 15 (2018) 353-374, Problem 3.3: what is the optimal universal $C$ such that
mu $\boxplus\, S(0,t)$ is unimodal for all $t \ge C D_{\text{mu}}^2$ and all compactly supported mu?
They prove $C \le 4$ and print $2 \le C \le 4$.

$C = 1$, and it is attained.

For the upper bound, the relevant function is $F_R(u) = \int d\text{mu}(x)/((x-u)^2 + R^2)$, and the claim
is that it has no local minimum of value at most $1/D^2$, where $D$ is the diameter of the support.
Fix a stationary point $u$ and put $y = R^2$. Reweight by

$$dQ \;\propto\; \big((x-u)^2 + y\big)^{-2}\, d\text{mu},$$

and write $s = x - u$, $a = E_Q[s^2]$, $b = E_Q[s^4]$. Stationarity of $F_R$ at $u$ says
$\int 2s\,(s^2+y)^{-2}\,d\text{mu} = 0$, that is $E_Q[s] = 0$: under $Q$ the variable is centred.
Two identities follow immediately. Writing $Z = \int (s^2+y)^{-2}\,d\text{mu}$,

$$F_R(u) = \int \frac{s^2+y}{(s^2+y)^2}\,d\text{mu} = Z\,(a+y), \qquad
1 = \int \frac{(s^2+y)^2}{(s^2+y)^2}\,d\text{mu} = Z\,(b + 2ay + y^2),$$

so that

$$\frac{1}{F_R(u)} = \frac{b + 2ay + y^2}{a+y}.$$

Next the second-derivative condition. Differentiating twice,
$F_R'' = \int\big[-2(s^2+y)^{-2} + 8s^2(s^2+y)^{-3}\big]\,d\text{mu}
= Z\big(6 - 8y\,E_Q[(s^2+y)^{-1}]\big)$, so at a local minimum
$3 \ge 4y\,E_Q[(s^2+y)^{-1}] \ge 4y/E_Q[s^2+y] = 4y/(a+y)$ by Jensen, whence

$$y \le 3a .$$

Two elementary moment inequalities for a centred variable supported in an interval of length $D$
complete the estimate. First, if the support of $s$ lies in $[l,h]$ with $h - l \le D$ and $E[s] = 0$
then $E[(s-l)(h-s)] \ge 0$ gives $a \le -lh \le D^2/4$. Second, taking an independent copy $s'$,
$(s-s')^4 \le D^2(s-s')^2$ pointwise, and for centred variables $E(s-s')^4 = 2b + 6a^2$ while
$E(s-s')^2 = 2a$, so

$$b + 3a^2 \le D^2 a .$$

Now

$$\frac{1}{F_R(u)} - D^2 = \frac{b + 2ay + y^2 - D^2 a - D^2 y}{a+y}
\;\le\; \frac{(y-3a)(y+a) + (4a - D^2)y}{a+y} \;\le\; 0,$$

the first inequality being exactly the statement $b + 3a^2 - D^2 a \le 0$ and the second following
from $y \le 3a$ and $4a \le D^2$. So $F_R(u) \ge 1/D^2$ at every local minimum, which is the upper
bound $C \le 1$.

The equality case is tight. For symmetric Bernoulli on $\{-1,1\}$ at $u = 0$ one has $a = b = 1$
and $D = 2$, and $F_R''$ is proportional to $3 - 4y/(1+y)$, which vanishes exactly at
$y = R^2 = 3 = 3a$ while $4a = 4 = D^2$: precisely the equality condition in both inequalities. There
$F_R(0) = 1/(1+3) = 1/4 = 1/D^2$ and $F_R(s) < F_R(0)$ for all $s \ne 0$, so the point is a strict
maximum and never a local minimum; for $y < 3$ it is a local minimum of value $1/(1+y) > 1/4$.

On the printed lower bound, said once and neutrally. The prior on a refereed paper is that the
reader has misread it, so the Bernoulli threshold was re-derived inside the paper's own frame with its
own criterion, that mu $\boxplus\, S(0,t)$ is unimodal if and only if $\xi_R(u) = 1/t$ has at most two
solutions for every $R$. For Bernoulli, $\xi_R$ has a local minimum at $0$ of value $1/(1+R^2)$ when
$R^2 < 3$, and a third solution appears exactly when $t < 1 + R^2 < 4$, so unimodality holds if and
only if $t \ge 4$, which is their stated threshold, with $D_{\text{mu}} = 2$. But the condition being
optimised is $t \ge C D_{\text{mu}}^2 = 4C$, so $t \ge 4$ forces only $C \ge 1$, not $C \ge 2$.
Either the printed $2$ divided by $D_{\text{mu}}$ instead of $D_{\text{mu}}^2$, or there is a reading
being missed; and if the printed $C \ge 2$ is right under some reading, then this theorem contradicts
the paper and one of the two is wrong. That fork is what to settle first, and the campaign records it
as an open obligation rather than as a correction to the paper.

This is the second Hasebe-Ueda problem in this part, result 3 being their 2020 multiplicative paper.
Two results against one group's stated problems in one session is itself a reason to look hard at the
later literature before claiming either.

I checked this independently. The two exact identities for $F_R(u)$ and $1/F_R(u)$ follow as displayed; the
gap between the numerator and the claimed bound is exactly $b + 3a^2 - D^2 a$, verified symbolically;
$E(s-s')^4 = 2b + 6a^2$ for centred independent copies comes out of the expansion with the cubic
cross terms killed by $E[s] = 0$, giving the coefficient $3$ exactly; the second-derivative formula is
as stated; and the Bernoulli equality case has $3 - 4y/(1+y)$ vanishing at $y = 3$ with

### 12. Aldous and Janson, Ansatz 20: false in both parts

Inside Open Problem 8 of arXiv:2303.02529, which asks for explicit bounds on $|a(n,m) - a(m)|$, the
authors write "In particular, prove the following Ansatz." Ansatz 20 says: for nonnegative $f$ with
$f(j) = O(j^k)$ for some finite $k$, (i) if $\sum_i a(i)f(i)$ converges then
$\sum_{i \le n} a(n,i)f(i)$ tends to it, and (ii) if it diverges then $\sum_{i\le n} a(n,i)f(i)$ is
asymptotic to $\sum_{i \le n} a(i)f(i)$. Here $a(n,j)$ is the probability that the tagged harmonic
descent chain started at $n$ visits $j$, and $a(j) = 6H_{j-1}/(\pi^2(j-1))$ is its fixed-$j$ limit.

Both parts fail, on one observation: $a(n,n) = 1$ exactly. The chain starts at $n$, so it visits $n$
surely, and a test function that charges the point $j = n$ along a subsequence contributes something
to which every fixed-$j$ limit is blind.

For (i), take $f(j) = \mathbf 1\{j = 2^r\}$, which is bounded and so trivially of polynomial growth.
Since $a(2^r) = O(r 2^{-r})$, the limiting series $S = \sum_r a(2^r)$ converges. At $n = 2^K$ the
finite sum is $\sum_{r \le K} a(2^K, 2^r)$, whose top term is $a(2^K,2^K) = 1$, so for any fixed
$M < K$ it is at least $1 + \sum_{r \le M} a(2^K, 2^r)$; letting $K$ and then $M$ go to infinity gives
$\liminf \ge 1 + S$ against a predicted $S$. False by a whole unit.

For (ii), take $g(j) = j\,\mathbf 1\{j = 2^r\}$, so $g(j) \le j$ and the growth hypothesis holds.
Since $a(j)\,j$ is asymptotically $(6/\pi^2)\log j$, the term $a(2^r)2^r$ is of order $r$ and the
truncated limiting sum at $n = 2^K$ is of order $K^2$, while the actual finite-chain sum is at least
its root term $a(2^K,2^K)\,2^K = 2^K$. The ratio $2^K/K^2$ diverges, so the claimed asymptotic
equivalence fails along the powers of two.

The discrepancy is exactly the root term, and no dominated-convergence proof can work. Computing
the visit probabilities directly from $a(n,n) = 1$ and
$a(n,m) = \sum_{i>m} a(n,i)/(H_{i-1}(i-m))$ shows the gap in part (i) converging to $1$ rather than
merely being bounded below by it. And the reason no dominated convergence argument can repair the
Ansatz is quantitative: $\sup_n a(n,i) = a(i,i) = 1$ on the diagonal while
$a(i) \approx (6/\pi^2)\log i / i$, so $\sup_n a(n,i)/a(i) = 1/a(i)$ diverges like $i/\log i$. Any
correct version needs a hypothesis controlling $f$ near the diagonal, not merely polynomial growth.

The repair, which is where the authors' own applications survive. Adding $f(n) \to 0$ to part (i)
makes the gap vanish in every case tested, and the analogue for part (ii) is
$f(n) = o(\sum_{i\le n} a(i)f(i))$. Their applications satisfy it: the Colless weight tends to $1/2$,
so the root term is $O(1)$ against a sum of order $\log^2 n$. Their stated monotonicity observation
also holds numerically -- over seventeen values of $n$ up to $1350$ and nine values of $i$ up to $89$,
$114$ consecutive pairs, no violation of $n \mapsto a(n,i)$ decreasing.

Why this reaches beyond the Ansatz. Aldous and Janson invoke Ansatz 20 to pass from the fixed-$j$
limits to balance-index asymptotics: "we seek to deduce the asymptotics of $E[I(n)]$ from the limits
$a(m)$. At this point we need to invoke Ansatz 20." So those derivations lean on a statement that is
false in general. That is not a claim that their index results are wrong -- the indices they treat use
regular, non-sparse weights, exactly the class the counterexample avoids -- but the Ansatz cannot be
used as stated, and any application needs a hypothesis excluding sparse root-charging test functions.
This is the most consequential single item in the campaign for that reason.

Scope. This refutes the displayed Ansatz, not Open Problem 8 as a whole: explicit bounds on
$|a(n,m) - a(m)|$ remain open and the right regularity class for a true version is undetermined. Note
also that this is the second Aldous-Janson item here; both are cases of a stated auxiliary claim
failing while the surrounding programme stands.

I checked this independently. $a(2^r)$ is summable with $\sum_{r \le 17} a(2^r) = 1.5221$; $a(j)\,j/\log j$ is
$0.690$, $0.659$, $0.646$ at $j = 10^2, 10^3, 10^4$, approaching $6/\pi^2 = 0.6079$ at the rate the
Euler constant in $H_{j-1}$ dictates; and solving the visit recursion gives $a(n,2) = 0.607952$,
$0.607935$, $0.607930$, $0.607928$ at $n = 256, 512, 1024, 2048$ against $6/\pi^2 = 0.607927$. The

root term $1$; the divergence ratios $1/a(i)$ at $i = 2, 5, 13, 55$ are $1.64$, $3.16$, $6.36$,
$19.41$; and the monotonicity check gives no violations in $81$ consecutive pairs. Agrees.

### The partial that came with them: P0189, Brown measures from strong convergence

Not settled, but the work splits cleanly and the split names the remaining step. The candidate
counterexample uses $q \times q$ clock and shift pairs with $U^q = I$ and $V^q = -I$, arranged so that
$U + V$ is nilpotent and every empirical eigenvalue measure is $\delta_0$, against a limiting Brown
measure that is not $\delta_0$.

The concrete half is confirmed exactly. At $q = 3, 4, 5, 8, 13, 32$ the identities $U^q = I$,
$V^q = -I$ and $(U+V)^q = 0$ all hold to machine precision, so $\det(zI - U - V) = z^q$ and the
empirical measure is $\delta_0$ at every $q$. The apparent growth of the largest computed eigenvalue
modulus with $q$ is the standard ill-conditioning of the eigenvalues of a nilpotent matrix rather than
a real spectrum. The operator norms $\|U+V\|$ converge to $2$.

The load-bearing step that is not checked is the other one: whether the clock and shift pair converges
strongly -- norm convergence for every noncommutative polynomial, not only for $U+V$ -- to the
limiting pair, and whether that limit's Brown measure for $u+v$ is what the argument claims. The pair
converges to a rotation-algebra pair rather than to a free pair, and the two have different Brown
measures, so the argument's citation has to match the limit it actually has. The 10 September
comparison reaches the same place from the publication side: it ranks P0189 a serious rival for the
campaign's flagship on reach, and declines to call it the strongest novel result precisely because the
limit is dependent and non-free and the approximation and determinant literature has not been
cleared.

### E0061, Rozikov's Problem 4: the non-cyclic sign condition is sufficient

Rozikov, *Quadratic stochastic operators: results and open problems*, arXiv:0902.4207, page 4. The
Volterra quadratic stochastic operators on the two-dimensional simplex are

$$V:\quad x' = x(1 + ay - bz), \quad y' = y(1 - ax + cz), \quad z' = z(1 + bx - cy),
\qquad a,b,c \in [-1,1].$$

> Theorem 3 [3]. If the parameters $a, b, c$ for the Volterra QSO (9) have the same sign and each is
> non-zero, then the ergodic theorem will fail for this operator.
> Problem 4. Is the condition of Theorem 3 sufficient for the ergodic theorem to hold?

The ergodic theorem in Ulam's sense means that the Cesaro averages $n^{-1}\sum_{k<n} V^k x$ converge
for every $x$ in the simplex. The answer is yes, and the proof is elementary.

Theorem: if it is not the case that $a, b, c$ are all nonzero of the same sign, then every trajectory
of $V$ converges to a fixed point, so in particular the Cesaro averages converge. With the source's
Theorem 3, the ergodic theorem holds if and only if $a, b, c$ are not all nonzero of one sign.

The proof splits on whether a coefficient vanishes. All three coordinates stay in $[0,1]$, they sum to
one, and each coordinate plane is invariant.

Suppose first that some coefficient is zero, say $a = 0$; the other two cases are the same after
relabelling. Then $x_{n+1} = x_n(1 - b z_n)$ and $y_{n+1} = y_n(1 + c z_n)$. Since $b$ has a fixed
sign and $z_n \ge 0$, the sequence $x_n$ is monotone, nonincreasing if $b \ge 0$ and nondecreasing if
$b \le 0$; likewise $y_n$ is monotone. Both are bounded, so both converge, and
$z_n = 1 - x_n - y_n$ converges too, and the limit is a fixed point because $V$ is continuous.

Suppose instead that all three are nonzero and not of one sign. Read the signs as a tournament on
three species: $a > 0$ means $x$ beats $y$, $b > 0$ means $z$ beats $x$, $c > 0$ means $y$ beats $z$.
All positive is the cycle $x \to y \to z \to x$, all negative the reversed cycle, and any mixture of
signs breaks the cycle, so the tournament is transitive and one species beats both others. Call it $w$
and the others $u, v$. Its update is

$$w_{n+1} = w_n\,(1 + p\,u_n + q\,v_n), \qquad p, q > 0,$$

so $w_n$ is nondecreasing and bounded by $1$, hence converges to some $w^* \ge w_0$. If $w_0 > 0$ then
$\prod_{k<n}(1 + p u_k + q v_k) = w_n/w_0$ converges to the finite positive limit $w^*/w_0$, and since
every factor is at least $1$ this forces $\sum_k (p u_k + q v_k) < \infty$, so $u_n \to 0$ and
$v_n \to 0$ and the trajectory converges to the vertex of $w$. If $w_0 = 0$ the trajectory lives on
the edge $\{w = 0\}$, where $V$ restricts to the two-species map $u' = u(1 + \alpha v)$,
$v' = v(1 - \alpha u)$; one coordinate is nondecreasing and the other nonincreasing, both converge,
and the same product argument sends the other to zero unless the growing one starts at zero. In every
case the trajectory converges. Convergent trajectories have convergent Cesaro averages, which is the
theorem. Nothing in the argument needs $|a|,|b|,|c| \le 1$ beyond keeping $V$ a map of the simplex.

The prior-art history is the interesting part of this row, and it is a two-step correction.
Ganikhodjaev and Zanin, arXiv:1205.3841 (2012, in Russian), state as their Theorem 1 that $V$ is
non-ergodic if and only if $a, b, c$ are nonzero and of one sign, which is exactly the equivalence
Problem 4 asks for, and attribute it to their own 2004 note in *Russian Mathematical Surveys* 59:3,
571-572. The row was first marked resolved in the literature on that basis. Reading the 2004 note in
full shows that its only theorem is the necessary condition, the failure in the cyclic case; the
sufficiency is not there. So the 2012 attribution does not hold, Rozikov's 2009 list poses the
sufficiency five years after the note, and the row moved back to proved-here. Novelty is modest: the
tournament picture is R. Ganikhodzhaev's, from Mat. Sb. 1992/93, and the argument may well be folklore
in that school, but it has not been found written out. The 10 September comparison lists E0061 as not a
new-theorem flagship for exactly this reason, since a corrected proof or attribution is a different
thing from a new theorem.

In Lean, `E0061.lean` certifies the product step in the form that matters for the argument: for
nonnegative $f$, $\prod_{k<n}(1+f(k)) \ge 1 + \sum_{k<n} f(k)$ (`prod_one_add_ge`), that a bounded
product forces a summable sum (`sum_le_of_prod`, `summable_of_prod`), and hence that the terms tend to
zero (`tendsto_zero_of_prod`). Standard axioms only.

Two remarks are worth keeping. In the degenerate case the limit need not be a vertex: with $a = 0$ the
whole edge $\{z = 0\}$ consists of fixed points and a trajectory can converge to any point of it, so
the ergodic theorem holds in Ulam's sense while the limit depends on the initial point. And the cyclic
case fails for the opposite reason from the one the proof uses: there the interior fixed point is a
repeller, no coordinate is monotone, and the trajectory spirals out to the boundary, visiting the three
vertices in turn for geometrically growing sojourns (12, 51, 1073 steps in a simulation at
$a = b = c = 1$), which is the mechanism of the 2004 note.

I checked this independently. Iterating the operator 100,000 steps from a random start and then
50,000 more, for six sign patterns: every non-cyclic pattern converges with zero drift over the second
block, and the $a = 0$ pattern converges to an interior point of an edge, $(0.0073, 0.9927, 0)$,
exactly as the degenerate remark says. The two cyclic controls park at a vertex for the whole horizon,
which is what geometrically growing sojourns look like at finite time. Agrees.

### E0063, Rozikov's Problem 10: every xi-QSO converges to the empty body, quadratically

Same source, Section 7. With $E_0 = \{0,1,\dots,m\}$, a set $F$ of females, its complement $M$ and $0$
the empty body, an $F$-QSO has $P_{ij,0} = 1$ whenever $i,j$ lie in the same class or either is $0$,
and arbitrary coefficients on cross pairs.

> Theorem 5 [39]. Any $F$-QSO has a unique fixed point $(1,0,\dots,0)$. Besides, for any
> $x_0 \in S^m$, the trajectory $\{x(n)\}$ tends to this fixed point exponentially rapidly.
> Problem 10. Consider a partition xi $= \{E_1,\dots,E_q\}$ of $E$ ... Assume $P_{ij,k} = 0$ if
> $i,j \in E_p$, for $p = 1,\dots,q$. Call the corresponding operator a xi-QSO. Is an analogue of
> Theorem 5 true for any xi-QSO?

Theorem: let $V$ be a xi-QSO with $q$ blocks, and write $x_0$ for the empty-body mass. Then for every
$x$ in the simplex

$$1 - V(x)_0 \;\le\; \Big(1 - \frac1q\Big)\,(1-x_0)^2. \tag{$*$}$$

Consequently $e_0 = (1,0,\dots,0)$ is the unique fixed point; along every trajectory
$y_{n+1} \le (1-1/q)\,y_n^2$ with $y = 1-x_0$, so $y_n \le (1-1/q)^n y_0$, which is Theorem 5's
exponential rate, and in fact the convergence is quadratic.

For the proof, let $e_p$ be the mass of block $p$, so $x_0 + \sum_p e_p = 1$. Pairs that produce the
empty body with probability one are: the pair $(0,0)$, contributing $x_0^2$; the pairs $(0,i)$ and
$(i,0)$ with $i \in E$, contributing $2x_0(1-x_0)$; and the same-block pairs, contributing
$\sum_p e_p^2$. Cross-block pairs contribute a nonnegative amount which is dropped. So

$$V(x)_0 \;\ge\; x_0^2 + 2x_0(1-x_0) + \sum_p e_p^2
\;\ge\; x_0^2 + 2x_0(1-x_0) + \frac{(1-x_0)^2}{q}$$

by Cauchy-Schwarz, and rearranging gives $(*)$. At a fixed point $y \le (1-1/q)y^2 \le (1-1/q)y$, so
$y = 0$, and $V(e_0) = e_0$ because $P_{00,0} = 1$.

The bound is sharp, and the sharpness says what the constant means: $(*)$ is an equality exactly when
the $q$ block masses are equal, the Cauchy-Schwarz case, and no cross-block pair produces the empty
body. At the other extreme, all mass in one block sends everything to the empty body in a single step.

Nothing about the cross-block coefficients matters, and that is the whole content. The obstruction the
ledger's first attempt recorded -- that cross-block pairs have arbitrary $P_{ij,k}$, so mass produced
at states other than $0$ is uncontrolled and can be recycled -- is real but irrelevant, because the
empty body absorbs at least a fixed fraction of the square of the off-empty mass at every step
whatever the cross-block pairs do.

In Lean, `E0063.lean` proves the one-step inequality from the mass lower bound and Mathlib's
`sq_sum_le_card_mul_sum_sq` (`one_step`), the iteration bound $y_n \le c^n y_0$ (`iterate_bound`), and
the fixed-point conclusion (`fixed_point_zero`), on standard axioms.

On scope, the result depends on the inherited convention that a pair containing the empty body
produces the empty body; the memo says so and explains why it is the only reading under which
$(1,0,\dots,0)$ is a fixed point at all. The publishability comparison records this as an
interpretation-dependent elementary result whose printed source wording is ambiguous, which is the
right weight. As for prior art, Rozikov's own later work on xi$^{(s)}$- and xi$^{(as)}$-QSOs uses a
different notion, a partition of the set of coupled pairs on the two-dimensional simplex; Problem 10
in its literal form was not found addressed.

Two floating-point traps are recorded for any QSO computation, both met on the way: $V$ is homogeneous
of degree two, so an unrenormalised iteration squares any rounding error in the total mass and
collapses to the zero vector once $x_0$ has reached one; and $1-x_0$ loses all precision once $x_0$ is
within $10^{-16}$ of one, so the off-empty mass must be summed directly.

I checked this independently. Four hundred random xi-QSOs with $m$ from $3$ to $8$, $q$ from $2$ to
$5$ and Dirichlet cross-block rows at three concentrations, twenty random starting points each: the
maximum of $(1-V(x)_0)/\big((1-1/q)(1-x_0)^2\big)$ over all evaluations is $0.9746$, never above $1$.
The memo's own run reports $0.9926$ over 6,000 evaluations. Agrees.

### E0064, Rozikov's Problem 11: uniqueness fails for five types

Same source, Problem 11: "Is Theorem 6 true for $m \ge 4$?", where Theorem 6 states that every
strictly non-Volterra quadratic stochastic operator on three types has a unique fixed point, that the
fixed point is not attractive, and that the operator has 2-cycles and 3-cycles depending on the
parameters. A quadratic stochastic operator is $V_k(x) = \sum_{i,j} P_{ij,k}x_ix_j$ with
$P_{ij,k} = P_{ji,k} \ge 0$ and $\sum_k P_{ij,k} = 1$; it is strictly non-Volterra when
$P_{ij,k} = 0$ whenever $k \in \{i,j\}$.

The non-attractiveness clause was already known to fail from $m = 4$ on: the symmetric equiprobable
operator has a globally attracting fixed point (Jamilov, Ladra and Mukhitdinov 2017), and the earlier
attempt on this row recorded it. What was open was uniqueness, and uniqueness fails from $m = 5$ on.

Here is the example at $m = 5$. Types $1,2,3$ form a face $F$; types $4,5$ are the rest. Set, on the
face, the symmetric operator $P_{ii,k} = 1/2$ for $i,k \in F$ with $k \ne i$ and $P_{ij,k} = 1$ when
$\{i,j,k\} = F$; and across, $P_{i4,5} = P_{i5,4} = 1$ for $i \in F$, $P_{44,5} = 1$, $P_{55,4} = 1$,
and $P_{45,k} = 1/3$ for $k \in F$. Every row sums to one, $P$ is symmetric in $(i,j)$, and
$P_{ij,k} = 0$ whenever $k \in \{i,j\}$, so $V$ is a strictly non-Volterra operator on five types. Its
coordinates are, for $k \in F$ with $\{a,b\} = F \setminus \{k\}$,

$$V_k(x) = \frac{x_a^2 + x_b^2}{2} + 2x_ax_b + \frac{2}{3}x_4x_5,$$

and $V_4(x) = 2(x_1+x_2+x_3)x_5 + x_5^2$, $V_5(x) = 2(x_1+x_2+x_3)x_4 + x_4^2$. There are two fixed
points, checked in exact rational arithmetic:

$$p = \Big(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13,0,0\Big), \qquad
q = \Big(\tfrac19,\tfrac19,\tfrac19,\tfrac13,\tfrac13\Big).$$

For $p$: $V_k(p) = (1/9+1/9)/2 + 2/9 = 1/3$ on the face and $V_4 = V_5 = 0$. For $q$:
$V_k(q) = (1/81+1/81)/2 + 2/81 + (2/3)(1/9) = 1/27 + 2/27 = 1/9$ and
$V_4(q) = 2(1/3)(1/3) + 1/9 = 1/3 = V_5(q)$. The point $p$ is the fixed point of the face
restriction, which is the symmetric strictly non-Volterra operator on three types; $q$ has every
coordinate positive. Their Jacobians on the tangent space of the simplex have eigenvalues
$(-2,-1,-1,2)$ at $p$ and $(-4/3,-1/3,-1/3,0)$ at $q$, so $p$ is a saddle of index $-1$ and $q$ has
index $+1$. The eigenvalue $2$ in the radial direction is Euler's identity for a degree-two homogeneous
map and plays no role on the simplex. Neither point is attractive.

For every $m \ge 6$ there is a two-face construction. Split the types into two disjoint triples $F$
and $G$ and choose any strictly non-Volterra operator whose pairs inside $F$ land in $F$ and whose
pairs inside $G$ land in $G$, with cross pairs anywhere allowed. The restriction to the face spanned
by $F$ is a strictly non-Volterra operator on three types, so by the source's own Theorem 6 it has a
fixed point in that face; likewise for $G$; and the two are distinct. Numerically, thirty random
operators of this kind on six types gave two fixed points in twenty-seven cases and three in three
cases, the third being a fully interior point. For $m \ge 7$, pad with further types.

The trick stops at $m = 5$ for a reason that also explains why $m = 4$ is different. Two invariant
triples must be disjoint, since a shared pair $\{i,j\}$ would have to land in two different third
types; so $m = 5$ admits at most one invariant triple and the second fixed point has to come from
outside the face, as in the example. At $m = 4$ a face-invariant operator has no fixed point outside
the face at all: if $F = \{1,2,3\}$ is invariant then $V_4(x) = \sum P_{ij,4}x_ix_j$ has no nonzero
term, because pairs inside $F$ land in $F$ and pairs containing $4$ cannot land on $4$, so
$V_4 \equiv 0$ and every fixed point has $x_4 = 0$.

What the search says about $m = 4$ is consistent with uniqueness surviving there. Random strictly
non-Volterra operators on four types with all allowed entries positive (400 operators), sparse ones
(150) and face-invariant ones (150) all have exactly one fixed point in the simplex, found by Newton
from 120 to 150 random starts and deduplicated at $10^{-7}$; the same for 150 random five-type
operators without an invariant face, and for 191 of 200 face-invariant five-type operators, the other
nine having two fixed points, which is how the example was found. So $m = 4$ is unresolved and
numerically supported, with no proof attempted. The natural route is an index count, since every fixed
point of index $+1$ would force uniqueness, and the five-type example shows that the count can involve
a saddle of index $-1$, so such an argument would have to use the dimension.

So the answer to Problem 11 as it stands: Theorem 6 does not extend to $m \ge 4$. Non-attractiveness
fails from $m = 4$, uniqueness fails from $m = 5$, uniqueness at $m = 4$ is unresolved and numerically
supported, and the cycle clause is not addressed.

The method lesson recorded in the brain node for this row is worth more than the example: when a
random search over the open simplex finds nothing, look for invariant faces. The boundary is where a
quadratic map on a simplex hides its extra fixed points, and an invariant face inherits the
lower-dimensional theorem for free.

I checked this independently. In exact rational arithmetic the operator's rows all sum to one and it is
strictly non-Volterra with no exceptions; $V(p) = p$ and $V(q) = q$ exactly; and the Jacobian
eigenvalues come out $(-2,-1,-1,2)$ plus the radial $2$ at $p$ and $(-4/3,-1/3,-1/3,0)$ plus the
radial $2$ at $q$. Agrees.

### E0077, the MMPP squared coefficient of variation: proved, then excluded because of He 2021

This row is the campaign's cleanest example of a correct proof that does not count, and the sequence
matters more than the theorem.

Asanjarani and Nazarathy, *Stationary Markovian arrival processes: results and open problems*,
arXiv:1905.01736, Section 4:

> We embarked on this research due to the folklore assumption that for MMPP, $c^2 \ge 1$ (III).
> Initially we believed that it is easy to verify, however to date there isn't a known proof for an
> arbitrary irreducible MMPP. ... Conjecture 4.1. For an irreducible MMPP, $c^2 \ge 1$.

The same authors had posted a proof in 2018, arXiv:1802.08400, and withdrew it in May 2019 with the
comment "There's a problem in the proof of Theorem 3.1". That attempt argued the companion stochastic
order by a sign-pattern argument on $(\text{pi} - \text{alpha})(-C)^{-1}$, entirely inside
matrix-analytic methods.

The proof is one line once the object is written correctly. Let $N$ be a stationary Cox process
directed by a stationary, almost surely diffuse random measure Lambda with intensity lambda* and
$\Lambda(\mathbb{R}) = \infty$ almost surely, and write $S(t) = P(N(0,t] = 0)$ for the time-stationary
void probability. Conditionally on Lambda the process is Poisson, so

$$S(t) = E\big[e^{-\Lambda(0,t]}\big] \;\ge\; e^{-E\Lambda(0,t]} = e^{-\text{lambda*}\,t}$$

by Jensen, using only that $E\Lambda(0,t] = $ lambda*$\,t$, which is stationarity of the directing
measure. The forward recurrence time has $P(T^{\text{pi}} > t) = S(t)$, so integrating gives
$E[T^{\text{pi}}] \ge 1/$lambda* $= E[T^{\text{alpha}}]$. The Palm inversion formula, which is Daley
and Vere-Jones volume II equation (3.4.17) and the source's own Lemma 2.1, reads
$E[T^{\text{pi}}] = $ lambda*$\,E[(T^{\text{alpha}})^2]/2$, so

$$c^2 = \frac{E[(T^{\text{alpha}})^2]}{E[T^{\text{alpha}}]^2} - 1
= 2\,\text{lambda*}\,E[T^{\text{pi}}] - 1 \;\ge\; 1 .$$

Equality forces $S(t) = e^{-\text{lambda*}t}$ for every $t$, and strict convexity of the exponential
then forces $\Lambda(0,t] = $ lambda*$\,t$ almost surely for every $t$, so Lambda is a multiple of
Lebesgue measure and $N$ is Poisson. For an MMPP this says all the rates are equal, which was the
source's other unproved belief. The hypothesis $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}) = \infty$ is not decorative: if the
directing measure vanishes with positive probability $p_0$, the Palm inversion formula acquires a
restriction to $\{T^{\text{pi}} < \infty\}$ and $E[T^{\text{alpha}}] = (1-p_0)/$lambda*, and the chain
of inequalities fails as written. That was pointed out by a Codex agent auditing the note.

Why it was open is the useful part. The reduction to $2\,$lambda*$\,E[T^{\text{pi}}] - 1$ was known to
the authors, as their Lemma 2.1 and equation (13). Every attempt then stayed inside the matrix
identity, where the non-reversible modulating chain makes the deviation matrix non-symmetric and the
quadratic form has no sign a priori. The Cox representation appears in the source only for the
overdispersion of counts, never for the inter-event time. The step nobody took is that the
time-stationary void probability is the expectation of a convex function of the directing measure.

A natural strengthening is false, and recording it keeps the proof honest about how little it needs.
One might hope that $\log S$ is convex, so that $T^{\text{pi}}$ has decreasing hazard, which would give
the inequality with room to spare. It is not: on the three-state cycle with rates
$(0.001, 140.65, 0.001)$ the second difference of $\log S$ reaches $-0.63$ on a grid of step $0.05$.
The proof needs only the chord inequality at each $t$.

In Lean, `E0077.lean` proves the Jensen step $e^{-E\Lambda} \le E e^{-\Lambda}$ on an arbitrary
probability space (`void_prob_ge`, from Mathlib's `ConvexOn.map_integral_le`), the integral comparison
$1/r \le \int_0^\infty f$ whenever $f \ge e^{-rt}$ (`integral_ge_inv`), the algebraic step from Palm
inversion to $c^2 \ge 1$ (`scv_ge_one`), their assembly into the Cox-process statement with the
representation facts as hypotheses (`cox_scv_ge_one`), and for the MMPP form the identity
pi$\,D(D-Q)^{-1} = $ pi from pi$\,Q = 0$ together with the resulting formula for $c^2$ (`vecMul_D_A`,
`mmpp_scv_formula`). Axioms `propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound` only, no `sorry`. The Cox
conditioning identity, stationarity of the directing measure and Palm inversion are hypotheses of the
assembly theorem rather than formalised facts.

Then came the exclusion. A 9 September audit checked the row's status claim rather than its
mathematics, and found Qi-Ming He, *Bounds on the mean and squared coefficient of variation of

### E0078, the companion stochastic order: refuted

Conjecture 4.2 of the same source says that for an irreducible MMPP,
$T_1^{\text{pi}} \ge_{\text{st}} T_1^{\text{alpha}}$, that is
pi$\,e^{Ct}\mathbf 1 \ge $ alpha$\,e^{Ct}\mathbf 1$ for all $t \ge 0$ with
alpha $= $ pi$\,D/($pi$\,D\mathbf 1)$. The authors proved it for Markov-switched Poisson processes and
for two-state MMPPs, tested $10^6$ random instances of orders 3 to 6 plus $10^6$ random order-4 cyclic
generators, and found no violation. The withdrawn 2018 note claimed it as Theorem 3.1.

The conjecture is false, and there are two statements: a theorem for a class of Cox processes and an
exact finite MMPP instance.

Theorem: let lambda be a bounded measurable $T$-periodic function on the line with minimal period $T$,
not almost everywhere constant, let theta be uniform on $[0,T)$, and let $N$ be the Cox process with
intensity lambda(theta $+ s$). Then $N$ is stationary with intensity
lambda* $= T^{-1}\int_0^T$ lambda, and the order fails: on a set of $t$ of positive measure,
$P(T^{\text{pi}} > t) < P(T^{\text{alpha}} > t)$.

The proof needs three facts about $S(t) = P(T^{\text{pi}} > t) = E[e^{-\Lambda(0,t]}]$.

The first is that the order is a monotonicity statement. Palm inversion gives
$P(T^{\text{pi}} > t) = $ lambda*$\int_t^\infty P(T^{\text{alpha}} > s)\,ds$, and $S$ is Lipschitz
here because lambda is bounded, so for almost every $t$ we have
$-S'(t) = $ lambda*$\,P(T^{\text{alpha}} > t)$. Hence the conjecture's inequality at such a $t$ is
exactly $S'(t) + $ lambda*$\,S(t) \ge 0$, that is $g'(t) \ge 0$ for
$g(t) = e^{\text{lambda*}t}S(t)$. So the conjecture says precisely that $g$ is nondecreasing, or
equivalently that the hazard rate of $T^{\text{pi}}$ never exceeds lambda*. Note $g(0) = 1$ and
$g'(0) = 0$: the hazard starts at lambda*.

The second is that $g(T) = 1$. Over a full period the exposure is deterministic,
$\Lambda(0,T] = \int_0^T$ lambda $= $ lambda*$\,T$ for every theta, so $S(T) = e^{-\text{lambda*}T}$
exactly.

The third is that $g(t) > 1$ for $0 < t < T$. Jensen gives $S(t) \ge e^{-\text{lambda*}t}$, which is
the E0077 inequality, with equality only if $\Lambda(0,t]$ is almost surely constant in theta. Its
theta-derivative is lambda(theta $+t) - $ lambda(theta), which vanishes for almost every theta only if
lambda is $t$-periodic, impossible for $0 < t < T$ when $T$ is the minimal period. So the inequality is
strict.

A Lipschitz $g$ with $g(0) = g(T) = 1$ and $g > 1$ on $(0,T)$ has $g' < 0$ on a set of positive measure
in $(0,T)$, and at those $t$ the order fails.

The mechanism in words: from a random instant, the survivors to time $t$ are the phases that have not
yet met the high-intensity part of the cycle; as $t$ approaches one period the cycle brings that part
around to every survivor at once, and the hazard of the time-stationary interval overshoots the average
rate. The event-stationary interval, which starts inside the high-intensity part, has already paid that
cost.

For the MMPP instance, take the 24-state cycle with $Q_{i,i+1} = 1$ modulo 24, $Q_{ii} = -1$, and rates
$\lambda_i = 2/3$ for $i = 0,\dots,11$ and $0$ otherwise. Then lambda* $= 1/3$, the mean lap is $24$
and the mean exposure per lap is $8$. At $t = 19.75$, with a 30-digit matrix exponential,

$$P(T^{\text{pi}} > t) = 0.01425361547, \qquad P(T^{\text{alpha}} > t) = 0.01542552227,$$

so the order is violated by 8.2 per cent of $P(T^{\text{pi}} > t)$. An independent exact certificate by
a Codex agent, rational uniformisation of the matrix exponential with 121 terms and a geometric
remainder, brackets the difference at $t = 79/4$ between $-0.0011719068073579858342212642800$ and
$-0.0011719068073579858342212642799$, so the violation is a proved inequality rather than a
floating-point one. Larger cycles violate it harder: 64 states with a block of 32 at rate $1/4$ gives,
at $t = 53$, $0.005276$ against $0.007865$, a 49 per cent excess of the hazard of $T^{\text{pi}}$ over
lambda*.

The MMPP instance is the same object as the theorem, and the limit is what connects them. A cycle of
$m$ states at rate $r$ with a block of $k = m/2$ consecutive states at rate $L$ is a Markov
approximation of the periodic intensity: the lap time is Gamma$(m,r)$, with relative spread
$m^{-1/2}$, and the exposure per lap is Gamma$(k,r)$ times $L$. Holding $m/r = T$ and $kL/r = E$ fixed
as $m$ grows, the exposure over any window converges in probability to the deterministic periodic
exposure, so $S_m$ and $-S_m'$ converge to the periodic process's by bounded convergence, and the
strict inequality holds for all large $m$. In the family with block $m/2$, exposure $8$ per lap and
$r = 1$, the order first fails at $m = 20$; the 24-state instance was chosen for cleaner numbers, not
as the minimum.

Why the source's search missed it is worth recording, because it is a statement about where in
parameter space the mechanism lives. Their random instances had uniform$(0,1)$ off-diagonal
generators, nothing close to a long deterministic cycle, and their cyclic experiment was order 4, where
the lap has relative spread $1/2$ and the mechanism has no room. It needs a cycle long enough that the
exposure per lap is nearly deterministic while the per-visit exposure $L/r$ stays below one: the Jensen
slack at a full lap, roughly $\exp(E^2/2k)$, must fall below the slack at a half lap, roughly
$\cosh(E/2)$. The single-hot-state cycle does not work at any length, because its slack is dominated by
the exponential holding time in the hot state, and the order holds there at every $m$ from 4 to 64.

Robustness and independent checks. Three computations of the 64-state instance agree to six digits: the
`scipy` matrix exponential, DOP853 integration of $u' = Cu$ at tolerance $10^{-11}$, and mpmath at 30
digits. Independently of the phase-type formula altogether, a simulation of the point process itself --
12 million holding intervals, 1.5 million arrivals, 2 million uniformly sampled instants -- gives
empirical intensity $0.12473$ against $1/8$, $E[T^{\text{alpha}}] = 8.017$ against $8$, $c^2 = 2.56$ so
that E0077 holds on the same path, and at $t = 53$ the two survival probabilities
$0.00794 \pm 0.00007$ and $0.00538 \pm 0.00005$, matching the matrix values, with the violation at 29
standard errors there and reversing to $+437$ at $t = 20$ as it must, since the order holds for small
$t$. With strictly positive rates and a backward jump rate added to every state, the 24-state instance
still violates the order, $0.011808$ against $0.012645$ at $t = 20$, so the violation is an open
condition in the parameters.

What survives: Conjecture 4.1 is unaffected, and the source's Lemma 2.1 and its Proposition 3.3, the
order for Markov-switched Poisson processes, are untouched. What falls is the picture in which the
order is the natural route to $c^2 \ge 1$. The means are ordered for every MMPP; the distributions are
not.

I checked this independently. Recomputing the 24-state instance with a 40-digit matrix exponential
gives $P(T^{\text{pi}} > 19.75) = 0.014253615466$ and
$P(T^{\text{alpha}} > 19.75) = 0.015425522274$, matching the memo to every digit it prints, with an
excess of $8.2218$ per cent; the 64-state instance at $t = 53$ gives $0.0052805$ against $0.0078708$,
an excess of $49.05$ per cent. For the theorem, the periodic-Cox $g$ computed by quadrature is $2.788$,
$6.822$, $2.788$ at $t = 6, 12, 18$ and exactly $1.000000000$ at $t = T = 24$, which is the second and
third facts directly. Agrees.

### E0046, Bandeira's Open Problem 1.2: proved independently, then found settled

Bandeira, *Ten lectures and forty-two open problems in the mathematics of data science* (2015), Open
Problem 1.2, attributed there to Bandeira, Kennedy and Singer, conjectures that the expected average
singular value of an $n \times n$ Gaussian matrix divided by $\sqrt n$ increases in $n$ in the real
case and decreases in $n$ in the complex case. The complex half was proved here and the row is now
recorded as explicitly known.

The route is the part that might still be of use. For $X$ with independent standard complex Gaussian
entries, $W = XX^*$ has Laguerre unitary eigenvalue density, and
$\text{alpha}_{\mathbb C}(n) = n^{-3/2}E\operatorname{tr} W^{1/2} = n^{-3/2}\sum_{k<n} a_k$ with
$a_k = \int_0^\infty x^{1/2}e^{-x}L_k(x)^2\,dx$. From the Laguerre generating function and a Mellin
transform, $a_k = \Gamma(3/2)\,r_k$ with
$r_k = \sum_{m \le k} (3/2)_m/m! \cdot \binom{1/2}{k-m}^2$, so $r_0 = 1$, $r_1 = 7/4$, $r_2 = 145/64$,
$r_3 = 687/256$, $r_4 = 49881/16384$. The generating function of $r_k$ is
$(1-t)^{-3/2}{}_2F_1(-1/2,-1/2;1;t)$, and pushing the hypergeometric equation through gives a two-term
recurrence not for alpha itself but for the partial sums $S_n = \sum_{k<n} r_k$:

$$n^2 S_{n+1} = \Big(2n^2 + \tfrac34\Big)S_n - n^2 S_{n-1}, \qquad S_1 = 1,\ S_2 = \tfrac{11}{4}.$$

That is the step that makes the problem elementary. Putting $\rho_n = S_{n+1}/S_n$ turns it into

$$\rho_n = 2 + \frac{3}{4n^2} - \frac{1}{\rho_{n-1}}, \qquad \rho_1 = \tfrac{11}{4},$$

and the claim alpha$_{\mathbb C}(n+1) < $ alpha$_{\mathbb C}(n)$ is exactly
$\rho_n < f(n) := (1+1/n)^{3/2}$. One elementary inequality closes it: for $0 \le x \le 1$,

$$(1+x)^{3/2} + (1-x)^{3/2} \ge 2 + \tfrac34 x^2, \tag{$*$}$$

with equality only at $x = 0$. To see it, put $u = \sqrt{1+x}$, $v = \sqrt{1-x}$, $s = u+v$; then
$u^2+v^2 = 2$, $uv = (s^2-2)/2$, $x^2 = 1 - u^2v^2$, and $u^3+v^3 = 3s - s^3/2$, so $(*)$ becomes
$(s-2)^2(3s^2+4s-8)/16 \ge 0$, and since $s^2 = 2 + 2uv \ge 2$ the second factor is at least
$6 + 4\sqrt2 - 8 > 0$. The induction is then one line: $\rho_1 = 11/4 < 2^{3/2} = f(1)$, and since
$\rho \mapsto 2 + 3/(4n^2) - 1/\rho$ is increasing,
$\rho_n < 2 + 3/(4n^2) - (1-1/n)^{3/2} < (1+1/n)^{3/2}$ by $(*)$ at $x = 1/n$.

The inequality is tight to second order -- both sides are $3/(2n) + 3/(8n^2) + O(n^{-3})$ and the
margin $f(n) - \rho_n$ is about $0.18/n^3$ -- which is why no bound on the finite-$n$ quarter-circle
correction could have proved it. The proof also gives the same conclusion for any weight whose partial
sums satisfy $n^2S_{n+1} = (2n^2+c)S_n - n^2S_{n-1}$ with $c \le 3/4$ and $\rho_1 \le 2^{3/2}$.

In Lean, `E0046.lean` certifies $(*)$ in the algebraic form with $u, v$ (`key_ineq`), the auxiliary
bound `inv_f_pred`, and the induction from the ratio recursion to $\rho_n \le f(n)$ (`rho_le_f`). The
recurrences themselves enter as hypotheses, being the symbolic computation above.

Prior art was checked after the proof, the same evening, and both halves had been settled in 2026.
Ondrej Hutnik, arXiv:2608.12151 (12 August 2026), proves the real increase quantitatively and cites for
the complex half his companion preprint, built on L. D. Abreu's recurrence in *Complex Analysis and
Operator Theory* 20 (2026) 159; Abreu's 2016 preprint claiming the complex decrease was withdrawn in
2023 because its Lemma 1 was wrong. The intake's own status search had missed all of it, and the phrase
that finds all three papers on the first page of results is "average singular value" with "Gaussian
random matrix", where the intake had searched for "monotonicity of singular values" and "moments of the
Marchenko-Pastur law". The lesson the campaign wrote down from this row, search before proving rather
than after, is the one the 9 September MMPP correction then had to write down again.

I checked this independently. The closed form gives $r_0,\dots,r_4$ as $1$, $7/4$, $145/64$,
$687/256$, $49881/16384$ exactly; both recurrences hold exactly on the rationals for $k, n \le 23$;
$\rho_n < (1+1/n)^{3/2}$ for every $n \le 24$ with margins $1.8\times10^{-4}$ at $n = 10$ and
$1.6\times10^{-5}$ at $n = 24$, consistent with the $0.18/n^3$ estimate; the factorisation of $(*)$ is
an exact identity with second factor $3.657$ at $s^2 = 2$; and alpha$_{\mathbb C}(n)$ decreases over
$n \le 24$ from $\sqrt\pi/2 = 0.886227$ toward $8/(3\pi) = 0.848826$. Agrees.

### E0060, Rozikov's Problem 3, and what "explicitly known" means in these ledgers

E0060 asks for a transversal Volterra operator with a periodic itinerary, and the answer is yes by
Zakharevich's three-state cyclic operator, whose itinerary is $1, 3, 2, 1, \dots$ with geometrically
growing sojourns. The row is labelled explicitly known because the example is in the literature; what
the campaign adds is the identification and a note on where the live question is, namely dimension
four, where a cyclic-type operator mixes three routes -- the sequence $1,4,3,2,1,4,2,1$ was observed --
consistent with Ganikhodjaev and Zanin 2012.

E0046 above is the other explicitly known row. Both are recorded that way in the ledger rather than
counted as results, and this report follows the ledger. The distinction the campaign draws here is the
right one and is worth stating once: a correct derivation of a known theorem is useful as a check on
the method and useless as a claim, and the only thing that separates the two cases is whether the
literature check happened before or after.

### E0104 and E0105, the mobile Boolean model: settled in restricted cases

Konstantopoulos, *The mobile Boolean model: an overview and further results*, arXiv:1407.6834,
Section 7, with the problem spelled out at the end of Section 5. Particles start at the points of a
homogeneous Poisson process of intensity lambda in $\mathbb{R}^d$, each carries a closed ball of radius
$r$, and each moves in a straight line $x + tv$ with independent identically distributed velocities.
$W(t)$ is the region covered up to time $t$, and the source obtains the detection time of a ball
$r_0B$ in its equation (15). Then:

> A more elaborate problem is the computation of the law of
> $\inf\{t \ge 0 : r_0B \cap W(t) \ne \emptyset,\ a \notin W(t)\}$ ... the first time that the
> particles will detect a fixed ball of radius $r_0$ before anyone of them goes close to some point
> $a$ (the enemy). This problem will be addressed in the future.

Open problem 3 restates that; open problem 2 asks for $P(S_{K_1} < S_{K_2})$ for two sets.

The reduction is immediate and is what makes the problem finite. Since $W(t)$ grows with $t$, the set
$\{t : K \cap W(t) \ne \emptyset,\ a \notin W(t)\}$ is $\{t : S_K \le t < S_a\}$, where $S_K$ and $S_a$
are the detection times of the ball $K = r_0B$ and of the point $a$. So tau $= S_K$ on
$\{S_K < S_a\}$ and $+\infty$ otherwise, and the whole problem is the joint law of the pair
$(S_K, S_a)$, which is the ball-and-point case of open problem 2.

For the joint law, note that a particle starting at $x$ with velocity $v$ detects $K$ by time $t$ if
and only if its centre comes within $R = r_0 + r$ of the origin at some $s \le t$, that is if and only
if $x$ lies in the capsule $C_R(t;v) = RB + [0,t](-v)$, a ball of radius $R$ swept along a segment; and
it covers $a$ by time $s$ if and only if $x$ lies in $C^a_r(s;v) = a + rB + [0,s](-v)$. The particles
form a marked Poisson process, so

$$P(S_K > t,\ S_a > s) = \exp\big(-\text{lambda}\,E_v \operatorname{Vol}
\big(C_R(t;v) \cup C^a_r(s;v)\big)\big),$$

and the two capsules have parallel axes. With $c = |v|$, $a_{\parallel} = a\cdot e$ for $e = -v/|v|$,
and $h$ the perpendicular offset of the enemy from the ball's track, the union volume is the sum of the
two capsule volumes minus an overlap integral along the axial coordinate, whose integrand is the
$(d-1)$-volume of two $(d-1)$-balls with centres $h$ apart: an interval overlap for $d = 2$, the
circular lens area for $d = 3$, and a standard one-dimensional integral in higher dimension. Both
displays are exact. Differentiating along the diagonal gives the law of tau: an atom at zero, a density
on $(0,\infty)$, and an atom at $+\infty$ equal to the probability that the enemy is reached first.
Ties $S_K = S_a > 0$ have probability zero, because the coincidence is a codimension-one condition on
$(x,v)$. For two balls the same formula holds with the two inflated radii and the perpendicular offset
of the centres, which is open problem 2 for balls.

The check against the particle system was run in $d = 2$ with lambda $= 0.3$, $r = 0.5$, $r_0 = 1$ so
$R = 1.5$, $a = (3,0)$, unit speed and isotropic direction: the formula by quadrature against a direct
simulation of the marked Poisson particle system over 60,000 independent configurations. The joint
survival function agrees to the third decimal at $t = 1, 3, 6$; the atom at zero is $0.6953$ from the
formula against $0.6967 \pm 0.0019$ measured; and $P(\text{tau} < \infty) = P(S_K < S_a)$ is $0.7670$
against $0.7671 \pm 0.0017$. The continuous part sits about $0.0014$ above the simulation at every
horizon, which is 1.5 standard errors and the size of the quadrature error in the numerical derivative;
a 20,000-run pilot had shown a $0.005$ gap that the larger run resolved as sampling noise.

What is and is not done is why the label says restricted. Done: the exact joint law for the homogeneous
inertial model with any velocity law of finite mean speed, hence the law of tau, explicit up to a
one-dimensional integral and the average over the velocity law. This is the homogeneous case, which is
the one the source solves for a single ball. For a non-homogeneous isotropic intensity the same
union-of-capsules formula holds with lambda$(dx)$ in place of lambda$\,dx$ and the integrals become the
source's "laborious" ones. Open problem 1 on coverage and open problem 5 on the stability of the
space-time service system are untouched. Prior art is unchecked beyond the source; the capsule volume
is classical and the union of two capsules is the only new ingredient.

I checked this independently. The atom at zero is the one closed-form number in the section and it
factors exactly, because the two disks are disjoint ($R + r = 2 < 3$): it is
$\big(1 - e^{-\text{lambda}\pi R^2}\big)e^{-\text{lambda}\pi r^2}
= 0.880037 \times 0.790081 = 0.695301$, which is also what the memo's expression
$e^{-\text{lambda}\omega r^d} - e^{-\Lambda(0,0)}$ evaluates to. That matches the formula's $0.6953$
and sits within one standard error of the measured $0.6967 \pm 0.0019$. Agrees.

### F0055, Janson's Conjecture 18.4: the largest box in a sparse allocation

Janson, *Simply generated trees, conditioned Galton-Watson trees, random allocations and
condensation*, Probability Surveys 9 (2012), Section 18.1. A weight sequence $w = (w_k)$ with
$w_0 > 0$ and $w_k > 0$ for some $k \ge 1$ defines the random allocation $B_{m,n}$ of $m$ balls in $n$
boxes, with $P(Y = y)$ proportional to $\prod_i w_{y_i}$ over compositions of $m$ into $n$ parts;
$\Phi(t) = \sum_k w_k t^k$ has radius of convergence rho, and $Y_{(1)}$ is the largest box. Their
Theorem 18.3 gives, for $m/n \to$ lambda in $(0,\nu)$, that
$Y_{(1)} \le \log n/\log(\text{rho}/\text{tau}) + o_p(\log n)$ where $\Psi(\text{tau}) = $ lambda.
Then:

> We conjecture that Theorem 18.3 holds also for lambda $= 0$. Since then tau $= 0$, this means the
> following. (This seems almost obvious given the result for positive lambda in Theorem 18.3, where
> the constant $1/\log(\text{rho}/\text{tau}) \to 0$ as lambda $\to 0$ and thus tau $\to 0$, but there
> is no general monotonicity and we leave this as an open problem.)
> Conjecture 18.4. If rho $> 0$ and $m/n \to 0$, then $Y_{(1)} = o_p(\log n)$.

Theorem: let $w_0 > 0$, $w_1 > 0$ and rho $> 0$, let $m = m(n)$ with $m/n \to 0$, fix any
$0 < r < $ rho and put gamma $= \min(1, w_1/w_0)$. Then for every delta $> 0$, with high probability

$$Y_{(1)} \le (1+\text{delta})\,\frac{\log n}{\log\big(\text{gamma}\,r\,n/(2m)\big)},$$

so $Y_{(1)} = O_p(\log n/\log(n/m)) = o_p(\log n)$, which is Conjecture 18.4.

The proof is a counting comparison that never tilts, which is the point. Write $Z_{k,N}$ for the
partition function $[t^k]\Phi(t)^N$. By exchangeability of the boxes,
$P(Y_1 = j) = w_j Z_{m-j,n-1}/Z_{m,n}$ and $P(Y_{(1)} \ge k) \le n\sum_{j \ge k}P(Y_1 = j)$. Fix
$1 \le j \le m$ and suppose $m \le n/4$. Relate configurations $y$ of $m-j$ balls in $n-1$ boxes to
configurations $z$ of $m$ balls in $n$ boxes as follows: choose a set $J$ of $j$ indices $i \le n-1$
with $y_i = 0$, set $z_i = 1$ for $i \in J$ and $z_i = y_i$ otherwise, and append an empty box. The
weight changes by the factor $w_0 (w_1/w_0)^j$. Each $y$ has at least $\binom{E(y)}{j}$ images, where
$E(y) \ge n-1-(m-j)$ is its number of empty boxes, and each $z$ has at most $\binom{S(z)}{j}$
preimages, where $S(z) \le m$ is its number of singleton boxes, since the preimage is determined by
which singletons are removed. Summing the weight over the relation in both orders and using
$\binom{m}{j}/\binom{N}{j} \le (m/N)^j$ with $N = n-1-m \ge n/2$,

$$\frac{Z_{m-j,n-1}}{Z_{m,n}} \le \frac{1}{w_0}\Big(\frac{w_0}{w_1}\Big)^j\Big(\frac{2m}{n}\Big)^j,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
P(Y_1 = j) \le \frac{w_j}{w_0}\Big(\frac{2m}{\text{gamma}\,n}\Big)^j. \tag{2}$$

Since $\Phi(r) < \infty$ we have $w_j \le \Phi(r)r^{-j}$, so with $q_n = 2m/(\text{gamma}\,r\,n) \to 0$
the union bound gives $P(Y_{(1)} \ge k) \le n\,(\Phi(r)/w_0)\,q_n^k/(1-q_n)$. Taking
$k_n = \lceil (1+\text{delta})\log n/\log(1/q_n)\rceil$ makes $n q_n^{k_n} \le n^{-\text{delta}}$,
and $k_n = o(\log n)$ because $\log(1/q_n) \to \infty$.

No monotonicity was needed anywhere, which is what the source's parenthesis asks for. Theorem 18.3
estimates $P(Y_1 = j)$ through the independent representation with the tilt tau(lambda) and a local
limit theorem, and that machinery degenerates as lambda $\to 0$; the comparison (2) instead moves the
$j$ balls of one box into $j$ of the many empty boxes, and its only input is that empty boxes
outnumber singletons by a factor $n/(2m)$. The two constants agree in order, since tau(lambda) is of
order lambda for small lambda because $\Psi(t) \sim (w_1/w_0)t$ at $0$.

The case $w_1 = 0$ is handled by the same argument with a weaker constant. Let
$D = \{k \ge 1 : w_k > 0\}$. If $\gcd(D) = d > 1$ then only $m$ divisible by $d$ occur, and dividing
all box counts by $d$ reduces to the weight sequence $(w_{dk})$, of radius rho$^d > 0$. So assume
$\gcd(D) = 1$: there are a finite $F \subseteq D$, $K = \max F$, and $j_0$ such that every $j \ge j_0$
is a sum of elements of $F$ with $p(j) \ge j/K$ parts. Replace "$j$ singletons" in the comparison by
"$p(j)$ boxes carrying the parts of $j$", assigned to the chosen empty boxes in a canonical order:
the weight factor is at least $w_0\,\text{gamma}_F^{p(j)}$, the image and preimage counts are
$\binom{n-1-m}{p(j)}$ and $\binom{m}{p(j)}$, and (2) becomes
$P(Y_1 = j) \le (w_j/w_0)(q')^j$ with $q' = (2m/(\text{gamma}_F n))^{1/K}$. The tail argument is
unchanged.

The bound is the right order. For Poisson weights $w_k = 1/k!$ the allocation is uniform multinomial,

### F0090, Kalai's Conjecture 8: the spectral entropy of the percolation crossing event

Kalai, *Three puzzles on mathematics, computation, and games*, Proceedings of the ICM 2018,
arXiv:1801.02602, Section 3.3.2, Conjecture 8: "Let $f$ represent the crossing event in planar
percolation. Show that $H(S(f)) = O(I(f))$ and $H(P(f)) = O(I(f))$." The same question is the first
open problem in the list closing Garban, Pete and Schramm, *The Fourier spectrum of critical
percolation*, Acta Mathematica 205 (2010), Section 11: "if $f_R$ is the $\pm1$ indicator function of
the left-right crossing in the square $[0,R]^2$, is it true that
$\mathrm{Ent}(f_R) = O(R^2\alpha_4(R))$?"

Theorem: for critical bond percolation on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ or critical site percolation on the
triangular lattice, there is a constant $C$ depending only on the lattice such that for every
$R \ge 2$, $H(S_{f_R}) \le C\,I(f_R)$ and $H(P_{f_R}) \le C\,I(f_R)$, where $S$ is the spectral sample
and $P$ the pivotal set. In particular $\mathrm{Ent}(f_R) = O(R^2\alpha_4(R))$.

The argument has one new step and three quoted inputs. The new step is a coding bound. Cover the square
by the dyadic squares of side $2^k$, $k = 0,\dots,K$, nested in the usual quadtree, and for a random
subset $A$ of the bits write $A_r$ for the set of $r$-squares that meet $A$. Describe $A$ from the
coarsest scale down: one bit saying whether $A$ is empty, then for each hit square of side $2^k$ four
bits saying which of its four children of side $2^{k-1}$ are hit, and at the finest scale a bounded
number of bits per hit unit square. The decoder always knows how many bits to read next, so the code is
uniquely decodable, and Shannon's source coding theorem gives

$$H(A) \;\le\; 1 + 4\sum_{k=1}^{K} E|A_{2^k}| + C_0\,E|A_1|. \tag{1}$$

Since $E|A| = I(f_R)$ for both the spectral sample and the pivotal set, the finest term is a multiple of
the influence, and everything rests on the coarser scales. That is what makes (1) a hierarchical version
of the entropy-influence inequality: the conjecture holds for any Boolean function whose spectral
sample hits the coarser dyadic squares no more often in total than it hits the finest ones, which for
critical percolation is the statement that the spectral sample is a fractal of dimension strictly less
than two.

The three quoted inputs are Garban, Pete and Schramm's Lemma 3.2, that
$P[S \cap B \ne \emptyset] \le 4\,\alpha_{\mathrm{box}}(B,Q_R)$ for a union of tiles $B$ meeting the
square in a nonempty connected set, with the same bound and no constant for the pivotal set, which
follows from the identity $P[S\cap B \ne \emptyset] = E[\mathrm{Var}(f_R \mid \text{bits outside } B)]$;
their (2.6), quasi-multiplicativity together with the polynomial lower bound
$\alpha_4(r,R) \ge C^{-1}(r/R)^{2-\epsilon}$; and the half-plane three-arm bound
$\alpha_3^+(r,R) \le C(r/R)^2$.

The one-scale sum is then geometry. Classify a square $B$ of side $r$ by its distance $d$ to the
boundary of $Q_R$ and by the distance $D$ to the second nearest side. For $d \ge 2r$ and $D \ge 8d$ the
four-arm event from $B$ to the four sides forces four alternating arms from $B$ to radius $d/2$, an
event of a disc inside the square, and three arms of alternating colours from radius $2d$ to $D/2$
inside the half-plane bounded by the nearest side; the two events live on disjoint bits, so

$$\alpha_{\mathrm{box}}(B,Q_R) \le \alpha_4(r,d/2)\,\alpha_3^+(2d,D/2) \le C\,\alpha_4(r,d)\,(d/D)^2 .$$

The number of $r$-squares with $d$ and $D$ in given dyadic ranges is at most $C(d/r)(D/r)$, so summing
over the dyadic classes and using quasi-multiplicativity twice gives
$E|A_r| \le C(R/r)^2\alpha_4(r,R) + C$. Inserting that in (1) and summing over scales,
$(R/r)^2\alpha_4(r,R) \le C R^2\alpha_4(1,R)r^{-\epsilon}$ makes the scale sum a geometric series, and
$I(f_R) \ge cR^2\alpha_4(1,R)$ absorbs the additive logarithm. On the triangular lattice the scale-$r$
term is $(R/r)^{3/4+o(1)}$, so the bound is dominated by its finest term, as a random fractal of
dimension $3/4$ with $R^{3/4+o(1)}$ points should be.

The exponent condition is where the result comes from and it is worth isolating: the scale sum converges
if and only if $r^2\alpha_4(1,r)$ grows polynomially, which is the quoted (2.6) on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and
the $5/4$ exponent on the triangular lattice. With the five-arm bound alone, whose exponent is exactly
two, the same argument would give only the trivial $O(I \log R)$.

Two independent checks are on record. On the $2\times2$ and $3\times3$ squares the exact spectrum by a
Walsh-Hadamard transform over all configurations gives $H(S) = 2.000$ bits with $I(f) = 1.000$ and
$H(S) = 5.331$ bits with $I(f) = 1.719$, and the coding inequality holds in both cases, as Shannon's
theorem says it must; this is a control on the definitions and not evidence for the asymptotic
statement. A Monte Carlo run on the pivotal set at $R = 16$ to $256$, with the pivotal rule
brute-force-verified against flipping on all 49,152 edge cases of the $3\times3$ square, gives
$E|P_r|/E|P|$ stable in $R$ at each scale -- $0.575$, $0.356$, $0.214$, $0.127$, $0.075$ at
$r = 2,4,8,16,32$ -- decaying like $r^{-0.74}$, with the scale-sum ratio climbing from $1.25$ to $1.44$
toward the predicted $\sum_k 2^{-3k/4} = 1.46$. That is the geometric decay the scale sum needs, seen
directly on $\mathbb{Z}^2$.

Status words, copied: author-checked argument with a first independent read, novelty not certified. The
independent reader found the inputs and the square scale-sum mechanism sound and no defect in the square
theorem, recommended keeping the theorem to squares, and corrected two commentary points that are now
in the memo: the majority function is not a counterexample to $H(P) = O(I)$ under the pivotal-set law,
since for $n = 2m-1$ the pivotal set is empty unless the sum is $\pm1$ and the ratio tends to
$2\log 2$, while $\mathrm{OR}_n$ is a counterexample with ratio $(n/2)\log 2$; and the triangular-lattice
exponent carries an $o(1)$ that cannot be dropped, which is why the theorem is stated in terms of

entropy, all of them general entropy-influence, Brascamp-Lieb, sparse-reconstruction or group-walk

### P0291, Leskela's Problem 3: a non-monotone chain with a supermodular time ordering

Leskela, *Ross's second conjecture and supermodular stochastic ordering*, Queueing Systems 100 (2022)
213-215, Problem 3 in the journal numbering: "Does there exist a stationary and ergodic CTMC
lambda $= ($lambda$(t))$ which is not stochastically monotone but satisfies (2)?", where (2) is the
property that $c_1 \le c_2$ implies lambda$_{c_1} \ge_{sm}$ lambda$_{c_2}$ for
lambda$_c(t) := $ lambda$(ct)$, and $X \le_{sm} Y$ for processes means
$E\varphi(X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n}) \le E\varphi(Y_{t_1},\dots,Y_{t_n})$ for all supermodular varphi.
Bauerle and Rolski proved (2) for every doubly stochastically monotone finite-state chain, and Leskela
notes that outside the monotone class "not much seems to be known ... even for state spaces of size 3".

The answer is yes, on three states. Take the star: on the intensity values $1 < 2 < 3$, from the middle
value $1$ jump to $2$ or to $3$ at rate $a$ each, and from $2$ and from $3$ return to $1$ at rate $b$,
for any $a, b > 0$. It is stationary, ergodic and reversible with pi $= (b,a,a)/(b+2a)$, and it is not
stochastically monotone, since $P_t(1,\{3\}) = at + O(t^2)$ while $P_t(2,\{3\}) = O(t^2)$. It satisfies
(2). Since (2) implies Ross's monotonicity by Bauerle and Rolski, the star is also a non-monotone
environment for which faster modulation never increases the mean stationary workload, so stochastic
monotonicity is not the characterisation that Leskela's Problem 4 asks for.

The proof has four moves. By stationarity the law of $(X(ct_1),\dots,X(ct_n))$ depends only on the gaps
$cg_k$, so it is enough to show that $F(g_1,\dots,g_{n-1}) = E\varphi(X(0),X(g_1),\dots)$ is
nonincreasing in each gap separately. Conditioning on the two coordinates that straddle the gap $g$
makes the prefix and suffix independent, so $F = \sum_{x,y}\text{pi}(x)P_g(x,y)\Phi(x,y)$ with $\Phi$
free of $g$. Since $Q$ is reversible with right eigenvectors $(1,1,1)$, $(0,1,-1)$, $(-2a/b,1,1)$ and
eigenvalues $0$, $-b$, $-(2a+b)$, the gap dependence is
$F(g) = C + \alpha e^{-bg} + \beta e^{-(2a+b)g}$, and $F' \le 0$ for every $g$ is equivalent, the
expression being affine in $u = e^{-2ag} \in (0,1)$, to two finite conditions free of $a$ and $b$:

$$\text{(C1)}\quad \Phi_{22} + \Phi_{33} \ge \Phi_{23} + \Phi_{32}, \qquad
\text{(C2)}\quad 2\Phi_{11} + \Phi_{22} + \Phi_{33} \ge \Phi_{12} + \Phi_{13} + \Phi_{21} +
\Phi_{31}.$$

The third move is the description that makes those two conditions provable: the star's non-monotonicity
is entirely a matter of which leaf. Let $Y_t = \mathbf 1\{X_t \in \{2,3\}\}$ be the skeleton, a
monotone two-state chain with rates $2a$ up and $b$ down. Given the whole skeleton path, the values of
$X$ on the successive leaf excursions are independent fair coins on $\{2,3\}$, because from the centre
the chain picks a leaf independently of the holding times and of the past, and it cannot pass from $2$
to $3$ without visiting $1$. (C1) then follows by coupling $A^2$ and $A^3$ on one skeleton with the
same coins off the current block: they differ exactly on the current block, and supermodularity of
varphi passes to the two-variable function obtained by setting that block constant, because the join
and meet of two block-constant vectors are block-constant.

(C2) needs the order-preserving coupling of the skeleton started at the centre with the skeleton
started at a leaf, coalescing at the first of the two competing jumps, and it reduces to a single
finite inequality $S(\psi) \ge 0$ for supermodular $\psi$ on $\{1,2,3\}^4$, where the four blocks are
the current prefix block, the overlap created if the centre-started skeleton jumps first, and their
suffix analogues. For $l = 3$ every term of $S$ is nonnegative by supermodularity; for $l = 2$ the
terms with a coin equal to $3$ are not individually signed, and the inequality holds only as a whole.
It is an exact nonnegative combination of eleven elementary increasing-difference inequalities, with
weights $2$ and $4$, verified in rational arithmetic. That the $l = 2$ terms are not individually
signed is why a direct coupling argument does not finish and a linear program was needed to find the
certificate.

What the proof does not use is as informative as what it does. The Bauerle and Rolski route, in which
monotone kernels propagate supermodularity through the chain, is unavailable: for the star,
conditioning on two interior coordinates does not preserve supermodularity, and a linear program finds
a supermodular varphi on $\{1,2,3\}^3$ whose conditional $\Phi$ has increasing differences as negative
as $-0.64$. Exchangeability of the leaves is load-bearing: with unequal return rates $b_2 < b_3$ the
linear-program test of (2) fails, worst violation $3.2\times10^{-2}$ at $n = 3$, while $b_2 > b_3$ and
unequal arrival rates pass.

The same day's addendum pushes on Leskela's Problem 4, the characterisation. With equal return rates
and any arrival rates the star still satisfies (2), the coin becoming biased with $p = a_2/(a_2+a_3)$
and the certificate splitting into four exact pieces $T_3, T_2, T_1, T_0$ of the cubic
$S_p = p^3T_3 + p^2qT_2 + pq^2T_1 + q^3T_0$; and if $b_3 > b_2$ the star violates (2) already at
$n = 2$, because Cauchy interlacing puts the slow eigenvalue strictly between the two return rates, the
slow-mode coefficient of $P_s(1,3)$ is then strictly positive, and the lower-orthant indicator
$\mathbf 1\{x \le 1, y \le 2\}$ is supermodular. So on stars, (2) holds if $b_2 \ge b_3$ and fails if
$b_2 < b_3$, with the $b_2 > b_3$ half of the conjectured characterisation unproved because the leaf
period then depends on the label and the skeleton is no longer autonomous. For a general reversible
three-state chain the two-point condition is exactly
$\ell_1(1)\ell_1(3) \le 0$ for the slow left eigenvector, and forty random reversible generators are
consistent with that being the characterisation of (2): ten monotone, thirteen non-monotone that
satisfy (2), which are thirteen more examples for Problem 3, and seventeen that fail.

Status words: author-checked proof with an exact finite certificate for its one combinatorial step; not
independently refereed; novelty not certified, no citation search beyond the source and the two papers
it cites. What is still owed is a referee of the spectral reduction and of the coupling's marginal
correctness, and a citation search on Leskela 2022 and Bauerle-Rolski 1998 for a prior three-state
example.

I checked the certificate independently. Over 4,000 random functions on $\{1,2,3\}^4$ the difference
between $S(\psi)$ and the eleven-term combination is at most $1.1\times10^{-14}$, so the identity is
exact; and over 2,000 random supermodular $\psi$ built from separable plus pairwise-product terms the
minimum of $S$ is $1.418$, strictly positive, with the linear-program minimum reported in the memo
being $0$ at the degenerate corner. Agrees.

### G0013 and P0289, which are settled elsewhere in this report

G0013, the Catalano and Lavenant conjecture on couplings farthest from independence, belongs to
chapter 10 and is not re-derived here. The structural reduction is that $W_2^2(\cdot,\text{product})$ is
convex, so the maximiser over couplings is an extreme point of the Birkhoff polytope, which after
discretisation to $n$ atoms is a permutation. An exhaustive search over all permutations found the
identity and the reversal as the unique maximisers for every $n$ from $3$ to $7$, and another lane
extended that search on 12 September to $n = 8$, with 5,282 canonical classes in 69 seconds, and to
$n = 9$, with 46,066 classes in 426 seconds, both confirming the conjecture, with the runner-up one
adjacent transposition away. Chapter 10 carries the run and its parameters.

P0289, Ross's second conjecture, is also in chapter 10, where the light-traffic expansion on which the
counterexample rests is re-derived from scratch and a second-order criterion is extracted: to second
order in the traffic parameter, faster modulation lowers the mean workload exactly when it lowers the
variance of the environment's average over one service time. That criterion explains why the
counterexample had to use a non-reversible environment, and chapter 10 states it with its scope. What
belongs here is only the cross-reference and the one-line status: the mechanism is verified, the
quantitative half was closed on 8 September, and the expansion it rests on was re-derived on
12 September.

### The 8 September reading of all 25 proof files

first-passage percolation question, whose header reads "ALREADY REFUTED IN THE LITERATURE. Ahlberg

### Intake D: eight literature exclusions, seven right and one wrong

Intake D's README already flagged that its eight literature exclusions came from abstracts and search
summaries rather than full texts. All eight were then checked by reading the source's own statement of
the question against the cited paper's own statement of what it proves. Seven hold.

D0022, Seppalainen's Problem 1 on disordered zero-range processes, asks to extend the hydrodynamic
description above the critical density or equivalently to more general rate functions, and the cited
Bahadoran, Mountford, Ravishankar and Saada paper covers an arbitrary bounded nondecreasing rate,
asymmetric rather than totally asymmetric, in the condensation domain: both halves. D0013, D0014 and
D0015, the three Angel, Holroyd, Romik and Virag sorting-network conjectures, are all covered by
Dauvergne's *The Archimedean limit of random sorting networks*, whose abstract names all three objects
and closes "These results prove conjectures of Angel, Holroyd, Romik, and Virag". D0096, on the
critical density for activated random walks, asks verbatim to prove the statement for unbiased walks on
$\mathbb{Z}^2$ and "at least prove that $\zeta_c < 1$ for some lambda", and the cited paper is titled
*The critical density for activated random walks is always less than 1*. D0001, on centres of
probability measures, is narrowed by its own source to the case of no finite mean, since a law with a
mean has its centre forced to equal the mean, and the cited paper is titled *Centers of probability
measures without the mean*, with Cauchy carrying an interval of centres. D0055 states the
Derrida-Retaux conjecture with its own hedge, "possibly under some additional integrability conditions
on $Y_0$", and says "We have not been able to prove or disprove the conjecture"; the cited
Chen, Dagard, Derrida, Hu, Lifshits and Shi paper proves it under such conditions, which is what the
hedge anticipates.

D0039 is the one that fails, and it is the only correction in the whole audit that adds a problem
rather than qualifying a claim. The row records Virag, *Operator limits of random matrices*, ICM 2014,
Question 1, and marks it resolved in the literature with `admitted: false`, citing Chhaibi and
Najnudel. Virag's Question 1, verbatim from the archived source at PDF page 6: "Does the beta-Hermite
measure and the Gaussian multiplicative cascade measure with the same Hausdorff dimension have the same
fractal spectrum?" The measure in question is the spectral measure at the first coordinate of the
Jacobi matrix of his Theorem 4.1, whose Hausdorff dimension Breuer, Forrester and Smilansky showed is
almost surely $(1 - 2/\beta)_+$. What Chhaibi and Najnudel prove is an exact identification of Kahane's
Gaussian multiplicative chaos on the circle with the circular beta ensemble, with $\gamma = 2/\beta$
for $\gamma \le 1$. That is the circular analogue. The question as Virag states it is about the
beta-Hermite measure, so the exclusion does not hold and D0039 should be re-admitted as open, with the
circular theorem recorded as the closest known relative and the obvious first thing to try.

How it happened is in the row's own fields. Its `status_search` quotes a search summary saying the
paper "answers positively a question raised by Virag on the fractal spectrum of a random measure
constructed from CbetaE" -- the circular ensemble -- and then records the row as resolved while noting
a caveat. The caveat was right and the disposition did not follow it. So the operational rule is not to
distrust the exclusions, which ran at seven correct out of eight, but this: when the search summary
carries any hedge about scope, that row must be read against the source before it is discarded,
because a resolved row is never revisited and every wrong exclusion is a live problem silently deleted.
That is a worse error than an overstated result.

### Intake D: six overstated partials, no wrong calculation

Every row that intake D's own status file flags as a partial result was checked. No calculation was
wrong anywhere. Two conclusions were phrased more strongly than their derivations support, and one
stated a bound that is true but vacuous.

D0005 proves correctly that every simplex in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is $(d+1)$-completely mixable, by the
rotation cyclically permuting the vertices, which acts on the centred span as the standard
representation of $\mathbb{Z}_{d+1}$ and so has $\sum_{k \le d} R^k = 0$ exactly, and correctly observes
that uniform measure on a body is 2-completely mixable if and only if the body is centrally symmetric
about its centroid. Its gap is the ellipsoid case, justified in one line by "rotate by $2\pi/n$ in a
plane, or use central symmetry". A single-plane rotation is the identity on the orthogonal complement,
so for $d > 2$ the map is not fixed-point-free and $\sum_k R^k$ is $n$ times a projection rather than
zero; and the orbit route cannot work at all when $d$ and $n$ are both odd, because an orthogonal map in
odd dimension always has a real eigenvalue and $-1$ is excluded when $n$ is odd. What works is a
block-diagonal rotation in each of $d/2$ planes when $d$ is even. The conclusion is very likely still
correct, the row's own search naming a paper that settles ellipsoids in a wider setting, but not by the
argument as written.

D0016 is the Brownian-sheet slice-zero count, and its method is calibrated: a first-moment box count in
the sheet's canonical metric reproduces two known facts as controls, the Euclidean dimension
$2 - d/2$ of the zero set at $k = 1$ and $3 - d$ for the pair set. Writing the exponent as
$E(k) = k(d-2) - 2$, the row says the count "predicts that every slice has at most one zero" in
$d = 3$. That overreads the case $k = 2$, whose exponent is exactly zero: a critical count is of order
one and decides nothing, as the row's own obstruction field admits. The correct reading is at most two
zeros, with two critical. The row's conclusion, that $d = 3$ answers yes against the source's
conjecture, is nonetheless supported by a cleaner route: $k \ge 3$ is strictly subcritical, so the count
excludes three or more common zeros outright.

D0010 asks for extra conditions making a unimodal law on $[a,b]$ $n$-completely mixable at the tight
mean $a + (b-a)/n$. Its setup is right: an $n$-mixing coupling makes the vector a random composition of
the fixed total $b-a$ into $n$ nonnegative parts, and the number $N_x$ of parts exceeding $x$ satisfies
$N_x x < b-a$ pathwise. It then takes expectations and derives
$F(a+x) \ge 1 - (b-a)/(nx)$, calling it a clean quantitative extra necessary condition. That inequality
is exactly Markov's: with $Y = X - a \ge 0$ and $E[Y] = (b-a)/n$, Markov gives
$P(Y > x) \le (b-a)/(nx)$ for any law with that mean, mixable or not, so it excludes nothing. The
repair is to keep the integrality: $N_x$ counts parts, so
$N_x \le \lceil (b-a)/x\rceil - 1$, and taking expectations over identical marginals gives
$F(a+x) \ge 1 - (\lceil (b-a)/x\rceil - 1)/n$, which strictly beats Markov. Normalising $b - a = 1$, for
$x \in (1/2,1]$ it reads $F(a+x) \ge 1 - 1/n$ against Markov's weaker $1 - 1/(nx)$.

D0002, D0003 and D0097 hold as written, the first two on joint mixability of binomials and on the orbit
criterion, the third being an honest conjecture rather than a claim: it carries the source's own
computation through with the two-dimensional Green function and outputs the prediction
$\zeta_c(\lambda)$ of order $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\log(1/\lambda)$ as lambda tends to zero, correctly
labelled.

Two further corrections belong to this pass. On the same day two Codex proofs settled the $d = 3$ half
of Khoshnevisan's Open Problem 1 and the $d = 5$ half of his Open Problem 3, both against the source's
own conjecture, and both were audited here line by line with no gap found: a two-parameter
Kolmogorov-Chentsov modulus, the exact slice-covariance determinant, a small-ball bound on separated
configurations, a deterministic grid union bound with a positive exponent, and countable exhaustion. And
in intake E, the E0052 row was found to misstate Feige's conjecture, restating an upper bound on
$P(\sum X_i \ge n+1)$ as a lower bound on $P(\sum X_i \le n+1)$; those are not complements, because
they overlap on the atom at $n+1$, which is exactly where the extremal example puts its mass, so two
downstream claims in the row do not hold as written. The row's central observation, that the conjecture
is genuinely stronger than the trivial Markov bound, survives once aimed at the correct statement.

Two of intake D's own status findings were also raised as separate audit reports that day. One records
that a calibrated box count had been promoted to a verified theorem before the uniform statement was
proved, and its recurrence rule is the transferable line: a calibrated exponent is a plausibility
control, not a verified uniform theorem, and a green check should name the exact gate it checked. The
other records that D0019, Khoshnevisan's Open Problem 4 on projections of the zero set, should not
count as currently open, because Chen and Woessner's Theorem 1.2 gives dimension preservation under
every nonzero linear projection on one event, including a projection chosen after seeing the sample,
and the paper names the source problem it answers.

### The three checks that earned their keep

Three checks caught everything the 8 September audits caught, and they cost minutes each.

Compare the objects by name. What does the source's question name, and what is the cited theorem about?
"beta-Hermite" against "on the circle" caught the one bad exclusion; "more general zero-range processes"
against "arbitrary bounded nondecreasing rate", and "no finite mean" against "without the mean",
confirmed good ones. One exclusion in eight was wrong, and the method fails silently exactly when the
cited theorem is about a neighbouring object.

Test a derived inequality against the trivial bound its own hypotheses supply, before calling it a
condition. D0010's extra necessary condition was Markov's inequality in disguise and excluded nothing;
keeping the integrality of the part count repaired it into something that bites. The same check is what
makes intake E's E0052 correction land the right way round: Markov gives $n/(n+1)$, which tends to one,
while the conjecture asserts $1 - 1/e$.

Read the result field against the obstruction field. Both overstated conclusions, D0005's ellipsoid
justification and D0016's "at most one zero", had the missing caveat already written in the row's own
obstruction text. Nobody had to find new mathematics to catch either; somebody had to read two fields
of the same row against each other.

### The four P30H findings of 9 September

The 9 September pass produced four confirmed findings, all of severity LOW, each with a canonical
report under the audit tree and a brain binding. LOW is the auditor's own classification and it means
something specific here: in each case a displayed statement or a stated premise is wrong while the
theorem or the programme it sits in is not refuted. None of them adds or removes a solved problem.

P30H-F001 is the F0057 count inclusions, described in its place in Part 2. The flaw: two displayed
inclusions omit the good event on which truncated counts represent the original population, and as
written both are false, with exact two-element and four-element counterexamples. What it changes: the
displayed events need their good event and its $o(1)$ failure probability, and the word "size-biased"
needs to become "tilted"; the asymptotic theorem stands. Status: corrected abstract core checked in
Lean, literal source correction pending, and as of the handoff not begun in either copy of the memo.

P30H-F002 is a sign. In the E0065 memo's Section 3.2 the contraction factor
kappa $= 2(1-\alpha)(1-m\alpha)$ is equated to the condition that the polynomial
$2m\alpha^2 - 2(m+1)\alpha + 1$ be positive. Exact expansion gives
kappa $- 1 = 2m\alpha^2 - 2(m+1)\alpha + 1$, so the sign is the other way: at $m = 3$ and
$\alpha = 3/20$, kappa $= 187/200 < 1$ while the polynomial is $-13/200$. What it changes: one
displayed equivalence, with the preceding contraction argument and the following root threshold both
using the correct direction, so the stated sufficient coefficient bound is not refuted. Status:
required correction is to replace the inequality direction in that memo; a formal control theorem
proves the equivalence for every real $m,\alpha$ and an exact rational witness checks the reversed
sign.

P30H-F003 is a scope overstatement in a Lean file's header comment, which calls
$V(x,1-x) = (2x(1-x), x^2 + (1-x)^2)$ an example whose trajectories all converge to the barycentre. The
endpoints are legitimate simplex vertices and they do not: $x = 0$ is fixed at zero and $x = 1$ maps to
zero. What it changes: the documentation, not the mathematics. The compiled theorem proves only the
scalar majorisation inequality and is unaffected, and the memo itself correctly distinguishes
regularity from uniqueness of the barycentre. Status: the comment should say that interior trajectories
converge and boundary starts reach the fixed vertex; control theorems prove that every iterate from
zero is zero and that the sequence does not tend to $1/2$.

P30H-F004 rejects a premise rather than correcting a display, and it is the one with a clean
counterexample. An attempt on the sparse-allocation family stated that the threshold events
$\{Y_i \ge h\}$ are negatively dependent under a product law conditioned on a fixed total, for every
total. Take three nonnegative integer coordinates conditioned to sum to six, with weights
$w_0 = w_2 = 1$, $w_6 = 1/9$, the other small weights zero and $w_j = 1/j!$ for $j \ge 7$ so that the
support is unbounded and the span is one. The only positive configurations are $(2,2,2)$ with
unnormalised weight $1$ and the three permutations of $(6,0,0)$ with weight $1/9$ each, so the
normaliser is $4/3$, each marginal $P(Y_i \ge 2)$ is $5/6$, the joint probability is $3/4$, and the
covariance of the threshold indicators is $3/4 - 25/36 = 1/18 > 0$. That defeats even pairwise negative
correlation. The zero weights are not essential: replacing all four by $\delta = 1/1000$ keeps the
covariance strictly positive, and an independent orbit calculation over the seven unordered orbits of
the 28 ordered compositions reproduces the enumeration exactly. As a negative control, the weights
$1/j!$ for every $j$ give the multinomial law, whose threshold covariance is $-34189/531441$, so the
test does not reject negative dependence in every fixed-total model. What it changes: an attempted
proof plan loses a step, and Poisson approximation has to be derived from factorial moments or another
valid argument rather than from conditioning alone. What it does not change: it is not a disproof of
Janson's Theorem 18.7 or of the sparse counterexample, both of which use different arguments. Status:
SENT, with the notification receipt recorded, the correction to be appended to the attempt history
without overwriting it, and human semantic closure explicitly not claimed.

Two further corrections of that day belong beside these. The MMPP prior-art correction is the E0077
exclusion described in Part 2, whose failure mechanism is that negative search results were treated as
evidence that a theorem had no earlier proof. And a martingale correction, on a harvested central limit
theorem rather than on a campaign row, found a false first identity in a stored proof:
$E\exp(it\sum X_k) = E\prod E[\exp(itX_k) \mid \mathcal F_{k-1}]$, which fails for

retained and a replacement proof by predictable truncation and a telescoping conditional Taylor bound

limit theorem, which is what makes it a proof defect rather than a hypothesis failure. The shared
mechanism recorded for both that and P30H-F004 is the same: a proof step that promotes local
conditional facts into a joint identity without deriving the joint structure.
