## L6. A square-function bound controls the cross and cubic terms

Let (M_k,F_k), 0<=k<=N, be a finite real martingale with M_0=0 and M_N in L^4. Put D_k=M_k-M_{k-1} and Q_N=sum D_k^2. No independence or conditional symmetry of the differences is assumed. Then

    E M_N^4 <= 81 E Q_N^2,
    E Q_N^2 <= 81 E M_N^4.

The terms in L5 also satisfy the absolute bounds

    sum E[M_{k-1}^2 D_k^2] <= 16 E Q_N^2,
    sum |E[M_{k-1} D_k^3]| <= 4 E Q_N^2,
    sum E D_k^4 <= E Q_N^2.

These coarse constants are sufficient for a conditional reduction. No optimal-constant claim is made.

Proof, including the maximal inequality used. Let S=max_{0<=k<=N}|M_k|. For lambda>0, stop at the first index with |M_k|>=lambda. Conditional Jensen gives E[|M_N| | F_k]>=|M_k|. Summing over the disjoint first-hit events therefore gives

    lambda P(S>=lambda) <= E[|M_N| 1_{S>=lambda}].

Since N is finite and each M_k is in L^4, so is S. Integrating the inequality against 4 lambda^2 d lambda and applying Holder yields

    E S^4 <= (4/3) E[|M_N| S^3]
             <= (4/3) ||M_N||_4 ||S||_4^3,
    ||S||_4 <= (4/3) ||M_N||_4.

The zero-norm case is immediate. Conditional Jensen also justifies each M_k in L^4 from M_N in L^4.

Write A=sum M_{k-1}D_k, x=||M_N^2||_2 and y=||Q_N||_2. All these norms are finite. The martingale differences M_{k-1}D_k are orthogonal in L^2, by conditioning a cross product on the later preceding sigma-field. Thus

    E A^2 = sum E[M_{k-1}^2 D_k^2]
           <= E[S^2 Q_N]
           <= ||S||_4^2 ||Q_N||_2
           <= (16/9) x y.

The pathwise identity M_N^2=2A+Q_N follows by expanding each squared increment. Minkowski gives

    x <= (8/3) sqrt(xy)+y.

If y>0, set z=sqrt(x/y). Then z^2<=(8/3)z+1, whose positive root is 3. Hence x<=9y. If y=0, every difference is zero almost surely and the conclusion also holds. Squaring proves E M_N^4<=81 E Q_N^2.

The same identity gives y<=x+(8/3)sqrt(xy); setting z=sqrt(y/x), or handling x=0 separately, gives y<=9x. This proves the reverse comparison.

Substitute x<=9y into E A^2<=(16/9)xy to obtain the cross-term bound 16y^2. Because Q_N^2 contains all the nonnegative D_k^4 terms, their expectations sum to at most y^2. Finally Cauchy--Schwarz first within each expectation and then across k gives

    sum |E[M_{k-1}D_k^3]|
      <= sum (E[M_{k-1}^2D_k^2])^(1/2) (E D_k^4)^(1/2)
      <= (sum E[M_{k-1}^2D_k^2])^(1/2) (sum E D_k^4)^(1/2)
      <= 4y^2.

In particular the cubic terms are bounded, rather than discarded. This completes the proof.

## L7. Exact remaining lattice condition and a predictable criterion

In the original specialization, T_n is the undirected nearest-neighbor passage time from (0,0) to (n,0) in Z^2 with iid Exp(1) edge weights. Enumerate the countably many edges, let F_k be the sigma-field generated by the first k weights, and set

    M_{n,k}=E[T_n | F_k]-E T_n,
    D_{n,k}=M_{n,k}-M_{n,k-1},
    sigma_n^2=Var(T_n),
    Q_{n,k}=sum_{j<=k} D_{n,j}^2.

Use a trivial F_0. T_n has finite fourth moment, since it is nonnegative and bounded above by the weight of the straight n-edge path. Its variance is positive: the minimum of the four weights incident to the starting vertex is a lower bound with unbounded support, while the straight-path upper bound is finite. Thus T_n cannot be almost surely constant. The endpoint is distinct for n>=1.

The following two conditions are equivalent up to universal constants:

    G: sup_n E|T_n-E T_n|^4 / sigma_n^4 < infinity;
    Q: sup_{n,k} E Q_{n,k}^2 / sigma_n^4 < infinity.

If G holds with constant K, conditional Jensen and the reverse inequality in L6 give Q with constant 81K. If Q holds with constant C, the forward inequality in L6 gives E M_{n,k}^4<=81C sigma_n^4. Moreover M_{n,k} converges to T_n-E T_n in the L^4 norm. To justify this step, cylinder random variables measurable with respect to some F_j are dense in L^4(F_infinity); conditional expectation is an L^4 contraction. Approximating T_n-E T_n by such a variable X gives, for k>=j,

    ||E[T_n-E T_n | F_k]-(T_n-E T_n)||_4
      <= 2 ||X-(T_n-E T_n)||_4.

T_n is F_infinity-measurable because it is an infimum over a countable collection of finite-path sums. The displayed approximation therefore proves the asserted convergence. Taking k to infinity yields G with constant 81C.

For a concrete sufficient criterion put

    v_{n,j}=E[D_{n,j}^2 | F_{j-1}],
    V_{n,k}=sum_{j<=k} v_{n,j}.

Suppose fixed finite constants a,b>=0 satisfy, uniformly in n,k,

    E V_{n,k}^2 <= a sigma_n^4,
    sum_{j<=k} E D_{n,j}^4 <= b sigma_n^4.

Then G holds with constant 81(sqrt(a)+sqrt(b))^2. Proof: the differences D_{n,j}^2-v_{n,j} form an L^2 martingale difference sequence. Orthogonality and conditional expectation give the exact identity

    E(Q_{n,k}-V_{n,k})^2
      = sum_{j<=k} (E D_{n,j}^4-E v_{n,j}^2)
      <= sum_{j<=k} E D_{n,j}^4.

Minkowski now bounds ||Q_{n,k}||_2 by ||V_{n,k}||_2 plus the square root of the jump-fourth-moment sum. Apply L6 and L^4 convergence. All uses of orthogonality concern actual martingale differences; no conditional probabilities are multiplied.

This criterion has not been verified for lattice FPP. The comparison Q<=>G is a reformulation of the missing moment estimate, not a proof that it holds. Unconditional identities E Q_{n,k}=E V_{n,k}=E M_{n,k}^2<=sigma_n^2 are insufficient to bound the required second moments of Q or V. The variance denominator here is that of the full T_n, not that of a truncated martingale.

## Counterexample to replacing the square-function second moment by its mean

Let 0<p<=1, B be Bernoulli(p), and epsilon be an independent symmetric sign. Use F_0 trivial, F_1=sigma(B), F_2=sigma(B,epsilon), and M_0=M_1=0, M_2=B epsilon/sqrt(p). This is a martingale with variance 1. Nonetheless

    Q_2=V_2=B/p,
    E Q_2=E V_2=1,
    E Q_2^2=E V_2^2=E M_2^4=1/p.

Thus the needed bound can fail arbitrarily badly even for two independent input variables and a two-step Doob martingale. Independence of the inputs does not make the conditional variance budget deterministic. This example is not a lattice FPP construction.
