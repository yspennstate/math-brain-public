# P0030: common-time and finite bridge cases, 2026-10-10

Status: unsolved in the inspected local material. The prior attempt identifies failure to align two separate random certificates. I continue with precise sufficient hypotheses and a stronger finite-segment version.

Lemma (common splice): for a possibly random time T, suppose the path processes B_plus(t)=X(T+t)-X(T) and B_minus(t)=X(T-t)-X(T), t>=0, are independent standard Brownian motions. Then the centered shifted path Y(s)=X(T+s)-X(T), s in R, has the standard two-sided Brownian law. For disjoint time intervals on either side, increment independence comes from the corresponding Brownian half. For an interval crossing 0, its increment is the sum of one increment from each half, hence Gaussian with variance equal to the interval length. Continuity is inherited. Randomness of T is harmless when these unconditional path-law hypotheses actually hold; mere independence of two non-Brownian selected halves is inadequate.

Lemma (finite bridge splice): fix L>=0. Let B_minus, B_mid on [0,L], and B_plus be three independent standard Brownian pieces, with both outer pieces defined for all nonnegative times. Define Y(s)=B_minus(-s) for s<=0; Y(s)=B_mid(s) for 0<=s<=L; and Y(s)=B_mid(L)+B_plus(s-L) for s>=L. Then Y is two-sided Brownian motion, anchored at 0. Split any finite family of disjoint increments at 0 and L; the resulting Brownian increments are independent and centered, and their variances add to interval lengths. This proves the law by Gaussian finite-dimensional distributions and continuity.

This is a sufficient compatibility test when two certificates are separated by a deterministic finite Brownian bridge independent of both tails. It does not establish that arbitrary separately certified forward/backward decompositions supply such a bridge. In particular, selecting a splice by a future value may bias a marginal. The toy rule T=0 if B_1>0, T=1 otherwise has B_(T+1)-B_T with mean E[B_1 1_(B_1>0)]=1/sqrt(2pi)>0, because the alternative future increment has mean zero; it is not a Brownian one-step increment. T is not asserted to be a stopping time.
