# P0458: exact normal-transform law, conditional application

Status: proof written; w346 KEEP pending. The general growing bilateral-preference degree question remains unverified in the cached literature. The previous candidate's degree convergence is an assumption in the application below, not established again here.

Let Z be standard normal, phi its density, Phi its distribution function, and g(z)=phi(z)+z*Phi(z). Put a=1/sqrt(pi), v=1/3+(sqrt(3)-2)/(2*pi), and W=(a-g(Z))/sqrt(v). Then E[W]=0, Var(W)=1, W has support (-infinity,a/sqrt(v)], has no atom at the finite endpoint, and is not Gaussian. For w<a/sqrt(v), define z=g^{-1}(a-sqrt(v)*w). Then

    P(W<=w)=1-Phi(z),
    density_W(w)=sqrt(v)*phi(z)/Phi(z).

Proof: differentiation gives g'=Phi>0 and g''=phi. Also g(z)=E[(z-Y)_+] for an independent standard normal Y, so g is positive; it tends to zero as z tends to minus infinity and diverges at plus infinity. Thus it is a continuous increasing bijection onto (0,infinity). The distribution and density follow by reversing its monotone change of variables. No finite Z maps to the upper endpoint, so that endpoint has no mass.

For the constants, normal integration by parts gives E[phi(Z)]=1/(2*sqrt(pi)) and E[Z*Phi(Z)]=E[phi(Z)], hence E[g(Z)]=a. Write A=E[phi(Z)^2]=1/(2*pi*sqrt(3)) and B=E[Z*phi(Z)*Phi(Z)]=A/2, by integrating the derivative of phi^2. Since Phi(Z) is uniform, E[Phi(Z)^2]=1/3. Applying E[Z^2*h(Z)]=E[h(Z)]+E[h''(Z)] to h=Phi^2 gives E[Z^2*Phi(Z)^2]=1/3+2A-2B=1/3+A. Therefore E[g(Z)^2]=A+2B+(1/3+A)=1/3+sqrt(3)/(2*pi). Subtracting a^2 gives v. All integrations have Gaussian tails, so the boundary terms vanish. A nondegenerate Gaussian has no finite upper endpoint; W does.

Conditional application: if the earlier packet proves (k-D)/sqrt(k) converges to g(Z) with convergence of the first two moments in a specified k,n regime, subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation gives (D-E[D])/sqrt(Var(D)) converging to W. This consequence does not extend the packet to other k regimes. The saved attempt's error O(k^2/n+k^5/n^2) tends to zero, for example, when k tends to infinity and k=o(n^(2/5)); that observation alone is not a proof of the whole graph limit.

Check: deterministic quadrature of the first two moments, normalization, and inverse-transform round trips. The mathematical proof is analytic; the checks do not validate the earlier graph comparison.
