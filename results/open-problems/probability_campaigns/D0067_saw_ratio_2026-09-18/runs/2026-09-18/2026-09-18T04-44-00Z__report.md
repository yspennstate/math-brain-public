# 4. Mathematical idea before code

There are two independent symmetries in a two-end prescription:

1. a **global** square-lattice symmetry `g in D_4`, which rotates or reflects the entire walk;
2. a **relative** orientation `q in D_4` between the start pattern and the terminal pattern read backward.

The previous argument summed over the eight global images but fixed `q=I`. That leaves seven relative-orientation classes unused for every `k`.

The key observation is that every pair `(T_k,qT_k)` is jointly proper: both are finite, each has an open escape direction, and after translating the second copy far away one can connect their tips through a remote empty corridor. The connector can be lengthened by disjoint three-edge detours, while translating the terminal root by one lattice unit flips the endpoint bipartition class. Thus the pair occurs at arbitrarily large admissible lengths in both parities. Madras's two-end theorem therefore gives a positive lower frequency for **every fixed relative orientation**.

Because the exact initial patterns form a prefix-free family and distinct relative terminal patterns have distinct exact terminal segments, all these events are disjoint. That is B14 and yields the strict metric movement.

B25 then expands the slack itself into nine endpoint-contact indicators and reduces them to four symmetry orbits. B28 tests whether pattern typicality can force those four probabilities to converge; it does not, because all available end-pattern ratio information is still parity-preserving.

# 5. B14 — all relative `D_4` orientations

## 5.1 Definition of the events

Let `G=D_4`, the eight symmetries of the square lattice fixing the origin. For `k>=1`, `g,q in G`, let

\[
E_{n;k,g,q}
\]

be the event that an `n`-step SAW

- begins with the exact ordered pattern `gT_k`, and
- when its terminal segment is read backward from the endpoint and recentered there, that segment is the exact ordered pattern `gqT_k`.

For the identity global orientation define

\[
\tau_{k,q}:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P_n(E_{n;k,I,q}).
\tag{5.1}
\]

Global `D_4` symmetry of uniform rooted square-lattice SAW gives

\[
\mathbb P_n(E_{n;k,g,q})
=
\mathbb P_n(E_{n;k,I,q})
\quad\text{for every }g.
\tag{5.2}
\]

## 5.2 Joint properness for every fixed `(k,q)`

Every `T_k` is finite and self-avoiding. It ends at `(0,-1)`, and from that tip the downward ray

\[
(0,-1),(0,-2),(0,-3),\ldots
\]

immediately leaves the finite pattern and never returns to it. Applying any `q in D_4` gives an analogous escape ray for `qT_k`.

Fix `k` and `q`. Place `T_k` at the origin. Translate the root of the terminal copy `qT_k` so far away that the two finite pattern boxes and long initial pieces of their escape rays are disjoint. Continue both tips along their escape rays past both pattern boxes. In the complement of the two finite occupied sets, choose a sufficiently large rectangular corridor joining the two remote ray points. Traversing the corridor and then the translated terminal pattern backward gives a self-avoiding walk with the requested two end patterns.

This construction gives arbitrarily long realizations. More explicitly, in a remote empty horizontal part of the corridor, replace any unused horizontal edge by the three-edge detour

\[
(x,0)\to(x,1)\to(x+1,1)\to(x+1,0),
\]

which adds exactly two edges without changing the connector endpoints; choosing separated edges gives arbitrarily many such additions. This fills all sufficiently large lengths in one parity class. Translating the terminal root by one lattice edge changes the bipartition class of the two endpoints, and rebuilding the remote corridor yields the other parity class. Hence the pair is compatible at arbitrarily large lengths of both parities.

Therefore the hypotheses needed for Madras's two-end end-pattern theorem are met for every **fixed** pair `(T_k,qT_k)`. Thus

\[
\boxed{\tau_{k,q}>0\qquad(k\ge1,\ q\in D_4).}
\tag{5.3}
\]

No uniform lower bound in either `k` or `q` is used.

## 5.3 Disjointness

The previous run proved that the collection

\[
\{gT_k:k\ge1,\ g\in D_4\}
\tag{5.4}
\]

is prefix-free as a collection of exact ordered initial patterns. Therefore events with different `(k,g)` are disjoint already from their initial segments.

If `(k,g)` is the same but `q!=q'`, the two terminal patterns `gqT_k` and `gq'T_k` are distinct exact paths of the same length, because the `D_4` orbit of `T_k` has size eight. A walk cannot have both as its exact terminal segment. Hence

\[
\{E_{n;k,g,q}\}_{k,g,q}
\quad\text{is pairwise disjoint.}
\tag{5.5}
\]

## 5.4 Slack contribution and strict metric movement

On every `E_{n;k,g,q}`, both endpoint roots are trapped by their prescribed patterns, so

\[
(D_0,D_+)=(0,0),\qquad S_n=9.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Fix `K`. By disjointness,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E S_n
&\ge
9\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{g\in D_4}\sum_{q\in D_4}
\mathbb P_n(E_{n;k,g,q})\\
&=
72\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{q\in D_4}
\mathbb P_n(E_{n;k,I,q}).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.7}
\]

Take `liminf` in `n`. The sum is finite, so

\[
\liminf_n\mathbb E S_n
\ge
72\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{q\in D_4}\tau_{k,q}.
\tag{5.8}
\]

Now let `K->infinity`. Monotonicity gives

\[
\boxed{
\liminf_n\mathbb E S_n\ge72T_{\rm rel},
\qquad
T_{\rm rel}:=
\sum_{k\ge1}\sum_{q\in D_4}\tau_{k,q}.
}
\tag{5.9}
\]

Combining (5.9) with (3.5),

\[
\limsup_n r_n
\le
U_{\rm rel}:=
\frac{\mu^2+9-72T_{\rm rel}}6.
\tag{5.10}
\]

Kesten's theorem gives

\[
r_n r_{n+1}=\frac{\sigma_{n+2}}{\sigma_n}\to\mu^2.
\tag{5.11}
\]

Consequently

\[
\liminf_n r_n\ge\frac{\mu^2}{U_{\rm rel}}.
\tag{5.12}
\]

The product limit also forces `U_rel>=mu`; otherwise eventually both consecutive ratios would be strictly below `mu`, contradicting (5.11). Equivalently,

\[
72T_{\rm rel}\le(3-\mu)^2.
\tag{5.13}
\]

The upper and lower ratio bounds are reciprocal around `mu`, so

\[
\boxed{
A_{\rm SAW}
\le
\log\!\left(\frac{U_{\rm rel}}\mu\right)
=
\log\!\left(
\frac{\mu^2+9-72T_{\rm rel}}{6\mu}
\right).
}
\tag{5.14}
\]

The old constant is the identity-relative slice

\[
T_*=\sum_{k\ge1}\tau_{k,I}.
\tag{5.15}
\]

By (5.3), every off-diagonal term is positive, e.g. `tau_{1,R90}>0`, and hence

\[
T_{\rm rel}>T_*.
\tag{5.16}
\]

Therefore (5.14) is a **strict improvement** of the frozen metric.

# 6. B25 — complete endpoint-contact decomposition

The degree slack has a more informative exact expansion than merely `S_n=(3-D_0)(3-D_+)`.

Orient the start local frame by the first edge of the walk. Among the three neighbors of the origin other than the first visited neighbor, label the directions

\[
B,L,R
\]

(back, left, right). Let `J_0^alpha` be the indicator that the corresponding neighbor is visited later by the walk.

At the terminal endpoint, read the walk backward and use the same convention relative to the first backward edge. Let `J_+^beta` indicate that the corresponding non-predecessor neighbor is occupied elsewhere by the walk.

Because exactly one neighbor at each endpoint is occupied by the incident walk edge,

\[
3-D_0=J_0^B+J_0^L+J_0^R,
\qquad
3-D_+=J_+^B+J_+^L+J_+^R.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
S_n
=
\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{B,L,R\}}
J_0^\alpha J_+^\beta.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Taking expectations expresses the slack as the sum of nine two-end near-contact probabilities.

Reflection swaps `L<->R` simultaneously at both ends, while reversal swaps the two ends. These operations reduce the nine ordered pairs to four orbits:

1. `(B,B)`, size 1;
2. `(B,L),(B,R),(L,B),(R,B)`, size 4;
3. `(L,L),(R,R)`, size 2;
4. `(L,R),(R,L)`, size 2.

If `a_n,b_n,c_n,d_n` denote one representative probability from these four orbits, then exactly

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E S_n=a_n+4b_n+2c_n+2d_n.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Together with (3.5), Question 1.3 is therefore equivalent to

\[
a_n+4b_n+2c_n+2d_n\to(3-\mu)^2.
\tag{6.4}
\]

This is a finite endpoint-view reduction, but it does not by itself move the metric beyond B14.

## 6.1 Counterexample to the obvious contact-switching transfer

Suppose an extra neighbor `v` of the origin is visited for the first time at time `t>1`. Since `v` is adjacent to the origin and `Z^2` is bipartite, `t` is odd. The path segment

\[
\omega_0,\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_t=v
\]

plus the missing nearest-neighbor edge `(v,omega_0)` is a self-avoiding polygon of length `t+1`, necessarily even.

The most natural endpoint-contact surgery replaces the length-`t` cap by the direct edge from the origin to `v`, changing the total walk length by

\[
t-1,
\]

which is **even**. The same statement holds at the terminal endpoint after reversal.

Hence every surgery built only by opening/closing one of these endpoint contact caps is parity-preserving. It cannot directly compare `sigma_n` with `sigma_{n+1}`. This is the exact failure mode of the most tempting B25 transfer.

The finite contact decomposition is still useful: any successful proof must either establish convergence/decorrelation of these four orbit probabilities without such a surgery, or introduce a genuinely parity-breaking geometric operation.

# 7. B28 — why pattern typicality alone does not close the parity gap

Madras's end-pattern theorem supplies more than positivity for a fixed pattern: the fraction of `N`-step walks with that pattern and the corresponding fraction at `N+2` have ratio tending to one. This is powerful, but it is still a **two-step** regularity statement.

It is compatible with different limiting values on the two parity classes. Abstractly, the positive sequence

\[
x_n=\begin{cases}a,&n\text{ even},\\ b,&n\text{ odd},\end{cases}
\qquad a\ne b,
\tag{7.1}
\]

satisfies `x_{n+2}/x_n=1` identically and nevertheless does not converge. Thus the `N`/`N+2` end-pattern ratio cannot, by itself, provide the missing parity mixing.

Kesten's internal-pattern theorem likewise says that proper internal patterns occur linearly often in a typical long walk, with an exponentially small exceptional family. But the pattern theorem as used in the source is precisely what yields the parity-preserving two-step ratio theorem; it contains no automatic cross-parity identification.

Therefore the attempted B28 transfer fails at a precise point:

> **pattern typicality plus two-step end-pattern regularity does not identify the even and odd endpoint-contact laws.**

To turn pattern typicality into a proof of Question 1.3, one still needs an additional parity-breaking ingredient, for example one of:

- an odd length-changing bounded-multiplicity transformation whose global intersections are controlled;
- a theorem forcing the two parity subsequential endpoint-view laws to coincide;
- a genuine endpoint regeneration/decorrelation mechanism strong enough to make the four probabilities in (6.3) converge to the same parity-independent limits.

No such ingredient is proved in this run.
