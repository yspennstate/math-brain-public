## 5.1 Common-negative majority family: exact non-realizability theorem

The abstract no-go family from the previous report has the following literal vertex-state realization. Let

\[
V(B)=W\,\dot\cup\, Z,
\qquad |W|=2m+1,\quad |Z|=m,
\]

with `m>=1`. For every `(m+1)`-subset `T\subseteq W`, take

\[
S=Z,\qquad U=W\setminus T.
\]

Then `|T|-|S|=1`. The abstract majority obstruction would require **every** such pair `(Z,T)` to be a positive FIRST-SCALE barrier.

### Theorem 5.1 — common-negative majority obstruction is impossible

For fixed even `k>=4` and `r>2k`, there is no finite graph `B` satisfying FIRST-SCALE for which every pair above is a positive `k`-factor barrier.

### Proof

Assume such a graph exists.

For every `(m+1)`-subset `T\subseteq W`, positivity in FIRST-SCALE and (4.4) give

\[
e(B[T])\le \frac{k-2}{2}=:J.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Sum (5.1) over all `T\in\binom{W}{m+1}`. Every edge of `B[W]` is counted in exactly

\[
\binom{2m-1}{m-1}
\]

of these induced subgraphs, because after fixing its two endpoints the other `m-1` vertices of `T` may be chosen from the remaining `2m-1` vertices. Hence

\[
e(B[W])\binom{2m-1}{m-1}
\le
J\binom{2m+1}{m+1}.
\]

The binomial ratio is

\[
\frac{\binom{2m+1}{m+1}}{\binom{2m-1}{m-1}}
=
\frac{(2m+1)(2m)}{(m+1)m}
=
\frac{2(2m+1)}{m+1}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
e(B[W])
\le
(k-2)\frac{2m+1}{m+1}.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Now use degree capacity. Since `V(B)=W\dot\cup Z`,

\[
\sum_{w\in W}d_B(w)=2e(B[W])+e(W,Z).
\]

Also

\[
e(W,Z)\le\sum_{z\in Z}d_B(z)\le r|Z|=rm.
\]

Thus

\[
\begin{aligned}
s(W)
&=r|W|-\sum_{w\in W}d_B(w)\\
&\ge r(2m+1)-2e(B[W])-rm\\
&=r(m+1)-2e(B[W]).
\end{aligned}
\]

Since `D>=s(W)`, (5.2) gives

\[
D
\ge
r(m+1)-2(k-2)\frac{2m+1}{m+1}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

But FIRST-SCALE requires

\[
D\le2R=2r-4k.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Subtracting the right side of (5.4) from the lower bound in (5.3) gives

\[
r(m-1)+4k
-2(k-2)\frac{2m+1}{m+1}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

If `m=1`, (5.5) equals

\[
4k-3(k-2)=k+6>0.
\]

If `m>=2`, then `(2m+1)/(m+1)<2`, so (5.5) is strictly larger than

\[
r(m-1)+4k-4(k-2)=r(m-1)+8>0.
\]

Thus (5.3) forces `D>2r-4k`, contradicting FIRST-SCALE. This proves the theorem. `\square`

# 6. B14 — the exact zero-slack substrate is cactus-like, but near-tight barriers are not yet cactus arcs

\tag{6.3}

This is a real structural bridge to cactus theory. Classical cactus representations compactly encode all minimum cuts, and in a bridgeless graph the 2-edge-cut relation is represented by cactus cycles after contracting the 3-edge-connected pieces. A current 2026 example of this principle is Ghanbari–Šámal's algorithm for well-spread perfect matchings in bridgeless cubic graphs, whose main structural device is a cactus representation of 2-edge-cuts followed by reductions/gluing.

\[
d-q\le k-2,
\]

not necessarily zero. Its `O(k)` excess can arise through internal `T` edges, components with three or more `T` boundary edges, and asymmetric `S` incidence. There is also a useful prior-art warning from modern general-factor theory. The local all-or-none degree set `{0,k}` has a gap of length `k-1`, so for the source regime `k>=4` it is outside this generic symmetric-delta-matroid class. In contrast `{0,2}` has only a one-step gap. This gives a precise structural reason that generic delta-matroid exchange / matching-gadget machinery and the sharp `k=2` loop behavior should not be assumed to extend to even `k>=4`.

# 7. B20 — why the actual greedy signed-repair algorithm still fails

The positive FIRST-SCALE constraints all have unit demand:

\[
|X\cap T|-|X\cap S|\ge1.
\tag{7.1}
\]

An obvious greedy repair procedure is:

1. take a currently violated positive row;
2. choose a vertex in its `T` side, preferably one lying in many other violated positive `T` sides;
3. add it to `X`;
4. repeat until all positive rows are satisfied, while checking all signed rows.

The previous abstract majority family showed why a purely scalar version could require arbitrarily many choices. Theorem 5.1 removes that **specific** failure mode from genuine graphs: a fixed common negative side cannot support the complete majority clutter inside FIRST-SCALE.

But the exact signed formulation reveals the remaining failure. If a chosen vertex lies in `S` for another row, it contributes `-1` there. In particular, even a row with rounded demand `rho=0` may become violated after the choice. Thus positive-side hitting is not monotone.

The new overlap-core theorem (5.10) further says that, for three simultaneously conflicting positive rows with empty common `T`, the vertices that are positive in two rows span only `O_k(1)` internal edges. Their many incident edges therefore leave the overlap core. A successful charging proof would need to show that each greedy failure forces `Omega(r)` **fresh** external incidence/cut structure, so only `O_k(1)` failures can coexist under the first-scale budget.

That freshness statement is exactly where the algorithm breaks. An external incidence may enter a large near-`r`-regular `U` component attached through a 2-edge-cut or near-2-edge-cut and can then be reused by several different signed rows through different orientations. Low vertex deficiency does not charge the size of such a component. The 2-cut cactus can compactly reuse the same underlying cycle structure for many cuts. I do not have a valid bound on this reuse multiplicity for the `O(k)`-perturbed barriers.

Therefore the algorithmic obstruction is now sharply identified:

\[
\boxed{
\text{bound the reuse multiplicity of one 2-cut / near-2-cut incidence structure
across sign-conflicting FIRST-SCALE barriers.}
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

If that reuse is `O_k(1)`, greedy repair can plausibly be charged to `Omega(r)` fresh structure and FIRST-SCALE ROUNDING follows. If it is unbounded, the repeated orientation pattern is the natural seed for a genuine counterexample family.
