# Negation-first stress test: the `D/r` scale is locally sharp

Before trying to prove `(LOCAL-REPAIR)`, test whether its right scale can already fail in an elementary bridgeless block.

For an integer `t>=1`, assume `r-t>=max{k,2}` and let

\[
B_{r,t}:=K_{r-t,r}.
\]

This graph is connected and bridgeless, and `Delta(B_{r,t})=r`. On the side of size `r-t`, every vertex has degree `r`, hence zero `r`-deficiency. On the side of size `r`, every vertex has degree `r-t`, hence deficiency `t`. Consequently

\[
D_r(B_{r,t})=rt.
\tag{1}
\]

Any `k`-regular subgraph of a bipartite graph covers the same number of vertices on the two sides: if those numbers are `u` and `w`, then its edge count is simultaneously `ku` and `kw`, so `u=w`. Therefore at most `r-t` vertices can be covered on either side, and

\[
f_k(B_{r,t})\le 2(r-t).
\]

Conversely, choose any `r-t` vertices on the larger side. The resulting `K_{r-t,r-t}` contains a spanning `k`-factor whenever `k<=r-t` (for example, take `k` members of a 1-factorization). Thus

\[
f_k(B_{r,t})=2(r-t),
\qquad
\boxed{g_k(B_{r,t})=t=D_r(B_{r,t})/r.}
\tag{2}
\]

So `(LOCAL-REPAIR)` cannot have an asymptotically smaller scale than `D/r`, even locally. The first two deficiency scales are already witnessed by `t=1,2`. This is not a counterexample; it is an exact extremal calibration supporting the conjectured scale.

# B3 — prove something stronger: exact signed-barrier formulation

The first run identified the conceptual gap: an ordinary `k`-factor theorem decides whether *every* vertex is covered, whereas a maximum `k`-regular subgraph has the allowed degree set `{0,k}`. For even `k`, however, the parity term in Tutte's `f`-factor theorem has a useful rigidity: changing a vertex demand from `k` to `0` does not change any parity.

This yields an exact formulation of the entire local repair problem as one simultaneous signed barrier-hitting problem.

## Theorem B3.1 (exact omitted-set / signed-barrier equivalence)

For disjoint `S,T subseteq V(B)`, let `R_0=V(B)\(S union T)`, and let `q(S,T)` be the number of components `Q` of `B[R_0]` for which `e_B(Q,T)` is odd. Define the constant-`k` Tutte deficiency

\[
\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)
:=k|T|-k|S|-d_{B-S}(T)+q(S,T).
\tag{3}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
 g_k(B)
 =\min\Bigl\{|X|:\
 k\bigl(|X\cap T|-|X\cap S|\bigr)
 \ge \operatorname{def}_k(S,T)
 \text{ for every disjoint }S,T
 \Bigr\}.
}
\tag{4}
\]

### Proof

Fix `X subseteq V(B)` and define a vertex demand

\[
f_X(v)=\begin{cases}
0,&v\in X,\\
k,&v\notin X.
\end{cases}
\]

An `f_X`-factor is exactly a `k`-regular subgraph covering every vertex outside `X` and no vertex of `X`: vertices of `X` have factor degree zero, and all other vertices have factor degree `k`. Therefore `g_k(B)` is the minimum `|X|` for which an `f_X`-factor exists.

For disjoint `S,T`, Tutte's `f`-factor theorem uses

\[
\operatorname{def}_{f_X}(S,T)
=f_X(T)-f_X(S)-d_{B-S}(T)+q_{f_X}(S,T),
\]

where a component `Q` of `B-S-T` is counted in `q_{f_X}` if

\[
f_X(Q)+e_B(Q,T)
\]

is odd. Since `k` is even, `f_X(Q)=k|Q\setminus X|` is always even. Hence

\[
q_{f_X}(S,T)=q(S,T),
\]

independently of `X`. Also

\[
f_X(T)=k(|T|-|X\cap T|),
\qquad
f_X(S)=k(|S|-|X\cap S|).
\]

Substitution gives the exact identity

\[
\operatorname{def}_{f_X}(S,T)
=
\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)
-k|X\cap T|+k|X\cap S|.
\tag{5}
\]

Tutte's theorem says an `f_X`-factor exists iff (5) is nonpositive for every disjoint `S,T`. Rearranging gives exactly (4). ∎

### Meaning

This is stronger than merely saying that the problem is a `{0,k}` general-factor problem. It identifies the exact combinatorial object that must be rounded: every positive constant-`k` barrier asks the omitted set to have more mass on its `T` side than on its `S` side.

The missing source theorem is therefore equivalent to proving that the entire signed system (4) always has a `0/1` solution `X` of size `O_k(D/r)` in a connected bridgeless `Delta<=r` host.

## Lemma B3.2 (every positive barrier has small imbalance and consumes deficiency)

Write

\[
a=|S|,\qquad b=|T|,\qquad h=b-a.
\]

If `def_k(S,T)>0`, then

\[
\boxed{h\ge1,}
\tag{6}
\]

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)\le kh,}
\tag{7}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)
\le
\frac{s(T)-s(S)-R h}{2}.
}
\tag{8}
\]

In particular every positive barrier satisfies

\[
\boxed{s(T)-s(S)>Rh.}
\tag{9}
\]

### Proof

The first-run B11 proof already showed that if `a>=b`, then the corresponding factor quantity `Phi=-def_k` is nonnegative. Hence a positive deficiency forces `b>a`, proving (6).

Because `k` is even, every component counted by `q(S,T)` has a positive odd number of edges into `T`; therefore

\[
q(S,T)\le e(R_0,T)\le d_{B-S}(T).
\]

Using (3),

\[
\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)
=kh-d_{B-S}(T)+q(S,T)
\le kh,
\]

which proves (7).

For (8), each component counted by `q` either has at least three edges to `T`, or has exactly one edge to `T`. In the latter case bridgelessness forces at least one additional edge from that component to `S`; otherwise its sole edge to `T` would be a bridge. Thus

\[
2q(S,T)\le e(R_0,T)+e(R_0,S).
\tag{10}
\]

Degree sums over `S union T` give

\[
e(R_0,T)+e(R_0,S)
=r(a+b)-s(S)-s(T)-2e(S)-2e(T)-2e(S,T).
\tag{11}
\]

Also

\[
d_{B-S}(T)=rb-s(T)-e(S,T).
\tag{12}
\]

Substituting (10)--(12) into `Phi=-def_k` yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(S,T)
&=ka+d_{B-S}(T)-kb-q(S,T)\\
&\ge
\left(\frac r2-k\right)h
+\frac{s(S)-s(T)}2
+e(S)+e(T)\\
&\ge
\frac{Rh+s(S)-s(T)}2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Negating gives (8). If `def_k>0`, the right side of (8) must be positive, which is (9). ∎

## Corollary B3.3 (quantitative simple-b-matching relaxation)

Let

\[
\gamma_k(B):=
 k|V(B)|-
 \max_{H\subseteq B,\ d_H(v)\le k}
 \sum_{v\in V(B)}d_H(v).
\]

Thus `gamma_k(B)` is the total degree deficit of a maximum simple `k`-bounded subgraph. By the generalized Tutte–Berge formula of Schrijver (in the Qu–West formulation),

\[
\gamma_k(B)=\max_{S,T}\operatorname{def}_k(S,T).
\]

Lemma B3.2 gives

\[
\boxed{
\gamma_k(B)
\le
\max\left\{0,\frac{D-(r-2k)}2\right\}.
}
\tag{13}
\]

### Proof

A nonpositive barrier contributes nothing to the maximum. For a positive barrier, (6) gives `h>=1`, and (8), together with `s(T)-s(S)<=D`, gives

\[
\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)
\le\frac{D-Rh}{2}
\le\frac{D-R}{2}.
\]

Taking the maximum proves (13). ∎

At `D<=R`, (13) recovers the first-run spanning threshold: the maximum bounded degree sum equals `k|V|`, hence a spanning `k`-factor exists.

There is also a useful *multiplicity relaxation* of the signed system. If the `0/1` requirement on `X` is temporarily replaced by arbitrary nonnegative weights `x_v`, then

\[
x_v:=\frac{s(v)}{R}
\tag{14}
\]

satisfies every signed barrier inequality, because for every positive barrier

\[
x(T)-x(S)
=\frac{s(T)-s(S)}{R}
>h
\ge\frac{\operatorname{def}_k(S,T)}k.
\tag{15}
\]

Its total mass is exactly `D/R`. In a nontrivial connected bridgeless graph, `d(v)>=2`, so each coordinate in (14) is at most

\[
\frac{r-2}{r-2k}=1+O_k(r^{-1}).
\]

This is **not** a proof of `(LOCAL-REPAIR)`: the relaxation permits a vertex to carry weight slightly above one and, more importantly, does not solve the simultaneous `0/1` rounding of signed `S/T` barriers. It does show that the desired `D/r` scale is already present exactly in the dual obstruction geometry; the missing step is concentration/rounding, not another power of `r` in the coarse barrier bound.

# B19 — make it an algorithm, then locate the exact failure

A natural algorithm is now visible: repeatedly find a positive Tutte barrier and omit a vertex from its `T` side. Equation (5) says that such an omission lowers that barrier deficiency by exactly `k`; omitting a vertex from its `S` side raises it by `k`. The difficulty is therefore not finding a local augmenting move. It is coordinating moves so that repairing one barrier does not create or worsen another.

Before promoting any augmenting-path argument, test whether small *degree* deficiency of a `k`-bounded subgraph controls vertex omissions. It does not, even qualitatively, in general graphs.

## Proposition B19.1 (degree-deficit-to-support conversion can have an arbitrarily large cascade)

Let `K` be a connected `k`-regular graph and let `e` be a non-bridge edge. Put

\[
H:=K-e.
\]

Then `H` is connected and has a `k`-bounded subgraph with total degree deficit exactly `2`, but `H` has no nonempty `k`-regular subgraph.

### Proof

The whole graph `H`, viewed as a `k`-bounded subgraph of itself, has every vertex at degree `k` except the two endpoints of `e`, which have degree `k-1`. Hence its degree sum is `k|V(H)|-2`, so the generalized Tutte–Berge deficit is at most `2`. Since `H` has no spanning `k`-factor (the two endpoints do not even have host degree `k`) and the deficit has the parity of `k|V(H)|`, it is in fact exactly `2`.

Now suppose `F` is a nonempty `k`-regular subgraph of `H`. Neither endpoint of `e` can belong to `F`, since its host degree in `H` is only `k-1`. If `F` contains any other vertex `v`, then `d_H(v)=k`; to have degree `k` in `F`, *all* edges of `H` incident to `v` must be selected. Thus every neighbor of `v` belongs to `F`. Repeating this argument along paths in the connected graph `H` propagates membership until one reaches an endpoint of `e`, a contradiction. Therefore `F` is empty. ∎

This does **not** refute the source or `(LOCAL-REPAIR)`: the source-relevant parameter `r` is asymptotically much larger than fixed `k`, whereas the example's natural maximum degree is `k`; if it is viewed with a much larger external `r`, its `D_r` is already of order `r|V|`, and `D_r/r` is of order `|V|`.

What it does refute is a tempting proof pipeline:

> small generalized Tutte–Berge degree deficit -> few initially deficient vertices -> delete them -> small `{0,k}` support loss.

Deletion can cascade through an entire saturated component. Any successful B19 algorithm must exploit the **near-`r`-regular host geometry and the signed barrier resource (9)**, not just the `k`-bounded degree deficit (13).

The exact algorithmic target is now:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Find }X\subseteq V(B),\quad |X|=O_k(D/r),\quad
|X\cap T|-|X\cap S|\ge \operatorname{def}_k(S,T)/k
\text{ for every positive barrier.}
}
\tag{SIGNED-REPAIR}
\]

Because the left side is integral, one may replace the right side by its ceiling. A greedy algorithm must come with a barrier-coordination/uncrossing theorem or a charging invariant that prevents repeated use of the same deficiency resource.

# B30 — one deficiency scale at a time

Lemma B3.2 makes the scale localization exact.

## Theorem B30.1 (barrier imbalance is bounded by the deficiency scale)

For every positive constant-`k` Tutte barrier `(S,T)`, with `h=|T|-|S|`,

\[
\boxed{
1\le h<\frac{D}{r-2k}.
}
\tag{16}
\]

Consequently, if `D<=m(r-2k)` for an integer `m>=1`, then every positive barrier has

\[
\boxed{h\le m-1.}
\tag{17}
\]

### Proof

The lower bound `h>=1` is (6). From (9),

\[
D\ge s(T)-s(S)>Rh,
\]

which gives (16). If `D<=mR`, then `h<m`; since `h` is a positive integer, `h<=m-1`. ∎

This turns the first scale beyond the B11 spanning regime into a much smaller problem.

### First supercritical scale

If

\[
R<D\le2R,
\]

then every positive barrier has exactly

\[
h=1.
\]

By (7), every such barrier has `0<def_k(S,T)<=k`. Since the left side in (4) is an integer multiple of `k`, the exact omission condition for *every* positive barrier on this scale collapses to

\[
\boxed{|X\cap T|-|X\cap S|\ge1.}
\tag{SIGNED-TRANSVERSAL-1}
\]

Thus the first supercritical case is no longer an arbitrary general-factor instance: it is a signed transversal problem for a family of `h=1` barriers, each of which consumes more than `R` net units of vertex deficiency by (9).

This special case remains unproved. It is a natural next target because `B_{r,1}=K_{r-1,r}` lies on this scale and has exactly one unavoidable omission.

### Requested wider scale `D<=2r`

If `D<=2r` and `r>6k`, then

\[
\frac{D}{R}\le\frac{2r}{r-2k}<3,
\]

so every positive barrier has

\[
h\in\{1,2\}.
\]

The exact signed requirement (4) is therefore only a one-unit or two-unit imbalance requirement. The calibration graph `K_{r-2,r}` has `D=2r` and `g_k=2`, showing that two omissions genuinely occur at this boundary.

What is still missing is a **gluing theorem**: low imbalance of each individual barrier does not by itself guarantee that a single small set `X` satisfies all barriers simultaneously. The signed nature matters because placing a vertex in `T` helps a barrier while placing it in `S` hurts it.
