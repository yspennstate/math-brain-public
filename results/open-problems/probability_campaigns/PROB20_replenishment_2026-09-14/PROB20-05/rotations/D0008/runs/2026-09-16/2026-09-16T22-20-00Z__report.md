## 1. Exact source scope and current status

The frozen source class remains Wang's condition (b): if `F` has a monotone density on its essential support `[a,b]`, with finite mean `mu`, then `F` is `n`-completely mixable exactly when

\[
a+\frac{b-a}{n}\le \mu\le b-\frac{b-a}{n}.
\]

Open Problem 8 asks for an explicit copula / explicit joint complete mix for a distribution already known to be `n`-CM under such a sufficient condition. Approximation, a weak-limit existence argument, or a construction covering only a subclass is not the requested endpoint.

The current run recovered the full author-hosted working-paper proof behind condition (b): Bin Wang and Ruodu Wang, *The Complete Mixability and Convex Minimization Problems with Monotone Marginal Densities* (JMVA 2011; current author-hosted PDF). Its Lemma 2.6 is a finite combinatorial peeling theorem for decreasing mass functions; Theorem 2.4 passes to continuous monotone densities by discretizing and then taking a weak limit. Most importantly, Remark 2.3 states explicitly that the paper did **not** construct the random variables with constant sum even though existence had been proved. Thus the explicitness gap in Open Problem 8 is genuinely the gap between the finite peeling and the continuum coupling, not a missing citation to a formula already present in that proof.

## 3. B12 — a two-point extreme reduction for the entire decreasing `n=3` source class

It is enough to work with decreasing densities; increasing densities are reflected. Affinely normalize the source interval and then, for `n=3`, normalize the center as follows. If `X` has a decreasing density on `[0,1]` and mean `mu in [1/3,1/2]`, define

\[
Y=\frac{X}{\mu}-1.
\]

Then `Y` has mean zero, a decreasing density `p` on a subset of `[-1,2]`, and we extend `p` by zero to all of `[-1,2]`.

### 3.1 Layer representation

Every nonincreasing probability density `p` on `[-1,2]` can be represented (in the Lebesgue–Stieltjes sense if necessary) as a mixture of initial-interval uniforms:

\[
P=\int_{[-1,2]} \operatorname{Unif}[-1,t]\,H(dt),
\tag{L}
\]

where `H` is a probability measure. Equivalently, for a.e. `x`,

\[
p(x)=\int_{[x,2]}\frac{1}{t+1}\,H(dt).
\]

For a right-continuous monotone version of `p`, one may take the Stieltjes measure

\[
H(dt)=-(t+1)\,dp(t)+3p(2)\,\delta_2(dt),
\]

with the endpoint interpreted in the usual measure sense. Because the conditional mean of `Unif[-1,t]` is `(t-1)/2`, the zero-center condition gives the conserved quantity

\[
\boxed{\int t\,H(dt)=1.}
\tag{M}
\]

Thus the original continuum density is encoded by a probability law `H` on `[-1,2]` with one scalar moment constraint.

### 3.2 Explicit decomposition of the moment law into two-point extremes

Write `H_-` for the restriction of `H` to `[-1,1)`, `H_+` for the restriction to `(1,2]`, and let `h_1=H({1})`. Define the common deficit/surplus mass

\[
D=\int_{r<1}(1-r)H(dr)=\int_{s>1}(s-1)H(ds),
\]

where equality is exactly (M). If `D=0`, then `H=delta_1` and `P=Unif[-1,1]`.

Assume `D>0`. For every `r<1<s`, define the two-point mean-one law

\[
E_{r,s}
=\frac{s-1}{s-r}\delta_r
 +\frac{1-r}{s-r}\delta_s.
\tag{E}
\]

Define the finite mixing measure

\[
M(dr,ds)=\frac{s-r}{D}\,H_-(dr)H_+(ds).
\tag{B}
\]

Then direct integration gives

\[
\int \frac{s-1}{s-r}M(dr,ds)=H_-(dr),
\qquad
\int \frac{1-r}{s-r}M(dr,ds)=H_+(ds),
\]

and consequently

\[
\boxed{
H=h_1\delta_1+\iint E_{r,s}\,M(dr,ds).
}
\tag{D}
\]

Because the map `H -> P` in (L) is affine, every decreasing zero-center source density therefore has the explicit decomposition

\[
P
=h_1\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1]
 +\iint P_{r,s}\,M(dr,ds),
\tag{R}
\]

where

\[
P_{r,s}
=\frac{s-1}{s-r}\operatorname{Unif}[-1,r]
 +\frac{1-r}{s-r}\operatorname{Unif}[-1,s].
\tag{T}
\]

Each `P_{r,s}` has center zero. Hence **if one supplies an explicit 3-complete-mix copula `Gamma_{r,s}` for every pair `r<1<s`, then (R) immediately supplies an explicit complete mix for every decreasing source density**: sample the component according to `h_1 delta_1 + M`, sample that component's zero-sum triple, and finally randomize the coordinates if desired. Mixtures preserve the common constant sum because every component is centered at zero.

This is a genuine reduction of the full `n=3` source class to a two-parameter, two-step density family. It is not yet a solution: the generic `P_{r,s}` kernel is the remaining exact obligation.

**B12 outcome:** the conserved quantity is the first moment of the layer endpoint law, and the full source class reduces explicitly to the family (T). This substantially narrows the target without changing `n_explicit`.

## 4. B27 — two exact failures of apparently universal operators

### 4.1 Why coupling the layer radii is too restrictive

A tempting use of (L) is to couple three layer endpoints so that their sum is constant and then, conditional on those endpoints, jointly mix the three uniforms. This fails even for a simple source-valid law.

On `[0,3]`, consider

\[
f_*(x)=\frac23\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}(x)+\frac16\mathbf 1_{(1,3]}(x).
\]

It is decreasing, integrates to one, and has mean one, so it is the boundary `n=3` source case. Its initial-interval layer law is

\[
\frac12\delta_1+\frac12\delta_3,
\]

because

\[
f_* =\frac12\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]
      +\frac12\operatorname{Unif}[0,3].
\]

If three such layer lengths were coupled with constant total `6` (the total length needed for conditional uniform centers to sum to three), their possible sums would be only `3,5,7,9`; `6` is impossible. Thus the radius-coupling lift cannot be universal.

This is **not** a counterexample to complete mixability. In fact an explicit complete mix is immediate: let `U~Unif[0,1]`, take

\[
(U,U,3-2U),
\]

and apply an independent uniform random permutation. The sum is exactly three, while the pooled marginal is exactly `f_*`.

So the failure is informative: a source-general construction must mix *inside* the layer components rather than require the layer labels themselves to be completely mixable.

### 4.2 Why the one-curve equal-pair ansatz is also not universal

A second attractive idea is to look for an exchangeable mix obtained by randomly permuting

\[
(T,T,-2T).
\]

If `T` has density `g`, then the resulting common marginal density must satisfy

\[
p(x)=\frac23g(x)+\frac16g(-x/2).
\tag{Q}
\]

For bounded `g`, (Q) uniquely gives

\[
g(x)=\frac32\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\left(-\frac14\right)^k
p\!\left(\left(-\frac12\right)^k x\right).
\tag{S}
\]

Take the simple source-valid decreasing triangular density on `[-1,2]`

\[
p_{\triangle}(x)=\frac{4-2x}{9}.
\]

It has mean zero. Every iterated argument in (S) remains in `[-1,2]`, so the two geometric series can be summed exactly:

\[
\boxed{g(x)=\frac8{15}-\frac{8x}{21}.}
\]

This is negative for `x>7/5`. Therefore no probability density `g` can make the equal-pair curve produce this triangular source law. Again the source theorem is not refuted; only this proposed universal support geometry is.

**B27 outcome:** both a layer-label complete mix and the simplest singular one-curve operator fail exactly. The successful kernel for the two-step extremes (T) must permit a genuinely richer partner split, matching the multi-pair structure seen in Wang–Wang's discrete peeling.

## 5. B30 — explicit finite-scale source-general epsilon complete mixes for `n=3`

The finite combinatorial part of Wang–Wang is constructive. The nonconstructive point is the passage from those discrete complete mixes to a continuous complete mix. Instead of taking that weak limit, this run lifts each finite complete mix back to the **exact original marginal** and tracks the resulting sum error.

### Theorem (explicit exact-marginal epsilon mix, `n=3`)

Let `F` have a monotone density on `[a,b]`, mean `mu`, and satisfy Wang's `n=3` mean condition. Put

\[
D_0=b-a,
\qquad
m=\frac{\mu-a}{D_0},
\qquad
\rho=\min(m,1-m)\in[1/3,1/2].
\]

For every integer `N>=1` there is an explicit randomized finite procedure producing `X_1^{(N)},X_2^{(N)},X_3^{(N)}` such that each coordinate has **exactly** law `F` and

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\left|X_1^{(N)}+X_2^{(N)}+X_3^{(N)}-3\mu\right|
\le \frac{21D_0\rho}{N}
\le \frac{21D_0}{2N}.
}
\tag{A}
\]

Hence, given `epsilon>0`, choosing `N >= 21D_0 rho/epsilon` yields an explicit exact-marginal coupling whose expected absolute constant-sum error is at most `epsilon`.

This theorem is **PARTIAL** for Open Problem 8: the sum is not identically constant at finite `N`.

### Proof

Reflect if necessary, so after affine normalization to `[0,1]` the density is decreasing and its mean `rho` lies in `[1/3,1/2]`. Let `Z` denote the normalized `[0,1]` variable and set

\[
Y=\frac{Z}{\rho}-1.
\]

Then `Y` has mean zero and a decreasing density on `[-1,1/rho-1] subset [-1,2]`; extend the density by zero to `[-1,2]`. Let `P` be its law.

For each `N`, let

\[
\bar Y_N=\frac{\lfloor NY\rfloor}{N}
\]

and write `\bar P_N` for its law. Let `\widehat P_N` be the discrete uniform law on

\[
S_N=\{-1,-1+1/N,\ldots,2\}.
\]

Set

\[
m_N=\mathbb E\bar Y_N\in[-1/N,0].
\]

Since `\widehat P_N` has mean `1/2`, define

\[
\lambda_N=\frac{-m_N}{1/2-m_N}.
\]

Then

\[
0\le\lambda_N<\frac2N
\]

and

\[
P_N=(1-\lambda_N)\bar P_N+\lambda_N\widehat P_N
\tag{P_N}
\]

has mean zero. Equal-grid cell masses of a decreasing density form a decreasing sequence, and convex mixing with the uniform grid mass preserves that property, so `P_N` has a decreasing probability mass function on `S_N`.

Apply Wang–Wang Lemmas 2.6 and 2.8 with `d=2`. Their induction is an explicit finite peeling: it writes the mass function of `P_N` as a finite nonnegative linear combination of simple mass functions, each consisting of exactly three integer grid points (with multiplicity) whose sum is zero. Therefore one obtains an explicit random triple

\[
(Z_1^{(N)},Z_2^{(N)},Z_3^{(N)})
\]

with every coordinate distributed as `P_N` and

\[
Z_1^{(N)}+Z_2^{(N)}+Z_3^{(N)}=0
\quad\text{a.s.}
\tag{0}
\]

For each simple component, sample a uniform random permutation of its three-point multiset; sample the component with probability proportional to its peeling coefficient. This turns the finite mass decomposition into an explicit sampler.

Now lift each discrete coordinate back to the exact continuous law `P`. In one dimension, use the monotone quantile coupling. Concretely, if `F_N` is the CDF of `P_N`, then conditional on `Z_i^{(N)}=z`, draw

\[
V_i\sim\operatorname{Unif}[F_N(z^-),F_N(z)]
\]

and put

\[
Y_i^{(N)}=F_P^{-1}(V_i).
\]

The union of those rank intervals is `[0,1]`, with interval length equal to the corresponding atom mass, so each `V_i` is uniform and each `Y_i^{(N)}` has **exactly** law `P`.

The coupling cost is the one-dimensional Wasserstein-1 distance. First,

\[
W_1(P,\bar P_N)\le \frac1N
\]

by the defining floor coupling. Second, because both laws are supported on an interval of diameter three,

\[
W_1(\bar P_N,P_N)
\le 3\lambda_N
<\frac6N.
\]

Therefore

\[
W_1(P,P_N)<\frac7N.
\]

Using (0) and the triangle inequality,

\[
\mathbb E\left|Y_1^{(N)}+Y_2^{(N)}+Y_3^{(N)}\right|
\le \sum_{i=1}^3\mathbb E|Y_i^{(N)}-Z_i^{(N)}|
<\frac{21}{N}.
\]

Undoing the scale by `rho` and then by `D_0` gives (A). Reflection does not change the absolute sum error. QED.

### What this adds to the published weak-limit proof

The 2011 proof constructs completely mixable `P_N` and then invokes closure under weak convergence to establish existence for `P`. The finite coupling above instead returns to the exact target marginal at every `N`. Thus it gives a concrete source-general sampler with an explicit error guarantee, not merely a numerical rearrangement heuristic. What it still lacks is **projective consistency / a contractive correction** that would make the exact-marginal couplings converge as random vectors to a single explicit zero-error coupling.

That is now the continuum bottleneck in a sharper form.
