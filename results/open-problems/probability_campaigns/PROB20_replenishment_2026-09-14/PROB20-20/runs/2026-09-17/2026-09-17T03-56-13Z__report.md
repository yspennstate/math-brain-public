> “Is \(Y_{(1)}/n\to_p0\) possible when \(0<\nu<\lambda\)?”

Janson then says Example 18.36 makes this possible on a subsequence, conjectures it is impossible on the full sequence, and states the stronger persistent-condensation alternative: some fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) and some subsequence should satisfy \(Y_{(1)}>\varepsilon n\) w.h.p. The source quantifiers relevant here are therefore:

- one fixed weight sequence \(w=(w_k)_{k\ge0}\);
- an admissible integer sequence \(m_n\) with \(m_n/n\to\lambda\);
- fixed \(0<\nu<\lambda\);
- full-sequence dust is stronger than dust on a selected subsequence.

For a fixed law with radius \(\rho=1\) and \(\Phi(1)<\infty\), normalize

\[
\pi_k=\frac{w_k}{\Phi(1)},\qquad
\nu=E_\pi X,
\]

and let \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) be iid with law \(\pi\). Then the weighted allocation obeys the exact conditioning identity

\[
(Y_1,\dots,Y_n)
\overset d=
(X_1,\dots,X_n)\mid\{S_n=m_n\},
\qquad S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i.
\tag{2.1}
\]

\[
\delta:=\lambda-\nu>0.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Janson's Lemma 18.31 remains the source-side upper control: for every deterministic \(K_n\to\infty\), the conditioned mass in boxes at most \(K_n\) is at least \(\nu n+o_p(n)\), hence the total mass above \(K_n\) is at most \(\delta n+o_p(n)\). In particular,

\[
\limsup_n E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\le\delta.
\tag{2.3}
\]

## 4. Mathematical idea before proof

### B7 — compare the subsequential scaling regimes through two envelope exponents

The preceding q-box identity is more flexible than the same-exponent theorem used it. Suppose one fixed law satisfies

\[
c_-k^{-\beta_+}
\le w_k\le
c_+k^{-\beta_-},
\qquad
2<\beta_-\le\beta_+<\infty.
\tag{4.1}
\]

The notation is intentional: \(\beta_-\) controls the slower-decaying upper envelope, while \(\beta_+\) controls the faster-decaying lower envelope.

The denominator cost of the exact conditioning event is governed by \(\beta_+\), because a deliberately placed macroscopic coordinate may cost as much as \(n^{-\beta_+}\). The entropy of fragmenting that coordinate is governed by \(\beta_-\), because the numerator may use the heaviest allowed fragments. The competition is therefore not one exponent but the gap between two scaling laws.

### B23 — test against the answer the proposer did not want for 18.41

The recovered monotone-cliff law answers Janson 18.41 negatively: monotone weights do **not** force a unique giant. That is exactly the right stress law for 18.39. A useful persistence theorem should survive even when one-giant condensation fails.

The new theorem does: it proves a positive full-sequence macroscopic maximum under (4.1), and for the monotone-cliff exponents it gives \(Y_{(1)}\ge(\delta/2-o(1))n\) w.h.p. on the full admissible sequence.

### B30 — localize the fragmentation count and identify the exact gluing threshold

For q candidate macroscopic fragments, the refined coalescence identity contributes an extra target-count factor \(1/K\). The resulting q-fold fragmentation exponent closes exactly when

\[
\boxed{
\beta_+<2+q(\beta_--2).
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Thus every *finite* polynomial envelope spread admits a finite q. The minimum such q tells us how many macroscopic pieces the coarse envelope permits before one piece is forced to carry a fixed share of the missing mass.

This is the scale-gluing obstruction isolated in this run.

## 6. Main theorem: asymmetric polynomial-envelope persistence

### Theorem 6.1

Let \(w=(w_k)_{k\ge0}\) be one fixed full-support weight sequence with \(w_0>0\). Suppose there are constants

\[
2<\beta_-\le\beta_+<\infty,
\qquad
0<c_-\le c_+<\infty,
\]

such that for every sufficiently large integer \(k\),

\[
\boxed{
 c_-k^{-\beta_+}
 \le w_k\le
 c_+k^{-\beta_-}.
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Let \(m_n/n\to\lambda>\nu\), where \(\nu\) is the boundary mean of this same fixed law. Define

\[
q_*
:=
\left\lfloor
\frac{\beta_+-2}{\beta_--2}
\right\rfloor+1.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Then \(q_*\ge2\), and along the **full admissible sequence**,

\[
\boxed{
P\!\left(
\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge
\frac{\delta-\varepsilon}{q_*-1}
\right)\to1
\quad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
\liminf_{n\to\infty}E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge
\frac{\lambda-\nu}{q_*-1}.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

In particular, the frozen limsup metric satisfies the same lower bound.

If

\[
\beta_+<2\beta_--2,
\tag{6.5}
\]

then \(q_*=2\), and the stronger one-giant conclusion holds:

\[
\boxed{
Y_{(1)}/n\to_p\delta,
\qquad
Y_{(2)}/n\to_p0,
\qquad
E[Y_{(1)}/n]\to\delta.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

### Proof

#### Step 1: boundary normalization and finite mean

From (6.1),

\[
\limsup_{k\to\infty}w_k^{1/k}=1,
\]

so the radius is \(\rho=1\). The upper envelope with \(\beta_->2\) gives

\[
\Phi(1)=\sum_kw_k<\infty,
\qquad
\sum_kkw_k<\infty.
\]

Thus the boundary normalization

\[
\pi_k=w_k/\Phi(1)
\]

is a full-support probability law with finite mean \(\nu\). The same two-sided envelope holds for \(\pi_k\) after changing constants.

No finite-variance assumption is needed.

#### Step 2: polynomial lower bound for the exact conditioning event

By the ordinary law of large numbers, there exists a deterministic sequence \(\eta_n\downarrow0\) such that

\[
P\left(
|S_{n-1}-(n-1)\nu|\le\eta_n n
\right)\to1.
\tag{6.7}
\]

For all large n this event has probability at least \(1/2\). Its interval contains at most \(2n+3\) integers after \(\eta_n\le1\), so there exists an integer \(r_n\) in the interval with

\[
P(S_{n-1}=r_n)\ge\frac{1}{2(2n+3)}.
\tag{6.8}
\]

Set

\[
k_n:=m_n-r_n.
\]

Then

\[
k_n=\delta n+o(n).
\tag{6.9}
\]

Full support and the lower envelope give

\[
\pi_{k_n}\ge C n^{-\beta_+}
\tag{6.10}
\]

for all sufficiently large n. Hence the single explicit route

\[
\{X_1=k_n,\ S_{n-1}=r_n\}
\subseteq\{S_n=m_n\}
\]

gives

\[
\boxed{
P(S_n=m_n)\ge Cn^{-(\beta_++1)}.
}
\tag{6.11}
\]

This is only a denominator lower bound; no local one-big-jump asymptotic is assumed.

#### Step 3: every sublinear power cutoff captures all missing mass

Fix \(0<\gamma<1\) for the moment and set

\[
K_n=\lfloor n^\gamma\rfloor.
\tag{6.12}
\]

Let

\[
Z_i=X_i\mathbf1_{\{X_i\le K_n\}}.
\]

Then

\[
0\le Z_i\le K_n,
\qquad EZ_i\le\nu,
\]

and, without assuming \(EX^2<\infty\),

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_i)
\le EZ_i^2
\le K_nEZ_i
\le\nu K_n.
\tag{6.13}
\]

For every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), Bernstein's inequality therefore yields

\[
P\left(
\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i>(\nu+\varepsilon)n
\right)
\le
\exp\{-c_\varepsilon n/K_n\}
\tag{6.14}
\]

for a constant \(c_\varepsilon>0\).

Condition on \(S_n=m_n\) and use (6.11):

\[
P\left(
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}>(\nu+\varepsilon)n
\right)
\le
C^{-1}n^{\beta_++1}
\exp\{-c_\varepsilon n/K_n\}
\to0,
\tag{6.15}
\]

because \(n/K_n=n^{1-\gamma+o(1)}\) dominates \(\log n\).

Janson's Lemma 18.31(i) gives the opposite conditioned inequality

\[
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}
\ge\nu n+o_p(n).
\tag{6.16}
\]

Combining (6.15)--(6.16),

\[
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}
=\nu n+o_p(n),
\]

hence

\[
\boxed{
T_n:=\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i>K_n\}}
=\delta n+o_p(n).
}
\tag{6.17}
\]

This verifies the high-mass hypothesis (5.4) for any fixed \(\gamma\in(0,1)\).

#### Step 4: asymmetric q-fold convolution bound

Fix an integer \(q\ge2\). For \(c\ge q(K+1)\),

\[
A_{q,K}(c)
=
\sum_{\substack{a_1+\cdots+a_q=c\\a_i>K}}
\prod_{i=1}^q w_{a_i}.
\]

In every composition, some coordinate is at least \(c/q\). Choose such a coordinate in at most q ways. Using the upper envelope for all q factors and then dropping the sum constraint on the remaining q-1 coordinates,

\[
\begin{aligned}
A_{q,K}(c)
&\le
C_q c^{-\beta_-}
\left(\sum_{a>K}a^{-\beta_-}\right)^{q-1}\\
&\le
C_q c^{-\beta_-}
K^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.18}
\]

For large K, every such c is in the lower-envelope range, so

\[
w_c\ge c_-c^{-\beta_+}.
\]

Therefore, uniformly for \(q(K+1)\le c\le m_n=O(n)\),

\[
\frac{A_{q,K}(c)}{w_cw_0^{q-1}}
\le
C_q c^{\beta_+-\beta_-}
K^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}
\le
C_q n^{\beta_+-\beta_-}
K^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
Q_{q,K}(m_n)
\le
C_q n^{\beta_+-\beta_-}
K^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}.
}
\tag{6.19}
\]

#### Step 5: refined target counting and the threshold

Apply the pinned bound (5.3) with \(K=K_n\). Since \(m_n=O(n)\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
E(R_{K_n})_q
&\le
C_q\frac{n^q}{K_n}
 n^{\beta_+-\beta_-}
 K_n^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}\\
&=
C_q n^{q+\beta_+-\beta_-}
K_n^{-D_q},
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.20}
\]

where

\[
D_q
:=1+(q-1)(\beta_--1)
=(q-1)\beta_- -q+2.
\tag{6.21}
\]

With \(K_n=n^{\gamma+o(1)}\),

\[
E(R_{K_n})_q
\le
n^{q+\beta_+-\beta_- -\gamma D_q+o(1)}.
\tag{6.22}
\]

A \(\gamma<1\) making the exponent negative exists exactly when

\[
q+\beta_+-\beta_-<D_q.
\tag{6.23}
\]

Substituting (6.21) and simplifying gives

\[
\boxed{
q(\beta_--2)>\beta_+-2,
}
\tag{6.24}
\]

or equivalently

\[
\boxed{
\beta_+<2+q(\beta_--2).
}
\tag{6.25}
\]

When (6.24) holds, choose

\[
\frac{q+\beta_+-\beta_-}{D_q}<\gamma<1.
\tag{6.26}
\]

Then

\[
E(R_{K_n})_q\to0.
\tag{6.27}
\]

#### Step 6: optimize q

The smallest integer q satisfying (6.24) is exactly

\[
q_*
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{\beta_+-2}{\beta_--2}
\right\rfloor+1.
\]

Apply the persistence consequence (5.6)--(5.7) with \(q=q_*\) and (6.17), obtaining (6.3)--(6.4).

If (6.5) holds, then q=2 already satisfies (6.25), so \(q_*=2\). The q=2 clause (5.8) gives (6.6). This completes the proof. \(\square\)

## 7. Stress test: the fixed monotone-cliff law that defeats unique condensation

The neighboring recovered memo for Janson Problems 18.41/18.42 constructs a single fixed monotone full-support law using dyadic plateaus and sparse cliffs. It proves

\[
e^{-4}k^{-6/\log2}
\le w_k\le
e^3k^{-3/\log2}
\tag{7.1}
\]

and, along its cliff subsequence, proves a second macroscopic box. Therefore it genuinely refutes the claim that monotonicity forces (18.106)--(18.107).

For Theorem 6.1, set

\[
\beta_-:=\frac3{\log2},
\qquad
\beta_+:=\frac6{\log2}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

We now determine \(q_*\) exactly, not by floating-point arithmetic. Let \(L=\log2\). Then

\[
\frac{\beta_+-2}{\beta_--2}
=
\frac{6-2L}{3-2L}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Because \(L>0\),

\[
\frac{6-2L}{3-2L}>2.
\]

Also

\[
\log2=\int_1^2\frac{dx}{x}
<\frac{1+1/2}{2}
=\frac34,
\tag{7.4}
\]

where the strict inequality is the trapezoid bound for the strictly convex function \(1/x\). Hence \(4L<3\), which is equivalent to

\[
\frac{6-2L}{3-2L}<3.
\]

Therefore

\[
2<\frac{\beta_+-2}{\beta_--2}<3,
\qquad
\boxed{q_*=3}.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Theorem 6.1 consequently yields, for this **same fixed law**, for every \(\lambda>\nu\) and every full admissible sequence \(m_n/n\to\lambda\),

\[
\boxed{
P\!\left(
\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\ge\frac{\delta-\varepsilon}{2}
\right)\to1
\quad(\varepsilon>0),
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\liminf_nE\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\ge\frac{\lambda-\nu}{2}.
}
\tag{7.7}
\]

This is exactly the distinction demanded by Problem 18.39: the law can fail **unique** condensation along special scales, yet it still cannot have a vanishing maximum on the full sequence.

The neighboring memo's explicit cliff calculation had supplied \(Y_{(1)}\ge Y_{(2)}\ge\delta n/13\) on its selected cliff subsequence. Equation (7.7) upgrades the stress-law statement to a full-sequence macroscopic lower bound of \(\delta/2\), under the same fixed law.

This does **not** prove that \(\delta/2\) is sharp for that law, and it does not contradict the two-giant cliff subsequence.

## 8. What this rules out, and the exact remaining obstruction

The preceding runs had already ruled out several tempting routes to full-sequence dust:

- smooth regularly varying tails;
- stretched exponential tails;
- concave hazards with a uniform elasticity gap;
- almost-exponential and iterated-log hazards;
- sparse record windows with fixed or vanishing rate gaps;
- one-sided microscopic window bonuses;
- same-exponent two-sided power envelopes.

Theorem 6.1 adds a qualitatively different restriction. Even if a fixed law oscillates across a **finite polynomial exponent band**, the largest box stays macroscopically positive on the full sequence. Quantitatively, if

\[
c_-k^{-\beta_+}\le w_k\le c_+k^{-\beta_-},
\qquad 2<\beta_-\le\beta_+<\infty,
\]

then the number

\[
q_*
=
\left\lfloor\frac{\beta_+-2}{\beta_--2}\right\rfloor+1
\]

bounds the fragmentation complexity strongly enough to force one box to carry at least \(1/(q_*-1)\) of the missing mass asymptotically.

Thus a counterexample to Janson 18.39 cannot merely alternate between polynomial exponents in a fixed finite range above 2. It must evade the q-fold coalescence mechanism at **every finite q**, which requires a much more severe scale dependence: for any useful cutoff, either the effective upper/lower exponent spread must become unbounded, the lower support must develop deep holes, or the q-fold convolution defect must remain large because of highly structured local suppression/entropy.

This is B30's precise gluing obstruction for the next run.

## 9. Counterexample checks against the theorem

Before promoting Theorem 6.1, I checked the obvious transfer failures.

### 9.1 It does not smuggle in a varying law

All constants \(c_-,c_+,\beta_-,\beta_+\) belong to one fixed \(w\). Only \(K_n\) and the conditioning total vary with n.

### 9.2 It does not promote a chosen subsequence

The proof constructs \(K_n=n^\gamma\) for every sufficiently large n and proves (6.3) on the full admissible sequence. The Janson-18.41 cliff subsequence is used only to stress-test the conclusion against failure of unique condensation.

### 9.3 It does not require finite variance

The only concentration input uses the truncated variables \(Z_i\), with the deterministic bound

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_i)\le\nu K_n.
\]

Thus \(2<\beta_-\le3\) is allowed.

### 9.4 It does not imply one giant when q is larger than 2

For q>2 the theorem proves at most q-1 boxes above the chosen cutoff w.h.p. and hence a macroscopic largest box. It does **not** claim \(Y_{(2)}=o_p(n)\). The monotone-cliff example is therefore compatible with the theorem.

### 9.5 It does not reuse the invalid 18.40 rate comparison

The proof uses only: the exact iid-conditioning identity, an explicit polynomial denominator lower bound, Bernstein on a truncated iid sum, Janson Lemma 18.31, and the pinned exact coalescence identity. No F0059/F0060 asymptotic rate necessity appears.
