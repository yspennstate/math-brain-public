## 3. B23 first: try to build the opposite behavior at the finite-mean boundary

The previous dyadic theorem failed below `a=2` only because its explicit conditioning denominator cost `exp(-O(log^2 n))`. It was therefore natural to try to defeat persistence by pushing the dyadic scale weights all the way down to the slowest standard summable profile.

Write generally

\[
 w_{2^r}=2^{-r}u_r.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Then

\[
 \sum_r2^rw_{2^r}=\sum_ru_r.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Thus finite boundary mean asks, up to the harmless normalization by `Phi(1)`, precisely for summability of `u_r`.

The most obvious almost-divergent logarithmic stress family is

\[
 u_r=\frac1{(r+2)(\log(r+2))^{1+\eta}}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

For every `eta>0`, the series in (3.2) converges; at the formal endpoint `eta=0`,

\[
 \sum_r\frac1{(r+2)\log(r+2)}=\infty.
\tag{3.4}
\]

So the apparent next counterexample at `eta=0` exits the source regime: it has `nu=infinity`, not `0<nu<lambda<infinity`.

The substantive question is therefore whether every `eta>0`, however small, can still spread the missing mass into dust. The answer for this fixed dyadic family is **no**: the rest of the report proves the strong persistent-condensation conclusions (0.5)--(0.8).

This is a counterexample-first result in the intended sense. The stress family was chosen to make the previous proof fail. A different mechanism repairs the proof instead of changing the law with `n`.

## 4. Source admissibility of the fixed critical-log law

Fix `eta>0` once and for all and use (0.1)--(0.2).

### Lemma 4.1 — radius, span and finite positive boundary mean

The law has

\[
 \rho=1,
 \qquad
 \omega=\sup\{k:w_k>0\}=\infty,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{span}(w)=1,
 \qquad
 0<\nu<\infty.
\tag{4.1}
\]

#### Proof

Along the support `k=2^r`,

\[
 w_{2^r}^{1/2^r}
 =\exp\left\{
 \frac{-r\log2+\log u_r}{2^r}
 \right\}\longrightarrow1,
\]

so the radius is one. The support is unbounded. Since `2^0=1` and `u_0>0`, we have `w_1>0`, hence span one.

Moreover,

\[
 \Phi(1)=1+\sum_{r\ge0}2^{-r}u_r<\infty,
\tag{4.2}
\]

and

\[
 \Phi'(1)
 =\sum_{r\ge0}2^rw_{2^r}
 =\sum_{r\ge0}u_r
 <\infty
\tag{4.3}
\]

by the integral test, because `eta>0`. The derivative is positive, so

\[
 \nu=\Phi'(1)/\Phi(1)\in(0,\infty).
\]

This proves (4.1). `square`

Because every positive integer has a binary expansion using `O(log m)` supported occupancies and there are `n` boxes, every integer sequence `m_n=O(n)` is admissible for all sufficiently large `n`.

## 5. B7: use the source's own slow cutoff instead of paying a conditioning denominator

\[
 \Omega_n\to\infty

such that every diverging cutoff below `Omega_n` has the exact missing-mass split.

Define

\[
 M_n:=\min\{\Omega_n,\sqrt n\},
 \qquad
 \ell_n:=\lfloor\log_2M_n\rfloor,
 \qquad
 K_n:=2^{\ell_n}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Then

\[
 K_n\to\infty,

 K_n\le\Omega_n,
 \qquad
 K_n\le\sqrt n=o(n).

 \boxed{
 C_n:=\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i>K_n\}}

Because the only positive supported occupancies are powers of two, if

No rate for `Omega_n` is assumed. It may grow arbitrarily slowly. This is exactly why the next step must not require a prescribed quantitative cutoff.

## 6. B30: separate a negligible deterministic baseline from the excess mass

The placement activity of a dyadic box at scale `r` is

\[
 \lambda_{n,r}
 :=\frac{n w_{2^r}}{w_0}
 =n2^{-r}u_r.
\tag{6.1}
\]

If we coalesce arbitrary boxes directly, small scales may have large activities and the inverse fiber may carry too much entropy. Instead we reserve a deterministic baseline number of boxes at every scale and coalesce only the excess above that baseline.

Choose

\[
 A_r:=(\log(r+2))^{\eta/2}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Then `A_r` is nondecreasing, `A_r->infinity`, and

\[
 \sum_{r\ge0}A_ru_r
 =\sum_{r\ge0}
 \frac1{(r+2)(\log(r+2))^{1+\eta/2}}
 <\infty.
\tag{6.3}
\]

For every tail scale `r>=r_(0,n)`, define the deterministic baseline

\[
 b_{n,r}:=\lfloor A_r\lambda_{n,r}\rfloor
\tag{6.4}
\]

and the random excess count

\[
 e_{n,r}:=(N_{2^r}-b_{n,r})_+.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Define baseline capacity and excess mass

\[
 B_n:=\sum_{r\ge r_{0,n}}2^rb_{n,r},
 \qquad
 E_n:=\sum_{r\ge r_{0,n}}2^re_{n,r}.
\tag{6.6}
\]

### Lemma 6.1 — the baseline mass is negligible

\[
 B_n=o(n).
\tag{6.7}
\]

#### Proof

By (6.1) and (6.4),

\[
 B_n
 \le\sum_{r\ge r_{0,n}}2^rA_r\lambda_{n,r}
 =n\sum_{r\ge r_{0,n}}A_ru_r.
\]

Since `r_(0,n)->infinity` and (6.3) is summable, the tail sum tends to zero. `square`

### Lemma 6.2 — excess mass still carries all missing mass asymptotically

\[
 \boxed{E_n=\delta n+o_p(n).}
\tag{6.8}
\]

#### Proof

For every scale,

\[
 N_{2^r}-(N_{2^r}-b_{n,r})_+
 =\min\{N_{2^r},b_{n,r}\}.
\]

Therefore

\[
 0\le C_n-E_n
 \le\sum_{r\ge r_{0,n}}2^rb_{n,r}
 =B_n=o(n).
\]

Combine with (5.4). `square`

This is the key localization. No matter how slowly the source cutoff grows, only `o(n)` tail mass is protected as baseline; a linear excess mass remains available for exact coalescence.

## 7. Top bit of the excess mass and a deterministic coalescence map

For a positive integer `E`, write

\[
 P(E):=2^{\lfloor\log_2E\rfloor}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Thus

\[
 P(E)\le E<2P(E),
 \qquad
 P(E)>E/2.
\tag{7.2}
\]

On the high-probability event

\[
 \mathcal G_n:=\left\{\frac\delta2n\le E_n\le\frac{3\delta}{2}n\right\},
\tag{7.3}
\]

which has probability tending to one by (6.8), put

\[
 P=P(E_n)=2^j.
\tag{7.4}
\]

Then `P=Theta(n)` uniformly on `G_n`, and consequently

\[
 j=\log_2n+O(1).
\tag{7.5}
\]

### Lemma 7.1 — the top scale has zero deterministic baseline

Uniformly on `G_n`, for all sufficiently large `n`,

\[
 b_{n,j}=0.
\tag{7.6}
\]

#### Proof

Since `P=Theta(n)`, `n/P` is bounded. Therefore

\[
 A_j\lambda_{n,j}
 =\frac nP A_ju_j
 =O\left(
 \frac1{(j+2)(\log(j+2))^{1+\eta/2}}
 \right)
 \to0.
\]

Hence its floor is zero. `square`

Now restrict to a source histogram in `G_n` with **no box of size `P`**. By (7.6), its excess count at scale `j` is zero. Also no excess box can exceed `P`, because a single such box would have size at least `2P>E_n`.

The excess multiset therefore consists entirely of powers of two below `P`, with total mass `E_n`, whose top binary bit is exactly `P`.

### Lemma 7.2 — a submultiset of excess boxes sums exactly to `P`

There exist integers `d_r`, `r_(0,n)<=r<j`, such that

\[
 0\le d_r\le e_{n,r},
 \qquad
 \sum_{r<j}d_r2^r=P.
\tag{7.7}
\]

#### Proof

Pair equal dyadic excess boxes repeatedly and carry each pair to the next dyadic scale. The final carried representation is the binary expansion of `E_n`. Since `P` is its top bit, one terminal carried `P` occurs. Trace its ancestry backward. Those original excess boxes form the desired submultiset. `square`

Fix once and for all a deterministic carrying/tie-breaking rule so that one such vector `d=(d_r)` is chosen for every bad source histogram.

Let

\[
 D:=\sum_rd_r.
\tag{7.8}
\]

Map the source histogram to a target histogram by replacing the selected `D` boxes by

- one box of size `P`, and
- `D-1` zero boxes.

Total mass and the number of coordinates are preserved.

More importantly, the **excess mass itself is preserved**. At selected lower scales, `d_r<=e_(n,r)` means the post-map count remains at least the baseline `b_(n,r)`, so the excess decreases by exactly `d_r`. At scale `j`, the baseline is zero and the newly created `P` contributes exactly `P` excess mass. Thus

\[
 E_n(\text{target})=E_n(\text{source}).
\tag{7.9}
\]

Hence `P(E_n)` is also preserved. This makes the source-to-target map fiberwise compatible with the random phase rather than imposing a deterministic phase by hand.

## 8. Exact histogram ratio with the baseline absorbing placement entropy

For a supported allocation histogram `N=(N_k)`, let

\[
 W(N)
 :=\frac{n!}{\prod_kN_k!}\prod_kw_k^{N_k}
\tag{8.1}
\]

be its aggregate contribution to the conditioned partition function.

Fix one bad source `N` and its target `T` under Section 7. Since the source has no `P` box, the target has exactly one new `P` box. At lower selected scales,

\[
 T_{2^r}=N_{2^r}-d_r,
\]

and

\[
 T_0=N_0+D-1.
\]

A direct factorial calculation gives

\[
 \frac{W(N)}{W(T)}
 =\frac{(N_0+D-1)!}{N_0!}
 \prod_r\frac{(N_{2^r}-d_r)!}{N_{2^r}!}
 \frac{\prod_rw_{2^r}^{d_r}}{w_Pw_0^{D-1}}.
\tag{8.2}
\]

Because `d_r<=e_(n,r)`,

\[
 N_{2^r}-d_r\ge b_{n,r}.
\tag{8.3}
\]

Therefore every factor in the falling factorial `(N_(2^r))_(d_r)` is at least `b_(n,r)+1`, while the zero-box factorial ratio is at most `n^(D-1)`. Consequently

\[
 \frac{W(N)}{W(T)}
 \le
 \frac1{\lambda_{n,j}}
 \prod_{r<j}
 \left(
 \frac{\lambda_{n,r}}{b_{n,r}+1}
 \right)^{d_r}.
\tag{8.4}
\]

Define the **post-baseline activity**

\[
 q_{n,r}:=\frac{\lambda_{n,r}}{b_{n,r}+1}.
\tag{8.5}
\]

Since `b_(n,r)=floor(A_r lambda_(n,r))`,

\[
 \boxed{
 q_{n,r}\le\min\{\lambda_{n,r},A_r^{-1}\}.
 }
\tag{8.6}
\]

The exact inverse-fiber problem is therefore reduced to a weighted dyadic partition problem with tiny activities. No informal `n^D` placement factor remains outside the estimate.

For a fixed target histogram, summing over every possible inverse dyadic decomposition only enlarges its true deterministic inverse fiber. Put

\[
 F_{n,j}
 :=\sum_{\substack{d_r\ge0,\ r_{0,n}\le r<j\\
                    \sum_rd_r2^r=2^j}}
 \prod_{r<j}q_{n,r}^{d_r}.
\tag{8.7}
\]

Then the full bad-source weight mapping to a fixed target is at most

\[
 \frac{F_{n,j}}{\lambda_{n,j}}W(T).
\tag{8.8}
\]

The remaining task is to prove that this ratio tends to zero uniformly for every linear-scale `P=2^j`.

## 9. Binary-tree domination of the entire inverse fragmentation fiber

Define recursively, beginning at `r_(0,n)`,

\[
 T_{n,r_{0,n}}:=q_{n,r_{0,n}},
\tag{9.1}
\]

and

\[
 T_{n,r}:=q_{n,r}+T_{n,r-1}^2
 \qquad(r>r_{0,n}).
\tag{9.2}
\]

Interpret `T_(n,r)` as the total weight of all ordered full binary decomposition trees of mass `2^r`: a node may stop as one leaf of mass `2^r`, contributing `q_(n,r)`, or split into two ordered children of mass `2^(r-1)`, contributing `T_(n,r-1)^2`.

### Lemma 9.1 — every dyadic histogram partition has a binary-tree representation

If nonnegative integers `(d_s)_(r0<=s<=r)` satisfy

\[
 \sum_sd_s2^s=2^r,
\]

then there exists a full binary decomposition tree with root mass `2^r` whose leaf histogram is exactly `(d_s)`.

#### Proof

Apply dyadic carrying to the multiset of leaves. Since the total is `2^r`, carrying terminates at one root `2^r`. Reverse the carrying operations. Every reversed carry splits one dyadic node into two equal children. The resulting rooted binary tree has the original multiset as its leaves. `square`

The tree may not be unique, which only helps an upper bound. Since (8.7) forbids a leaf at scale `j`, the root `2^j` is forced to split at least once. Therefore

\[
 \boxed{
 F_{n,j}\le T_{n,j-1}^2.
 }
\tag{9.3}
\]

This is the new replacement for the preceding report's one-shot exponential coefficient estimate.

## 10. Uniform collapse of the binary-tree recursion for the critical-log profile

The first input is the baseline bound. From (8.6) and monotonicity of `A_r`, define

\[
 \varepsilon_n:=A_{r_{0,n}}^{-1}.
\tag{10.1}

Since `r_(0,n)->infinity`,

\[
 \varepsilon_n\to0,
\tag{10.2}

and for all tail scales

\[
 q_{n,r}\le\varepsilon_n.
\tag{10.3}

### Lemma 10.1 — crude uniform recursion bound

For all sufficiently large `n`,

\[
 T_{n,r}\le2\varepsilon_n
 \qquad(r\ge r_{0,n}).
\tag{10.4}
\]

#### Proof

For large `n`, `epsilon_n<=1/4`. The base case follows from (10.3). If `T_(n,r-1)<=2epsilon_n`, then

\[
 T_{n,r}
 \le\varepsilon_n+4\varepsilon_n^2
 \le2\varepsilon_n.
\]

Induction proves (10.4). `square`

The crude bound tends to zero but has no rate, so it cannot by itself be compared with the tiny top activity `u_j`. We now use only the final `O(log j)` recursion levels, where the explicit scale profile is available.

Let

\[
 L_j:=\left\lfloor\frac12\log_2j\right\rfloor,
 \qquad
 r_*:=j-L_j-1.
\tag{10.5}
\]

Because `j=log_2n+O(1)`, while `r_(0,n)<=\tfrac12\log_2n+1`, we have

\[
 r_*\ge r_{0,n}
\tag{10.6}
\]

for all sufficiently large `n` on `G_n`.

Set

\[
 x_0:=T_{n,r_*},
 \qquad
 x_t:=T_{n,r_*+t},
 \qquad
 a_t:=q_{n,r_*+t},
 \qquad 1\le t\le L_j.
\tag{10.7}
\]

Then

\[
 x_t=a_t+x_{t-1}^2,
\tag{10.8}
\]

and (10.4) gives

\[
 x_0\le2\varepsilon_n.
\tag{10.9}
\]

We need one elementary unrolling inequality.

### Lemma 10.2 — max-form unrolling of a quadratic recursion

If `x_t<=a_t+x_(t-1)^2` with all quantities nonnegative, then

\[
 x_t\le
 \max\left\{
 \frac12(4a_{t-h})^{2^h}:0\le h\le t-1;
 \ \frac12(2x_0)^{2^t}
 \right\}.
\tag{10.10}
\]

#### Proof

Define `y_0=x_0` and

\[
 y_t:=2\max\{a_t,y_{t-1}^2\}.
\]

Since `a+b<=2 max(a,b)`, induction gives `x_t<=y_t`. Expanding the max recursion shows that a term first entering as `a_(t-h)` acquires coefficient

\[
 2^{2^{h+1}-1}
 =\frac12 4^{2^h},
\]

while the initial term acquires coefficient

\[
 2^{2^t-1}
 =\frac12 2^{2^t}.
\]

This is exactly (10.10). `square`

We apply (10.10) at `t=L_j`.

For `0<=h<=L_j-1`, the relevant scale is `r=j-1-h`. Since `P=2^j=Theta(n)`,

\[
 \lambda_{n,j-1-h}
 =\frac{n}{P}2^{h+1}u_{j-1-h}.
\tag{10.11}
\]

Uniformly for `h<=L_j`, we have `h=o(j)` and therefore

\[
 u_{j-1-h}\asymp u_j.
\tag{10.12}
\]

Thus, with a constant depending only on the fixed linear window in `G_n` and on `eta`,

\[
 a_{L_j-h}=q_{n,j-1-h}
 \le C2^hu_j.
\tag{10.13}
\]

The `h=0` term in (10.10) is therefore `O(u_j)`.

For `h>=1`, put `t=2^h`. Because `t<=2^{L_j}<=sqrt j` and

\[
 u_j\asymp\frac1{j(\log j)^{1+\eta}},
\tag{10.14}
\]

we have, uniformly in this range,

\[
 Ctu_j\le\frac{C'}{\sqrt j(\log j)^{1+\eta}}\to0.
\tag{10.15}
\]

Moreover the function

\[
 t\mapsto t\log(Ctu_j)-\log u_j
\]

is decreasing for `2<=t<=sqrt j` once `j` is large, because its derivative is `log(Ctu_j)+1<0`. Hence the largest ratio of the powered terms to `u_j` occurs at `t=2`, where it is `O(u_j)`. Consequently

\[
 (C2^hu_j)^{2^h}=o(u_j)
\tag{10.16}
\]

uniformly for `1<=h<=L_j-1`.

Finally, the ancestral term in (10.10) is negligible without any quantitative rate for `epsilon_n`. By (10.2), eventually `4epsilon_n<=1/2`, and by (10.9),

\[
 \frac12(2x_0)^{2^{L_j}}
 \le\frac12(4\varepsilon_n)^{2^{L_j}}
 \le\frac12\exp(-c\sqrt j)
 =o(u_j).
\tag{10.17}
\]

Combining (10.10), (10.13), (10.16), and (10.17) gives the decisive estimate

\[
 \boxed{
 T_{n,j-1}=O(u_j)
 }
\tag{10.18}

uniformly for every `P=2^j` in the fixed positive linear window generated by `G_n`.

Since

\[
 \lambda_{n,j}
 =\frac nP u_j
 =\Theta(u_j),

(9.3) and (10.18) imply

 }
\tag{10.20}

This is the exact energy-versus-entropy inequality needed by the coalescence map.

## 12. Phase-selected subsequence reaches the frozen metric ceiling

\tag{12.5}

 \limsup_nE[Y_{(1)}/n]\le\delta.

The subsequence (12.9) gives the reverse inequality. Hence

 b(w,\lambda)=\limsup_nE[Y_{(1)}/n]

This is the maximal possible value of the frozen metric for this fixed law.
