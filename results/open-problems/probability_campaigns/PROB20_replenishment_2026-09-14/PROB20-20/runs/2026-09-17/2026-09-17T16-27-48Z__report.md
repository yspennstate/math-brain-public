## 2. Exact source statement and hypotheses

Primary source rechecked 2026-09-17:

Svante Janson, *Simply generated trees, conditioned Galton--Watson trees, random allocations and condensation*, Probability Surveys 9 (2012), 103--252, arXiv:1112.0510v1, Section 18.6, Problem 18.39, printed page 122.

The currently exposed arXiv entry remains v1, submitted 2 December 2011.

Problem 18.39 asks whether

\[
Y_{(1)}/n\xrightarrow p0
\tag{2.1}
\]

can occur for a **single fixed weight sequence** when

\[
0<\nu<\lambda,
\qquad
m_n/n\to\lambda.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Example 18.36 shows that vanishing can occur on a subsequence. Janson conjectures that it cannot occur on the full sequence and states the slightly stronger expected alternative: there should exist some fixed `epsilon>0` and some subsequence along which

\[
Y_{(1)}\ge \epsilon n
\tag{2.3}
\]

with high probability. Problem 18.40 is separately the `nu=0` question.

The source cutoff theorem used below is Lemma 18.31. For `w_0>0`, unbounded support, `nu<lambda<infinity`, and `m_n/n->lambda`:

1. for every deterministic `K_n->infinity`, the mass above `K_n` is at most
   \[
   (\lambda-\nu)n+o_p(n);
   \tag{2.4}
   \]
2. there is a deterministic source-produced `Omega_n->infinity` such that whenever `K_n->infinity` and `K_n<=Omega_n`, the mass above `K_n` is exactly
   \[
   (\lambda-\nu)n+o_p(n).
   \tag{2.5}
   \]

This arbitrary-cutoff versus source-slow-cutoff distinction is essential and is preserved exactly.

Put

\[
\delta:=\lambda-\nu>0.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Taking any deterministic `K_n->infinity` with `K_n=o(n)` in (2.4) gives

\[
\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\le \delta+o_p(1),
\tag{2.7}
\]

because either `Y_(1)<=K_n=o(n)` or it is part of the mass above `K_n`. Since `0<=Y_(1)/n<=m_n/n=O(1)`,

\[
\limsup_n E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\le\delta.
\tag{2.8}
\]

Thus `delta` is the ceiling of the frozen limsup metric.

## 3. B20 first — attack the previous algorithm exactly where it fails

The previous rapid-radix theorem bounded the inverse fragmentation recursion by a crude scalar envelope

\[
T_{n,r}\le2\varepsilon_n,
\qquad \varepsilon_n\to0.
\tag{3.1}
\]

At a root of arity `c_j`, this gave

\[
F_{n,j}\le(2\varepsilon_n)^{c_j}.
\tag{3.2}
\]

To compare with a target of activity `Lambda_(n,j)`, the old proof therefore needed the root arity to be large enough that (3.2) beat that possibly tiny target. This is precisely why condition (1.1) appeared.

A counterexample-first stress test is the borderline unbounded radix

\[
\boxed{
c_r:=\max\{2,\lceil\log_2(r+3)\rceil\}.}
\tag{3.3}
\]

For the critical-log activity

\[
u_r:=\frac1{(r+2)(\log(r+2))^{1+\eta}},
\qquad \eta>0,
\tag{3.4}
\]

we have

\[
\frac{2^{-c_r}}{u_r}\asymp(\log r)^{1+\eta},
\tag{3.5}
\]

up to bounded factors. Hence the ratio in the previous theorem does **not** tend to zero; it diverges. At the same time `c_r->infinity`, so the older bounded-radix theorem does not apply either.

The attempted escape nevertheless fails. The flaw in the old algorithm is not mathematical condensation but information loss: replacing every post-baseline activity by the single number `epsilon_n` throws away the fact that near the root the actual activity `Lambda_(n,r)` may be much smaller than `epsilon_n`.

The fix is the two-scale envelope

\[
\boxed{M_{n,r}:=\min\{\varepsilon_n,\Lambda_{n,r}\}.}
\tag{3.6}
\]

The rest of the run proves that this envelope is stable under the exact fragmentation recursion whenever adjacent activity coefficients have no violent downward cliffs. No restriction at all on the support radices is then needed.

## 4. Fixed-law class

Let

\[
s_0:=1,
\qquad
s_r:=c_rs_{r-1},
\qquad
c_r\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
\tag{4.1}
\]

with **arbitrary** integer radices `c_r`; they may be bounded, unbounded, oscillatory, logarithmic, factorial-sized, or larger.

Let `(u_r)` be a fixed positive sequence satisfying

\[
\sum_{r\ge0}u_r<\infty,
\tag{4.2}
\]

and suppose that for some finite `R>=1` and all sufficiently large `r`,

\[
\boxed{
1\le\frac{u_{r-1}}{u_r}\le R.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Thus the activities are eventually nonincreasing and adjacent downward cliffs are uniformly bounded. No regular variation is assumed, and no bound is imposed on the support ratios.

Define one fixed weight sequence

\[
\boxed{
w_0:=1,
\qquad
w_{s_r}:=\frac{u_r}{s_r}\quad(r\ge0),
\qquad
w_k:=0\quad(k\ge1,\ k\notin\{s_r:r\ge0\}).
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

Because `s_0=1`, we have `w_1=u_0>0`, so the span is one.

### Lemma 4.1 — source admissibility

The radius of convergence of the generating function is `rho=1`, and

\[
0<\nu
=\frac{\sum_{r\ge0}u_r}
       {1+\sum_{r\ge0}u_r/s_r}
<\infty.
\tag{4.5}
\]

#### Proof

Condition (4.3) implies, for large `r`,

\[
u_r\ge C R^{-r}
\tag{4.6}
\]

for a fixed `C>0`. Also `s_r>=2^r`. Hence

\[
\frac{|\log u_r|}{s_r}\to0.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Since `s_r->infinity`, also `log s_r/s_r->0`. Therefore

\[
(w_{s_r})^{1/s_r}
=\exp\left(\frac{\log u_r-\log s_r}{s_r}\right)
\to1.
\tag{4.8}
\]

The off-support weights are zero, so the root-test limsup is one and `rho=1`.

Moreover

\[
\Phi(1)=1+\sum_r\frac{u_r}{s_r}<\infty,
\qquad
\Phi'(1)=\sum_ru_r<\infty.
\tag{4.9}
\]

Thus (4.5) follows from `nu=Psi(1)=Phi'(1)/Phi(1)`. Positivity follows from `u_0>0`. `square`

### Lemma 4.2 — a slowly growing summable baseline always exists

There is a deterministic nondecreasing sequence `A_r->infinity` such that

\[
\boxed{
\sum_r A_ru_r<\infty.
}
\tag{4.10}
\]

Consequently

\[
A_ru_r\to0.
\tag{4.11}
\]

#### Proof

Since the tail sums of `(u_r)` tend to zero, choose increasing indices `R_l` so that

\[
\sum_{r\ge R_l}u_r\le2^{-2l}.
\tag{4.12}
\]

Set `A_r=2^l` on `R_l<=r<R_(l+1)` and define it positively on the finite initial segment. Then

\[
\sum_rA_ru_r
\le C+\sum_{l\ge1}2^l\sum_{r\ge R_l}u_r
\le C+\sum_{l\ge1}2^{-l}<\infty.
\tag{4.13}
\]

The terms of a convergent positive series tend to zero, giving (4.11). `square`

## 5. Main theorem

### Theorem 5.1 — arbitrary-radix locally-regular sparse chains have a maximal limsup condensate

For the fixed weight law (4.4), assume (4.2)--(4.3). Let `lambda>nu` and let **any already-fixed admissible sequence** satisfy

\[
\frac{m_n}{n}\to\lambda.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Then there exists a deterministic subsequence `n_l` such that

\[
\boxed{
\frac{Y_{(1)}}{n_l}\xrightarrow p\lambda-\nu,
\qquad
E\frac{Y_{(1)}}{n_l}\to\lambda-\nu.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Consequently the frozen metric reaches its largest possible value:

\[
\boxed{
b(w,\lambda):=\limsup_nE[Y_{(1)}/n]=\lambda-\nu.}
\tag{5.3}
\]

The statement is a subsequence/limsup theorem. It does **not** assert full-sequence one-box condensation when the radices are unbounded.

The proof occupies Sections 6--11.

## 6. Source-valid missing mass and a negligible baseline

Let `Omega_n` be the deterministic sequence supplied by Janson Lemma 18.31(ii). Put

\[
H_n:=\min\{\Omega_n,\sqrt n\}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Choose `K_n` to be the largest supported scale `s_r` with `s_r<=H_n`. Then

\[
K_n\to\infty,
\qquad
K_n\le\Omega_n,
\qquad
K_n=o(n).
\tag{6.2}
\]

Let `r_n` be the first support index strictly above `K_n`. By the source lemma, under the conditioned allocation,

\[
C_n:=\sum_{r\ge r_n}s_rN_{s_r}
=\delta n+o_p(n).
\tag{6.3}
\]

Fix the baseline sequence from Lemma 4.2 and define

\[
\Lambda_{n,r}:=nw_{s_r}=\frac n{s_r}u_r,
\qquad
b_{n,r}:=\lfloor A_r\Lambda_{n,r}\rfloor,
\tag{6.4}
\]

\[
e_{n,r}:=(N_{s_r}-b_{n,r})_+.
\tag{6.5}
\]

The protected baseline mass is

\[
B_n:=\sum_{r\ge r_n}s_rb_{n,r}.
\tag{6.6}
\]

Using (6.4),

\[
B_n
\le n\sum_{r\ge r_n}A_ru_r
=o(n),
\tag{6.7}
\]

because `r_n->infinity` and the series in (4.10) converges.

Define the tail excess mass

\[
E_n:=\sum_{r\ge r_n}s_re_{n,r}.
\tag{6.8}
\]

Since

\[
0\le C_n-E_n\le B_n=o(n),
\tag{6.9}
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{E_n=\delta n+o_p(n).}
\tag{6.10}
\]

No fast-cutoff equality is used.

## 7. Exact source-to-target histogram comparison

Suppose `s_j` is the top supported scale of the excess mass `E_n`, and suppose there is no actual `s_j` box.

Repeated mixed-radix carrying

\[
c_{r+1}\text{ copies of }s_r
\longrightarrow
1\text{ copy of }s_{r+1}
\tag{7.1}
\]

converts the excess multiset to canonical mixed-radix form. Since the top canonical digit is at `s_j`, trace the ancestry of one terminal `s_j` backward. This selects integers `d_r`, `r_n<=r<j`, such that

\[
0\le d_r\le e_{n,r},
\qquad
\sum_{r<j}d_rs_r=s_j.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Let `D=sum d_r`. Replace those `D` lower boxes by one `s_j` box and `D-1` zero boxes. Total mass and box count are unchanged.

For a histogram `N`, its aggregate conditioned partition-function weight is

\[
W(N):=\frac{n!}{\prod_kN_k!}\prod_kw_k^{N_k}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

If the top baseline is zero, the exact factorial ratio gives

\[
\frac{W(\mathrm{source})}{W(\mathrm{target})}
\le
\frac1{\Lambda_{n,j}}
\prod_{r<j}
\left(
\frac{\Lambda_{n,r}}{b_{n,r}+1}
\right)^{d_r}.
\tag{7.4}
\]

Indeed, the `D-1` new zero boxes and the new root box contribute at most `n^D`; each removed excess box supplies a denominator at least `b_(n,r)+1`; and `n^D prod w_(s_r)^(d_r)/w_(s_j)` is exactly the right normalization in (7.4).

Put

\[
q_{n,r}:=\frac{\Lambda_{n,r}}{b_{n,r}+1}.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
q_{n,r}\le\min\{\Lambda_{n,r},A_r^{-1}\}.
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

Summing every possible inverse lower-scale decomposition only enlarges the true fiber. Define

\[
F_{n,j}:=
\sum_{\substack{d_r\ge0,\ r_n\le r<j\\
                 \sum_rd_rs_r=s_j}}
\prod_{r<j}q_{n,r}^{d_r}.
\tag{7.7}
\]

For a fixed target, all bad source histograms mapping to it have total weight at most

\[
\frac{F_{n,j}}{\Lambda_{n,j}}W(\mathrm{target}).
\tag{7.8}
\]

## 8. Fragmentation recursion

Define

\[
T_{n,r_n}:=q_{n,r_n}
\tag{8.1}
\]

and recursively for `r>r_n`,

\[
T_{n,r}:=q_{n,r}+T_{n,r-1}^{c_r}.
\tag{8.2}
\]

Every supported partition of `s_j` into scales at least `s_(r_n)` can be represented by reversing mixed-radix carries. A partition counted by `F_(n,j)` has no root leaf, so the root must split into `c_j` children. Therefore

\[
\boxed{F_{n,j}\le T_{n,j-1}^{c_j}.}
\tag{8.3}
\]

This is the same exact combinatorial recursion as in the preceding report. The new mathematics is the sharper invariant used to bound it.

## 9. B12 — the two-scale invariant

Set

\[
\varepsilon_n:=A_{r_n}^{-1}\to0.
\tag{9.1}
\]

Since `(A_r)` is nondecreasing, (7.6) gives for every `r>=r_n`

\[
q_{n,r}\le\min\{\Lambda_{n,r},\varepsilon_n\}.
\tag{9.2}
\]

Define

\[
\boxed{M_{n,r}:=\min\{\Lambda_{n,r},\varepsilon_n\}.}
\tag{9.3}
\]

For all sufficiently large `n`, `r_n` is beyond the finite exceptional range in (4.3). Hence

\[
\frac{\Lambda_{n,r-1}}{\Lambda_{n,r}}
=c_r\frac{u_{r-1}}{u_r},
\tag{9.4}
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
c_r
\le
\frac{\Lambda_{n,r-1}}{\Lambda_{n,r}}
\le Rc_r.
}
\tag{9.5}
\]

### Lemma 9.1 — invariant barrier

For all sufficiently large `n`, uniformly for every `r>=r_n`,

\[
\boxed{T_{n,r}\le2M_{n,r}.}
\tag{9.6}
\]

#### Proof

The base case follows from `T_(n,r_n)=q_(n,r_n)<=M_(n,r_n)`.

Assume inductively that

\[
T_{n,r-1}\le2M_{n,r-1}.
\tag{9.7}
\]

Write `c=c_r`. We show

\[
(2M_{n,r-1})^c\le M_{n,r}
\tag{9.8}
\]

for all large `n`.

**Case 1: `Lambda_(n,r-1)<=epsilon_n`.** Then `M_(n,r-1)=Lambda_(n,r-1)`. By the lower bound in (9.5),

\[
\Lambda_{n,r}\le\frac{\Lambda_{n,r-1}}{c}\le\varepsilon_n,
\tag{9.9}
\]

so `M_(n,r)=Lambda_(n,r)`. By the upper bound in (9.5),

\[
\frac{(2M_{n,r-1})^c}{M_{n,r}}
\le
Rc\,2^c\Lambda_{n,r-1}^{c-1}
\le
Rc\,2^c\varepsilon_n^{c-1}.
\tag{9.10}
\]

**Case 2: `Lambda_(n,r-1)>epsilon_n`.** Then `M_(n,r-1)=epsilon_n`.

If `Lambda_(n,r)>=epsilon_n`, then

\[
\frac{(2M_{n,r-1})^c}{M_{n,r}}
=2^c\varepsilon_n^{c-1}.
\tag{9.11}
\]

If instead `Lambda_(n,r)<epsilon_n`, then (9.5) yields

\[
\Lambda_{n,r}
\ge\frac{\Lambda_{n,r-1}}{Rc}
>\frac{\varepsilon_n}{Rc},
\tag{9.12}
\]

and therefore

\[
\frac{(2M_{n,r-1})^c}{M_{n,r}}
< Rc\,2^c\varepsilon_n^{c-1}.
\tag{9.13}
\]

Thus in every case the ratio in (9.8) is at most

\[
Rc\,2^c\varepsilon_n^{c-1}.
\tag{9.14}
\]

For `epsilon<=1/6`, the sequence

\[
a_c:=c2^c\varepsilon^{c-1},\qquad c\ge2,
\tag{9.15}
\]

is decreasing, because

\[
\frac{a_{c+1}}{a_c}
=2\varepsilon\frac{c+1}{c}
\le3\varepsilon\le\frac12.
\tag{9.16}
\]

Hence

\[
\sup_{c\ge2}Rc2^c\varepsilon^{c-1}
=8R\varepsilon.
\tag{9.17}
\]

Since `epsilon_n->0`, eventually `8R epsilon_n<=1`, proving (9.8). Finally

\[
T_{n,r}
=q_{n,r}+T_{n,r-1}^c
\le M_{n,r}+M_{n,r}
=2M_{n,r}.
\tag{9.18}
\]

This closes the induction. `square`

The important point is that (9.17) is uniform over **all** radices `c>=2`.

## 10. B24 — the extremal radix is two

The same calculation at the root gives the exact estimate needed for the fiber.

### Lemma 10.1 — uniform root-collapse estimate

For all sufficiently large `n`, every `j>r_n` in the locally regular range satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\frac{F_{n,j}}{\Lambda_{n,j}}
\le8R\varepsilon_n.
}
\tag{10.1}
\]

provided the root baseline is zero.

#### Proof

By (8.3) and Lemma 9.1,

\[
\frac{F_{n,j}}{\Lambda_{n,j}}
\le
\frac{(2M_{n,j-1})^{c_j}}{\Lambda_{n,j}}.
\tag{10.2}
\]

The same three cases used in (9.10)--(9.13), now with `r=j`, give

\[
\frac{(2M_{n,j-1})^{c_j}}{\Lambda_{n,j}}
\le
Rc_j2^{c_j}\varepsilon_n^{c_j-1}
\le8R\varepsilon_n.
\tag{10.3}
\]

The last inequality is exactly (9.17). `square`

Thus increasing a support radix cannot create the obstruction in this locally regular class. Once `epsilon_n` is small, the largest inverse-fragmentation error occurs at `c_j=2`. Large radices only raise a small quantity to a higher power.

This is the opposite of what the previous crude estimate suggested.

## 11. Phase selection and proof of Theorem 5.1

Choose a deterministic sequence `theta_l downarrow0` with `theta_l<delta/4`.

By (6.10), for every `l` there is an `N_l` such that for all `n>=N_l`,

\[
P\left(
\left|\frac{E_n}{n}-\delta\right|>\frac{\theta_l}{4}
\right)<\theta_l.
\tag{11.1}
\]

Choose any increasing support indices `j_l` large enough that

\[
n_l:=\left\lceil\frac{s_{j_l}}{\delta-\theta_l}\right\rceil
\ge N_l
\tag{11.2}
\]

and `j_l` is beyond the finite exceptional range of (4.3). Since `s_j->infinity`, this is always possible.

On the complement of the event in (11.1),

\[
(\delta-\theta_l/4)n_l
\le E_{n_l}
\le(\delta+\theta_l/4)n_l.
\tag{11.3}
\]

By (11.2), for all sufficiently large `l`,

\[
s_{j_l}<E_{n_l}<2s_{j_l}\le s_{j_l+1}.
\tag{11.4}
\]

Therefore the top supported digit of the **actual excess mass** is exactly `s_(j_l)` with probability tending to one. Also

\[
\frac{s_{j_l}}{n_l}\to\delta.
\tag{11.5}
\]

We must verify the zero-baseline condition at the root. From (6.4) and (11.5),

\[
A_{j_l}\Lambda_{n_l,j_l}
=A_{j_l}\frac{n_l}{s_{j_l}}u_{j_l}
\sim\frac{A_{j_l}u_{j_l}}\delta
\to0
\tag{11.6}
\]

by (4.11). Hence

\[
b_{n_l,j_l}=0
\tag{11.7}
\]

for all large `l`.

Now condition on the high-probability phase event in (11.4). If there is no actual `s_(j_l)` box, Section 7 maps the source histogram into a target with one such box. Summing (7.8) over all targets and using Lemma 10.1 gives

\[
P\left(
Y_{(1)}<s_{j_l},\ s_{j_l}<E_{n_l}<s_{j_l+1}
\right)
\le8R\varepsilon_{n_l}.
\tag{11.8}
\]

Since `r_n->infinity` and `A_r->infinity`,

\[
\varepsilon_{n_l}=A_{r_{n_l}}^{-1}\to0.
\tag{11.9}
\]

Together with (11.4),

\[
P(Y_{(1)}\ge s_{j_l})\to1.
\tag{11.10}
\]

Using (11.5),

\[
\liminf_l\frac{Y_{(1)}}{n_l}\ge\delta
\quad\text{in probability}.
\tag{11.11}
\]

The source upper bound (2.7) supplies the reverse inequality, so

\[
\boxed{
\frac{Y_{(1)}}{n_l}\xrightarrow p\delta.
}
\tag{11.12}
\]

Since `0<=Y_(1)/n_l<=m_(n_l)/n_l=O(1)`, boundedness gives

\[
\boxed{
E\frac{Y_{(1)}}{n_l}\to\delta.
}
\tag{11.13}
\]

Combining (11.13) with the universal ceiling (2.8) proves

\[
\boxed{b(w,\lambda)=\delta=\lambda-\nu.}
\tag{11.14}
\]

This proves Theorem 5.1. `square`

## 12. Concrete theorem strictly beyond both preceding sparse-chain theorems

Fix `eta>0` and take the critical-log profile

\[
\boxed{
u_r=\frac1{(r+2)(\log(r+2))^{1+\eta}}.}
\tag{12.1}
\]

It is summable, eventually decreasing, and

\[
\frac{u_{r-1}}{u_r}\to1,
\tag{12.2}
\]

so (4.3) holds for some finite `R`.

Now choose the borderline unbounded radices

\[
\boxed{
c_r=\max\{2,\lceil\log_2(r+3)\rceil\}.}
\tag{12.3}
\]

Then `c_r->infinity`, so the bounded-radix theorem from the preceding history does not apply. Also

\[
2^{-c_r}\asymp\frac1r,
\tag{12.4}
\]

while

\[
u_r\asymp\frac1{r(\log r)^{1+\eta}}.
\tag{12.5}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\frac{2^{-c_r}}{u_r}\asymp(\log r)^{1+\eta}\to\infty,

\tag{12.6}
\]

which is the **opposite** of the rapid-root condition required by the immediately preceding report.

Nevertheless Theorem 5.1 gives, for the one fixed law

\boxed{b(w,\lambda)=\lambda-\nu.}
\tag{12.8}
\]

Thus the new result genuinely fills the logarithmic-radix gap left by the previous theorem.

In fact Theorem 5.1 proves more: for this same critical-log activity profile, **every** integer radix sequence `c_r>=2` is covered. Support gaps may oscillate arbitrarily and may grow faster than factorially. The support geometry by itself is no longer a live obstruction in this sparse-chain model.
