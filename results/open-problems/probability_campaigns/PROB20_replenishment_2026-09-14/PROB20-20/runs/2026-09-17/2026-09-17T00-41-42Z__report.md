## 2. Exact source target and fixed-law model

The primary source was checked again on 2026-09-17 local time / 2026-09-17 UTC date boundary from Svante Janson, *Simply generated trees, conditioned Galton-Watson trees, random allocations and condensation*, Probability Surveys 9 (2012), arXiv:1112.0510, Section 18.6, Problem 18.39.

The source quantifiers are the following.

- There is one fixed weight sequence `w=(w_k)`.
- The allocation has `m=m(n)` balls and `n` boxes, with `m/n -> lambda`.
- The regime is `0 < nu < lambda`.
- The question is whether `Y_(1)/n ->_p 0` can hold along the full admissible sequence.
- Janson's Example 18.36 shows that such vanishing can occur along a subsequence of one fixed irregular law.
- Janson conjectures that full-sequence vanishing is impossible and, more strongly, that there are a fixed `epsilon>0` and a subsequence on which `Y_(1) >= epsilon n` with probability tending to one.
- Problem 18.40, immediately following, is a separate `nu=0` question.

Section 10 fixes the weighted allocation by

\[
P(B_{m,n}=y)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^n w_{y_i}}{Z(m,n)},
\qquad \sum_i y_i=m.
\tag{2.1}
\]

After the standard equivalent rescaling, we may work at boundary radius `rho=1` whenever `Phi(1)<infinity`. Put

\[
\Phi(1)=\sum_{k\ge0}w_k,
\qquad
\pi_k=\frac{w_k}{\Phi(1)},
\qquad
E_\pi X=\nu.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Then exactly

\[
(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)
\overset d=
(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\mid\{S_n=m\},
\qquad S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Write throughout

\[
\delta:=\lambda-\nu>0.
\tag{2.4}

Janson's Lemma 18.31 gives the universal upper control

\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\le\delta+o_p(1),
\tag{2.5}
\]

and boundedness by `m_n/n=O(1)` then yields

\[
\limsup_nE\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\le\delta.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Thus `delta` is the largest possible value of the frozen metric.

## 3. Frozen metric before this run

The arbitrary-law branch recovered from earlier reports is

\[
b_{\rm int}(w,\lambda)
=
[\lambda-\nu-\nu\log(\lambda/\nu)]\,\Theta_{\rm amp}(w,\lambda),
\tag{3.1}
\]

and an earlier fixed-law stress test proves that `Theta_amp` can be zero. Hence (3.1) is not a universal positive answer.

The newest sparse-window theorem before this run assumes exact log-linearity inside each window. With

\[
I_j=[K_j,\lfloor(1+\eta)K_j\rfloor]\cap\mathbb Z,
\qquad
w_k=e^{-h_jk}\quad(k\in I_j),
\tag{3.2}
\]

`K_{j+1}/K_j -> infinity`, `Delta_j=h_{j-1}-h_j>0`, and

\[
s_{j,\theta}=h_j+\theta\Delta_j,
\qquad
F_j(s)=E_\pi[e^{sX};X<K_j],
\tag{3.3}
\]

the exact transform defect is

\[
d_j(\theta)
=
\frac{[\log F_j(s_{j,\theta})-\nu s_{j,\theta}]_+}{\Delta_j},
\qquad
d_*(\theta)=\liminf_jd_j(\theta).
\tag{3.4}
\]

Under the stated sparse-window hypotheses, the preceding report proved

\[
b_{\rm win}(w,\lambda)
=
\sup_{0<\theta<1}
\left[\delta-\frac{d_*(\theta)}\theta\right]_+
\tag{3.5}
\]

as a lower-bound branch. It also exhibited an exact-linear law with vanishing fractional rate drops and `d_*(1/2)=0`, giving the optimal value `delta` along a subsequence.

The limitation addressed here is the exact identity `w_k=e^{-h_jk}` inside a whole macroscopic interval. A prospective counterexample can be much rougher: consecutive supported values may differ by enormous multiplicative factors even while their coarse record envelope has a favorable slope. The question is how much such microscopic asymmetry can be tolerated without destroying the persistent-subsequence comparison.

## 4. The three new methods and what they did

### B9 — randomize the microstructure: useful stress test, not a theorem by itself

The first attack was to replace a smooth current window by Bernoulli microstructure,

\[
w_k=e^{-h_jk+R_{j,k}},
\qquad
R_{j,k}\in\{0,\Omega_j\},
\tag{4.1}
\]

with the high-bonus sites chosen randomly.

This immediately exposes an important quantifier issue. Randomization over laws does not answer Janson's problem, which fixes one law before `n` varies. Moreover the admissible sequence `m_n` is arbitrary, so the reference giant size can repeatedly hit unusually favorable or unfavorable sites. An annealed average over the bonuses therefore cannot be inserted into a fixed-law proof.

What survives is stronger and deterministic: the two-count argument only needs a one-sided envelope on the microstructure. Once that was noticed, randomization became unnecessary. The successful theorem below holds for **every deterministic bonus pattern** inside the permitted envelope. Thus B9 changed the route, but no probabilistic claim about a random law is used.

### B15 — break the symmetry deliberately: successful

The useful asymmetric model is not a two-sided perturbation. Give some sites arbitrarily large positive bonuses relative to the coarse exponential profile, but never suppress a current-window site below the baseline. This preserves a reliable denominator configuration while allowing the numerator to exploit the worst bonus.

This asymmetry reduces the roughness cost from two oscillation amplitudes to one. It is the key new point of the run.

### B28 — replace `for all sites` by `typical sites`: attempted and rejected

I then tried to replace the supremum bonus by an average or a statement holding on `1-o(1)` of each window. That does not presently close. The denominator uses the deterministic value

\[
k_j=m_{n_j}-r_j,
\tag{4.2}
\]

where `r_j` is a near-mean background sum. The full-sequence assumption `m_n/n -> lambda` supplies no residue-class or equidistribution information forcing `k_j` into a typical subset. Likewise the capped numerator is a sum over all possible current-window values and may be dominated by an exceptional high-bonus site.

Therefore this run retains a uniform one-sided envelope. A typical-site replacement would require an additional smoothing statement that controls where the exact conditioning mass lands inside the window; no such statement is proved here.

## 5. Asymmetric bonus-window setup

Fix `eta>0`. Let

\[
1<K_1<K_2<\cdots,
\qquad
h_1>h_2>\cdots>0,
\qquad
h_j\downarrow0,
\tag{5.1}
\]

and

\[
I_j=[K_j,\lfloor(1+\eta)K_j\rfloor]\cap\mathbb Z.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Assume the windows are eventually disjoint and

\[
\frac{K_{j+1}}{K_j}\longrightarrow\infty.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Define one fixed weight sequence by

\[
w_0=w_1=1,
\qquad
w_k=e^{-h_jk+r_{j,k}}\quad(k\in I_j),
\qquad
w_k=0\quad(k\ge2\text{ outside all }I_j),
\tag{5.4}
\]

where the microscopic bonuses satisfy

\[
0\le r_{j,k}\le\Omega_j.
\tag{5.5}
\]

The lower inequality in (5.5) is deliberate. A current-window coordinate is never penalized below the baseline `e^{-h_jk}`. This is what allows the denominator to be lower-bounded independently of the unknown exact landing site `k_j`.

Assume that `rho=1`, `Phi(1)<infinity`, and the boundary law `pi_k=w_k/Phi(1)` is span one with

\[
0<\nu=E_\pi X<\infty,
\qquad
0<\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}_\pi X<\infty.
\tag{5.6}
\]

The explicit family in Section 8 verifies all of these properties directly.

Put

\[
\Delta_j:=h_{j-1}-h_j>0,
\qquad
s_{j,\theta}:=h_j+\theta\Delta_j,
\quad 0<\theta<1.
\tag{5.7}
\]

As before define the exact truncated transform

\[
F_j(s):=E_\pi[e^{sX};X<K_j]
\tag{5.8}
\]

and its positive first-order defect

\[
d_j(\theta)
:=
\frac{[\log F_j(s_{j,\theta})-\nu s_{j,\theta}]_+}{\Delta_j},
\qquad
d_*(\theta):=\liminf_jd_j(\theta).
\tag{5.9}
\]

Assume the coarse rate advantage dominates placement entropy,

\[
\frac{\Delta_jK_j}{\log K_j}\longrightarrow\infty.
\tag{5.10}
\]

Measure the microscopic roughness on exactly the same energetic scale:

\[
\beta
:=
\limsup_{j\to\infty}
\frac{\Omega_j}{\Delta_jK_j}
\in[0,\infty].
\tag{5.11}
\]

Finally let

\[
c_\eta:=\min\{\eta,1\},
\qquad
\chi_\eta:=\frac1{1+c_\eta}.
\tag{5.12}
\]

The factor `chi_eta` comes from optimally placing the reference condensate as deep inside the current support window as the two-current-box exclusion permits.

## 6. Main theorem: persistent condensation with asymmetric microscopic bonuses

### Theorem 6.1 — bonus-window lower bound

Let one fixed weight law satisfy (5.1)-(5.11). Let `m_n` be any full admissible sequence with

\[
\frac{m_n}{n}\to\lambda>\nu,
\qquad \delta=\lambda-\nu.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Define

\[
\boxed{
 b_{\rm bon}(w,\lambda)
 :=
 \sup_{0<\theta<1}
 \left[
 \delta-
 \frac{d_*(\theta)+\chi_\eta\,\delta\beta}{\theta}
 \right]_+.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge b_{\rm bon}(w,\lambda).
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

More precisely, if for some `theta in (0,1)` and `t>0`,

\[
\theta(\delta-t)
>
 d_*(\theta)+\chi_\eta\,\delta\beta,
\tag{6.4}
\]

then there is a deterministic subsequence `n_j` along which

\[
P(Y_{(1)}>tn_j)\longrightarrow1.
\tag{6.5}
\]

The theorem is source-valid: the law is fixed once and for all, and the subsequence is extracted from that one law for the arbitrary given full sequence `m_n/n -> lambda`.

### Proof

Fix `theta,t` satisfying (6.4). If `beta=infinity`, (6.4) cannot hold, so assume `beta<infinity`.

Because the inequality is strict, choose a number `c` with

\[
0<c<c_\eta
\tag{6.6}
\]

sufficiently close to `c_eta`, and then choose `epsilon>0` sufficiently small, so that

\[
\theta(\delta-t)
>
 d_*(\theta)
+
\frac{\delta(\beta+\epsilon)}{1+c}
+3\epsilon.
\tag{6.7}
\]

This is possible because `1/(1+c)` decreases to `chi_eta` as `c` increases to `c_eta`.

Choose `q` with

\[
d_*(\theta)<q<
\theta(\delta-t)
-
\frac{\delta(\beta+\epsilon)}{1+c}
-2\epsilon.
\tag{6.8}
\]

By the definition of `liminf`, there is an infinite sequence of window indices, still denoted `j`, for which

\[
\log F_j(s_{j,\theta})-\nu s_{j,\theta}
\le q\Delta_j.
\tag{6.9}
\]

By the definition of `limsup`, after discarding finitely many of these indices,

\[
\Omega_j\le(\beta+\epsilon)\Delta_jK_j.
\tag{6.10}
\]

Set

\[
n_j:=\left\lfloor\frac{(1+c)K_j}{\delta}\right\rfloor.
\tag{6.11}
\]

Let `r_j` be an integer nearest to `nu(n_j-1)` and define the reference macroscopic coordinate

\[
k_j:=m_{n_j}-r_j.
\tag{6.12}
\]

Since `m_n/n -> lambda`,

\[
\frac{k_j}{n_j}\to\delta,
\qquad
\frac{k_j}{K_j}\to1+c.
\tag{6.13}
\]

Because `c<eta`, the reference value `k_j` lies strictly inside `I_j` for all sufficiently large `j`. Since the entire integer interval `I_j` is supported, `w_{k_j}>0`. Also, by (5.3),

\[
m_{n_j}<K_{j+1}
\tag{6.14}
\]

for all large `j`; hence no future window can contribute to the conditioned sum.

The lattice local CLT applies to the fixed boundary law because it is span one with finite nonzero variance:

\[
P(S_{n_j-1}=r_j)
=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi n_j}}.
\tag{6.15}
\]

The one-sided bonus condition `r_{j,k_j}>=0` gives

\[
\pi_{k_j}
\ge C_0e^{-h_jk_j},
\qquad C_0:=\Phi(1)^{-1}>0.
\tag{6.16}
\]

Therefore the exact conditioning event has the denominator lower bound

\[
P(S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})
\ge
\pi_{k_j}P(S_{n_j-1}=r_j)
\ge
\exp\{-h_jk_j-O(\log n_j)\}.
\tag{6.17}
\]

Because `h_j->0` and `k_j=Theta(n_j)`, this denominator is `e^{-o(n_j)}`.

Let

\[
R_j:=|\{i:X_i\ge K_j\}|.
\tag{6.18}
\]

By (6.14) and the support gaps, `R_j` counts exactly the coordinates in the current window. Split the exact sum event into the three cases `R_j=0`, `R_j>=2`, and `R_j=1`.

#### Case 1: no current-window coordinate

On `R_j=0`, every variable is below `K_j`. The positive exponential transform gives

\[
P(S_{n_j}=m_{n_j},R_j=0)
\le
 e^{-s_{j,\theta}m_{n_j}}
 F_j(s_{j,\theta})^{n_j}.
\tag{6.19}
\]

Divide by (6.17). Since `m_{n_j}=k_j+r_j`, `r_j=nu(n_j-1)+O(1)`, and (6.9) holds,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{P(S_{n_j}=m_{n_j},R_j=0)}{P(S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})}
&\le
-(s_{j,\theta}-h_j)k_j
+n_jq\Delta_j
+O(\log n_j)+O(s_{j,\theta})\\
&=
-\Delta_jn_j\{\theta\delta-q+o(1)\}
+O(\log n_j).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.20}
\]

The choice (6.8) implies `q<theta(delta-t)<theta delta`, so the coefficient is positive. Moreover `n_j=Theta(K_j)` and (5.10) imply

\[
\Delta_jn_j\gg\log n_j.
\tag{6.21}
\]

Hence

\[
P(R_j=0\mid S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})\to0.
\tag{6.22}
\]

Notice that no `Omega_j` loss appears here. This is exactly where the one-sided bonus assumption improves on a symmetric oscillation envelope: the reference giant is never less likely than the baseline `e^{-h_jk_j}`.

#### Case 2: at least two current-window coordinates

If `R_j>=2`, two coordinates contribute at least `2K_j`. From (6.11),

\[
\frac{2K_j}{n_j}\to\frac{2\delta}{1+c}.
\tag{6.23}
\]

Since `c<1`,

\[
\lambda-\frac{2\delta}{1+c}
=
\nu-\delta\frac{1-c}{1+c}
<\nu.
\tag{6.24}
\]

Thus there is a fixed `a_c>0` such that, after removing any two coordinates of size at least `K_j`, the remaining `n_j-2` nonnegative iid variables must have sum at most `(nu-a_c)(n_j-2)` for all sufficiently large `j`.

For any nonnegative nondegenerate random variable with finite mean, a negative exponential tilt exists. Therefore a standard Chernoff bound gives a constant `gamma_c>0` with

\[
P(S_{n_j-2}\le(\nu-a_c)(n_j-2))
\le e^{-\gamma_cn_j}.
\tag{6.25}
\]

A union bound over the selected pair yields

\[
P(S_{n_j}=m_{n_j},R_j\ge2)
\le n_j^2e^{-\gamma_cn_j}.
\tag{6.26}
\]

The denominator in (6.17) is only `e^{-o(n_j)}`, so

\[
P(R_j\ge2\mid S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})\to0.
\tag{6.27}
\]

This step is why `c<1` is retained and why the window-depth optimization stops at `min{eta,1}`.

#### Case 3: exactly one current-window coordinate but it is too small

Let

\[
A_j(t)
:=
\{S_{n_j}=m_{n_j},\ R_j=1,\ Y_{(1)}\le tn_j\}.
\tag{6.28}
\]

If the unique current-window coordinate equals `u`, then `u in I_j`, `u<=tn_j`, and all remaining coordinates are below `K_j`. Hence

\[
P(A_j(t))
\le
n_j
\sum_{\substack{u\in I_j\\u\le tn_j}}
\pi_u
 e^{-s_{j,\theta}(m_{n_j}-u)}
 F_j(s_{j,\theta})^{n_j-1}.
\tag{6.29}
\]

For `u,k_j in I_j`, the fixed normalization constant cancels and (5.4)-(5.5) give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{\pi_u}{\pi_{k_j}}
&=
-h_j(u-k_j)+r_{j,u}-r_{j,k_j}\\
&\le
-h_j(u-k_j)+\Omega_j.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.30}
\]

This is the only place the microscopic roughness enters. There is **one** `Omega_j` rather than `2Omega_j` because `r_{j,k_j}>=0`.

Divide a term of (6.29) by the denominator lower bound used in (6.17). Using (6.9),

\[
\log(\text{term ratio})
\le
-(s_{j,\theta}-h_j)(k_j-u)
+n_jq\Delta_j
+\Omega_j
+O(\log n_j).
\tag{6.31}
\]

There are only `O(K_j)=O(n_j)` possible values of `u`, so summing them changes the logarithm by only another `O(log n_j)`.

Uniformly for `u<=tn_j`, (6.13) gives

\[
k_j-u\ge(\delta-t)n_j+o(n_j).
\tag{6.32}
\]

Furthermore (6.10)-(6.11) imply

\[
\frac{\Omega_j}{n_j\Delta_j}
\le
\frac{\delta(\beta+\epsilon)}{1+c}+o(1).
\tag{6.33}
\]

Since `s_{j,theta}-h_j=theta Delta_j`, (6.31)-(6.33) yield

\[
\log\frac{P(A_j(t))}{P(S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})}
\le
-n_j\Delta_j
\left\{
\theta(\delta-t)-q-
\frac{\delta(\beta+\epsilon)}{1+c}
+o(1)
\right\}
+O(\log n_j).
\tag{6.34}
\]

The expression in braces is positive by (6.8), and (6.21) again makes the `O(log n_j)` term negligible. Hence

\[
P(A_j(t)\mid S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})\to0.
\tag{6.35}
\]

Combining (6.22), (6.27), and (6.35) gives

\[
P(Y_{(1)}>tn_j\mid S_{n_j}=m_{n_j})\to1.
\tag{6.36}
\]

By the exact iid-conditioning identity (2.3), this is the weighted-allocation statement (6.5).

Finally, `0<=Y_(1)/n<=m_n/n=O(1)`, so (6.5) implies

\[
\limsup_nE\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\ge t.
\tag{6.37}
\]

Let `t` increase to the right-hand threshold in (6.2) and then take the supremum over `theta`. This proves (6.3). `square`

## 7. Why this is a real extension of the previous window theorem

If `Omega_j=0`, then `beta=0` and Theorem 6.1 reduces exactly to the preceding sparse-window branch (3.5).

For `beta>0`, however, the present theorem permits ratios between neighboring weights inside the same window that diverge exponentially on the macroscopic energy scale. The new branch is

\[
\boxed{
 b_{\rm bon}(w,\lambda)
 =
 \sup_{0<\theta<1}
 \left[
 \lambda-\nu-
 \frac{d_*(\theta)+\chi_\eta(\lambda-\nu)\beta}{\theta}
 \right]_+.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Thus, on the asymmetric bonus-window class,

\[
\boxed{
 b(w,\lambda)
 \ge
 \max\{b_{\rm int}(w,\lambda),b_{\rm bon}(w,\lambda)\}.
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

The energetic interpretation is transparent. `theta Delta_j` is the coarse per-particle advantage of a current-window coordinate over the capped background. `d_*(theta) Delta_j` is the exact background free-energy defect. `chi_eta delta beta Delta_j` is the worst possible microscopic bonus per box after optimally locating the reference giant inside the window. Persistent condensation survives whenever the first quantity wins.

A two-sided perturbation `|r_{j,k}|<=Omega_j` would generally cost up to `2Omega_j` in (6.30), and it would also weaken the denominator in (6.16). The one-sided hypothesis is therefore substantive and is retained explicitly; this report does not silently claim a symmetric-roughness theorem.

## 8. Explicit highly oscillatory fixed-law family

The abstract theorem is useful only if its hypotheses can hold for genuinely rough fixed weights. This section gives such a family.

Fix

\[
0<a<\frac38,
\qquad
\eta=\frac12,
\qquad
h_j=2^{-j},
\qquad
K_j=\lceil e^{j^4}\rceil.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Then

\[
\Delta_j=h_{j-1}-h_j=h_j=2^{-j}.
\tag{8.2}
\]

On each window `I_j`, define the deterministic alternating bonus

\[
r_{j,k}
=
\begin{cases}
 a\Delta_jK_j,&k\text{ odd},\\
 0,&k\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
\tag{8.3}
\]

Use (5.4) for the fixed weights.

This law is extremely irregular locally. Inside one window, two adjacent sites of opposite parity may differ by the multiplicative factor

\[
\exp\{a\Delta_jK_j+O(h_j)\},
\tag{8.4}
\]

and `Delta_j K_j -> infinity` superexponentially. Thus the local oscillation factor itself tends to infinity enormously. The exact-linear theorem (3.5) does not apply.

Nevertheless all fixed-law requirements hold.

First, since `0<=r_{j,k}<=a h_jK_j` and `k>=K_j`,

\[
w_k
\le
\exp\{-(1-a)h_jK_j\}
\qquad(k\in I_j).
\tag{8.5}
\]

Because `h_jK_j=2^{-j}e^{j^4+o(1)}`, for every fixed `p>=0`,

\[
\sum_jK_j^{p+1}e^{-(1-a)h_jK_j}<\infty.
\tag{8.6}
\]

Hence `Phi(1)<infinity` and the boundary law has moments of every fixed order. Since `w_0=w_1=1`, the span is one and `0<nu<infinity`. Also `limsup_k w_k^{1/k}=1` because `h_j->0`, so the radius is indeed `rho=1`.

Second,

\[
\frac{K_{j+1}}{K_j}\to\infty,
\qquad
\frac{\Delta_jK_j}{\log K_j}
\asymp
\frac{2^{-j}e^{j^4}}{j^4}	o\infty.
\tag{8.7}
\]

Third, the roughness coefficient is exactly

\[
\beta=a.
\tag{8.8}
\]

It remains to control the transform defect.

### Lemma 8.1 — zero transform defect below the adjacent-window threshold

For every fixed

\[
0<\theta<1-2a,
\tag{8.9}
\]

the family (8.1)-(8.3) satisfies

\[
d_j(\theta)\to0.
\tag{8.10}
\]

#### Proof

Put

\[
s_j=s_{j,\theta}=(1+\theta)h_j.
\tag{8.11}
\]

The elementary inequality

\[
e^u-1-u\le\frac{u^2}{2}e^u
\qquad(u\ge0)
\tag{8.12}
\]

and `log x<=x-1` give

\[
[\log F_j(s_j)-\nu s_j]_+
\le
\frac{s_j^2}{2}
E_\pi[X^2e^{s_jX};X<K_j].
\tag{8.13}
\]

Thus it suffices to bound the truncated tilted second moment uniformly in `j`.

Consider first the immediately preceding window `I_{j-1}`. There

\[
h_{j-1}=2h_j,
\qquad
h_{j-1}-s_j=(1-\theta)h_j,
\tag{8.14}
\]

while the bonus is at most

\[
a\Delta_{j-1}K_{j-1}
=2ah_jK_{j-1}.
\tag{8.15}
\]

Since every `k in I_{j-1}` satisfies `k>=K_{j-1}`, its tilted weight is bounded by a fixed normalization constant times

\[
\exp\{-[(1-\theta)-2a]h_jK_{j-1}\}.
\tag{8.16}
\]

The coefficient is strictly positive by (8.9), and `h_jK_{j-1}` tends to infinity faster than any power of `j`. Multiplying by the `O(K_{j-1}^3)` polynomial factor needed for the number of sites and the `X^2` moment still gives a summable bound.

For an older window `I_i` with `i<=j-2`,

\[
h_i\ge4h_j,
\qquad
s_j<(2h_j)\le\frac12h_i.
\tag{8.17}
\]

Also `Delta_i=h_i`, so the bonus is at most `a h_iK_i`. Hence the tilted exponential factor on `I_i` is at most

\[
\exp\{-(1/2-a)h_iK_i\}.
\tag{8.18}
\]

Since `a<3/8<1/2`, these window contributions form a convergent series after multiplication by any fixed polynomial power of `K_i`.

The atoms at 0 and 1 contribute a bounded amount. Therefore there is a constant `C_theta<infinity`, independent of `j`, such that

\[
E_\pi[X^2e^{s_jX};X<K_j]\le C_\theta.
\tag{8.19}
\]

Using `s_j=Theta(h_j)` and `Delta_j=h_j`, (8.13) gives

\[
0\le d_j(\theta)
\le
\frac{C_\theta s_j^2}{2h_j}
=O(h_j)\to0.
\tag{8.20}
\]

This proves the lemma. `square`

Since `eta=1/2`,

\[
\chi_\eta=\frac{2}{3}.
\tag{8.21}
\]

Combining `beta=a` and Lemma 8.1 with Theorem 6.1 gives, for every `theta<1-2a`,

\[
\limsup_nE\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge
\delta\left(1-\frac{2a}{3\theta}\right).
\tag{8.22}
\]

Letting `theta` increase to `1-2a` yields the explicit source-valid lower bound

\[
\boxed{
\limsup_nE\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge
(\lambda-\nu)
\frac{3-8a}{3-6a},
\qquad 0<a<\frac38.
}
\tag{8.23}
\]

More strongly, for every

\[
0<t<
(\lambda-\nu)
\frac{3-8a}{3-6a},
\tag{8.24}
\]

there is a deterministic subsequence along which

\[
P(Y_{(1)}>tn)\to1.
\tag{8.25}
\]

This is not full one-box condensation, and it is not claimed to be sharp. Its point is robustness: even alternating microscopic bonuses whose log-amplitude is a fixed positive fraction of the record-gap energy cannot eliminate a macroscopic subsequence.

## 9. Counterexample tests against the new transfer

The new theorem was checked against the following failure modes before promotion.

### 9.1 Negative bonuses

If `r_{j,k}` is allowed to be as low as `-Omega_j`, the exact reference size `k_j` can land on a severely suppressed site. The denominator (6.16) then loses an `Omega_j` factor, while the numerator ratio in (6.30) may lose another. The present coefficient is therefore not valid under an unstated two-sided oscillation hypothesis. Such a variant would require a larger roughness penalty and is not asserted here.

### 9.2 Typical-site control

An average bound on `r_{j,k}` does not control the deterministic target `k_j`, nor the maximizer in the numerator sum. This is the precise failure of B28. A typical-site theorem needs an additional local-smoothing or residue-mixing input.

### 9.3 Bonuses comparable to the whole coarse gap

When `beta` is too large, (6.2) correctly collapses to zero. The theorem does not manufacture a positive bound when microscopic roughness can erase the entire record advantage.

### 9.4 Adjacent old-window resonance

The explicit alternating family requires `theta<1-2a`; at `theta=1-2a` the immediately preceding window loses the strict exponential margin in (8.16). The proof takes a supremum as `theta` approaches this boundary from below; it does not claim the endpoint estimate itself.

### 9.5 Two current-window giants

The construction still chooses `c<1`, so two current-window coordinates force the background a fixed amount below its mean. This is necessary because neighboring Janson Problems 18.41/18.42 show that irregular weights can genuinely favor multiple macroscopic boxes.

These checks preserve the exact scope of the theorem rather than silently promoting it to arbitrary irregular weights.
