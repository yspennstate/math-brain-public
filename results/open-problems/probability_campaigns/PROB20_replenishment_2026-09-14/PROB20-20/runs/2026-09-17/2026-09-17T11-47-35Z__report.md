## 3. B30: a universal multiplicative record-window lemma

This is a new deterministic reduction under the exact source hypotheses. It does not close the probabilistic problem.

Normalize the radius to one by replacing `w_k` with `rho^k w_k`; for fixed total mass `m` this multiplies every allocation weight by the same factor and leaves `B_{m,n}` unchanged. Normalize further to probability weights

\[
\pi_k=\frac{w_k}{\Phi(1)}.
\]

Then

\[
\sum_k k\pi_k=\nu<\infty,
\qquad
\limsup_{k:\pi_k>0}\pi_k^{1/k}=1.
\]

For supported `k>=1` define

\[
h(k):=\frac{\log(k\pi_k)}{k}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Since `sum k pi_k<infinity`, we have `k pi_k -> 0`; hence `h(k)<0` for all sufficiently large supported `k`. On the other hand

\[
\limsup h(k)=0.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Choose a fixed `K_0` after all exceptional positive values and define the tail record envelope

\[
H(x):=\max\{h(k):K_0\le k\le x,\ \pi_k>0\}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

For large `x`, `H(x)<0`, `H` is nondecreasing, and `H(x)->0`.

### Lemma 3.1 — record depth times scale diverges

\[
\boxed{-xH(x)\longrightarrow\infty.}
\tag{3.4}
\]

#### Proof

Let `b(x)` be a supported maximizer in (3.3). Because `H(x)->0` whereas every fixed finite set has record value bounded strictly below zero, `b(x)->infinity`. Since `x>=b(x)` and `H(x)=h(b(x))<0`,

\[
-xH(x)
\ge
-b(x)h(b(x))
=
-\log(b(x)\pi_{b(x)}).
\]

The last quantity tends to infinity because `k pi_k ->0`. This proves (3.4). `square`

### Lemma 3.2 — multiplicative record-window gaps are unbounded

For every fixed `theta in (0,1)`,

\[
\boxed{
\limsup_{x\to\infty}
 x\,[H(x)-H(\theta x)]
=\infty.
}
\tag{3.5}
\]

#### Proof

Assume the contrary. Then there are `C<infinity` and `x_0` such that

\[
H(y)-H(\theta y)\le C/y
\qquad(y\ge x_0).
\tag{3.6}
\]

Fix large `x` and put `y_r=x theta^{-r}`. Since `H(y_r)->0`, telescoping gives

\[
-H(x)
=
\sum_{r\ge0}
[H(y_{r+1})-H(y_r)].
\]

Apply (3.6) at `y_{r+1}`; because `theta y_{r+1}=y_r`,

\[
H(y_{r+1})-H(y_r)
\le
\frac{C}{y_{r+1}}
=
\frac{C\theta^{r+1}}{x}.
\]

Thus

\[
-xH(x)
\le
\frac{C\theta}{1-\theta},
\]

contradicting Lemma 3.1. `square`

### Corollary 3.3 — a genuine macroscopic record appears in the window

Along a sequence `x_j->infinity` there are supported points

\[
\theta x_j<b_j\le x_j
\]

such that

\[
\boxed{
 b_j\,[h(b_j)-H(\theta b_j)]\to\infty.
}
\tag{3.7}
\]

Indeed choose `b_j` attaining `H(x_j)`. The strict positive gap forces `b_j>theta x_j`, while `H(theta b_j)<=H(theta x_j)`.

### What this does and does not prove

Equation (3.7) removes the old `b_{i+1}>=2b_i` sparse-support assumption from the **deterministic** record-gap mechanism. It says every source-admissible finite-mean heavy tail has arbitrarily strong multiplicative record improvements at some macroscopic scales.

It does **not** show that those records dominate the entire capped conditioned partition function. The divergence in (3.7) may be arbitrarily slow as a function of physical scale, while the number of near-record fragmentation patterns may grow. That entropy issue is exactly where the historical F0059 argument stopped and it remains a genuine universal obstruction.

A useful stress family shows why no rate is hidden in Lemma 3.2. On dyadic support one may take, up to normalization,

\[
2^j\pi_{2^j}\asymp\frac{1}{j(\log(j+2))^2}.
\tag{3.8}
\]

Then the first moment contribution is summable and the radius is still one, but the natural consecutive dyadic record exponent grows only on the order of `log j`, i.e. only on the order of `log log b` at physical scale `b=2^j`. Hence “unbounded record gap” is not by itself a certificate that all fragmentation entropy is negligible.

This is labelled **PARTIAL** and does not move the arbitrary-law frozen metric.

## 4. B23 counterexample-first stress: flatten the favorable rate as far as the old method allows

\[
\boxed{
 w_0=1,\qquad
 w_k=
 \begin{cases}
 \exp\{-2^j/(j+1)\},&k=2^j,\ j\ge0,\\
 \exp\{-k^2\},&k\ge1\text{ and }k\ne2^j.

\tag{4.1}

For the favorable points `k=2^j`,

\[
-\frac{\log w_k}{k}=\frac{1}{j+1}\to0,
\tag{4.2}

and the ratio of consecutive favorable per-particle rates is

\[
\frac{1/(j+2)}{1/(j+1)}=\frac{j+1}{j+2}\to1.
\tag{4.3}

Thus there is **no fixed fractional rate drop** between successive favorable dyadic scales.

## 5. Exact logarithmic dyadic energy

For `j>=0` put

\[
a_j:=\frac{2^j}{j+1}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

For an integer `c>=0`, define its canonical binary energy

\[
E(c):=\sum_{j:\operatorname{bit}_j(c)=1}a_j
=\sum_{j:\operatorname{bit}_j(c)=1}\frac{2^j}{j+1}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

This is exactly the negative log-weight of the binary target in (4.1).

### Lemma 5.1 — binary expansion minimizes the energy among dyadic decompositions

Every finite multiset of powers of two summing to `c` has energy at least `E(c)`.

#### Proof

Whenever two copies of `2^j` occur, merge them into `2^{j+1}`. The mass is unchanged, while the energy decreases by

\[
2a_j-a_{j+1}
=
\frac{2^{j+1}}{(j+1)(j+2)}
>0.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Repeated merging terminates at the unique binary expansion of `c`. `square`

### Corollary 5.2 — subadditivity

\[
\boxed{E(a+b)\le E(a)+E(b).}
\tag{5.4}
\]

The union of the binary expansions of `a` and `b` is a dyadic decomposition of `a+b`, so Lemma 5.1 applies.

### Lemma 5.3 — target energy is near-exponential but sublinear

There is an absolute `C<infinity` such that, for all large `c`,

\[
\boxed{E(c)\le C\frac{c}{\log(c+2)}.}
\tag{5.5}
\]

#### Proof

If `2^J<=c<2^{J+1}`, then

\[
E(c)\le\sum_{j=0}^J\frac{2^j}{j+1}.
\]

Split at `J/2`. The lower half is `O(2^{J/2})`; in the upper half `1/(j+1)<=2/J`, so its sum is at most `4*2^J/J`. Thus the whole sum is `O(2^J/J)=O(c/log c)`. `square`

The trivial bound

\[
E(c)\le c
\tag{5.6}
\]

will also be useful for filler mass.

## 6. The exact forced top-bit defect is n/log^2 n

Let

\[
P(c):=2^{\lfloor\log_2 c\rfloor}=2^J
\tag{6.1}
\]

be the largest dyadic bit of `c`.

### Lemma 6.1 — avoiding the top bit costs a definite amount

Let a dyadic multiset sum to `c`, and suppose every part is strictly smaller than `P(c)=2^J`. Then its energy is at least

\[
\boxed{
E(c)+\Delta_J,
\qquad
\Delta_J:=\frac{2^J}{J(J+1)}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

#### Proof

In the coalescence process from Lemma 5.1, the terminal binary expansion contains one `2^J`, whereas the initial multiset contains none. At some stage `2^J` must therefore be created by

\[
2^{J-1}+2^{J-1}\longrightarrow2^J.
\]

The energy decrease in this one forced merge is exactly

\[
2\frac{2^{J-1}}{J}-\frac{2^J}{J+1}
=
\frac{2^J}{J(J+1)}.
\]

Every other merge also decreases energy. `square`

If `c=Theta(n)`, then `J=Theta(log n)` and

\[
\boxed{\Delta_J=Theta(n/(\log n)^2).}
\tag{6.3}
\]

The relative rate improvement in (4.3) tends to zero, but the **absolute merge defect** (6.3) still diverges much faster than the combinatorial entropy that will remain after a polylogarithmic cutoff.

## 8. The conditioning denominator is exp(-O(n/log n))

Let

\[
\pi_k=\frac{w_k}{\Phi(1)},
\]

and let `X_1,X_2,...` be iid with law `pi`, `S_n=sum_i X_i`. The balls-in-boxes allocation is exactly `(X_1,...,X_n)` conditioned on `S_n=m_n`.

The law has span one and finite positive variance.

Let

\[
L_n:=\lceil\log_2(m_n+1)\rceil+2=O(\log n).
\tag{8.1}
\]

Choose an integer `r_n` with

\[
r_n=\nu(n-L_n)+O(1).
\tag{8.2}
\]

The span-one finite-variance local central limit theorem gives

\[
P(S_{n-L_n}=r_n)\ge c n^{-1/2}
\tag{8.3}
\]

for some `c>0` and all sufficiently large `n`.

Put

\[
c_n:=m_n-r_n=\delta n+o(n).
\tag{8.4}
\]

Use the first `L_n` coordinates to realize the binary expansion of `c_n`, padded with zeros. Because each dyadic bit `2^j` has probability `Phi(1)^{-1}e^{-a_j}` and zero has probability `Phi(1)^{-1}`, this single exact route yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
P(S_n=m_n)
&\ge
\Phi(1)^{-L_n}e^{-E(c_n)}
P(S_{n-L_n}=r_n)\\
&\ge
\exp\{-C_0 n/\log n\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.5}
\]

for a finite constant `C_0`, using Lemma 5.3 and `L_n=O(log n)`.

This explicit denominator is the first of the three budgets needed below.

## 9. A polylogarithmic cutoff captures all missing mass for this law

The exact source Lemma 18.31(ii) is deliberately **not** invoked here, because it does not tell us that its unknown `Omega_n` dominates a chosen polylogarithmic cutoff.

Set

\[
K_n:=\lfloor(\log n)^4\rfloor,
\tag{9.1}
\]

and define

\[
Z_K:=X\mathbf1_{\{X\le K\}}.
\]

Let

\[
J(K):=\lceil\log_2K\rceil,
\qquad
 t_K:=\frac{1}{4(J(K)+1)}.
\tag{9.2}
\]

Thus

\[
t_{K_n}=Theta(1/\log\log n).
\tag{9.3}
\]

### Lemma 9.1 — moving tilted mgf has the correct first-order slope

\[
\boxed{
\log E e^{t_KZ_K}=\nu t_K+o(t_K).
}
\tag{9.4}
\]

#### Proof

First, the tilted second moments are uniformly bounded. If `k=2^j<=K`, then `j<=J(K)` and

\[
t_K\le\frac{1}{4(j+1)}.
\]

Hence

\[
k^2\pi_ke^{t_Kk}
\le
C k^2\exp\left\{-\frac{3k}{4(j+1)}\right\}.
\tag{9.5}
\]

The sum of the right-hand side over dyadic `k=2^j` is finite. If `k` is not dyadic, then for all large `K`, `t_K<1/4` and

\[
k^2\pi_ke^{t_Kk}
\le
C k^2e^{-k^2+k/4},
\tag{9.6}
\]

whose sum is finite. Consequently

\[
\sup_K E[Z_K^2e^{t_KZ_K}]<\infty.
\tag{9.7}
\]

Taylor's theorem gives

\[
Ee^{t_KZ_K}
=
1+t_KEZ_K+O(t_K^2).
\]

Since `EZ_K->EX=nu`, this is

\[
1+\nu t_K+o(t_K),
\]

and taking a logarithm proves (9.4). `square`

### Lemma 9.2 — upper deviation of the small boxes beats the conditioning denominator

For every fixed `epsilon>0`,

\[
P\left(\sum_{i=1}^nZ_{K_n,i}\ge(\nu+\epsilon)n\right)
\le
\exp\{-c_\epsilon n/\log\log n\}
\tag{9.8}
\]

for all large `n`.

#### Proof

Chernoff and Lemma 9.1 give

\[
\begin{aligned}
P\left(\sum Z_i\ge(\nu+\epsilon)n\right)
&\le
\exp\{-t_{K_n}(\nu+\epsilon)n\}
(Ee^{t_{K_n}Z_{K_n}})^n\\
&=
\exp\{-\epsilon nt_{K_n}+o(nt_{K_n})\},
\end{aligned}
\]

and (9.3) finishes the proof. `square`

### Lemma 9.3 — lower deviation is exponentially small

For every fixed `epsilon>0`,

\[
P\left(\sum_{i=1}^nZ_{K_n,i}\le(\nu-\epsilon)n\right)
\le e^{-c'_{\epsilon}n}
\tag{9.9}
\]

for all large `n`.

#### Proof

Choose a fixed `M` so large that

\[
E[X\mathbf1_{\{X\le M\}}]>\nu-\epsilon/4.
\]

For all large `n`, `K_n>=M` and hence `Z_{K_n}>=X1_{X<=M}`. A standard negative Chernoff bound for the fixed bounded variable `X1_{X<=M}` gives (9.9). `square`

### Proposition 9.4 — exact missing mass at the chosen cutoff

In the conditioned balls-in-boxes law,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i>K_n\}}
=\delta n+o_p(n).
}
\tag{9.10}
\]

#### Proof

Divide the iid deviation probabilities (9.8)--(9.9) by the explicit exact-sum denominator (8.5). Since

\[
\frac{n}{\log\log n}\gg\frac{n}{\log n}

and `n >> n/log n`, both conditioned deviation probabilities tend to zero. Thus

\[
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}
=\nu n+o_p(n).
\]

Subtract from `m_n=lambda n+o(n)`. `square`

## 10. Exact coalescence map: the second and third budgets

For an allocation `y=(y_1,...,y_n)`, let

\[
B(y):=\{i:y_i>K_n\},
\qquad
C_B(y):=\sum_{i\in B(y)}y_i.
\tag{10.1}
\]

Let `L_n` be as in (8.1). Define a deterministic selected set `I(y)` as follows:

- if `|B(y)|>=L_n`, set `I(y)=B(y)`;
- if `|B(y)|<L_n`, add the first `L_n-|B(y)|` unused coordinates (in increasing index order) to `B(y)`.

Then

\[
|I(y)|\ge L_n
\]

and the filler mass

\[
D(y):=\sum_{i\in I(y)\setminus B(y)}y_i
\]

satisfies

\[
0\le D(y)\le L_nK_n=O((\log n)^5).
\tag{10.2}
\]

Set

\[
C_I(y):=C_B(y)+D(y).
\]

Replace the selected occupancies by the binary expansion of `C_I(y)`, padded with zeros and placed in a fixed deterministic order; leave all unselected coordinates unchanged. Denote the result by `T(y)`. Because a binary expansion of a number at most `m_n` uses fewer than `L_n` bits, the target always fits inside `I(y)`.

### Lemma 10.1 — source/target energy gap below the top bit

Suppose every large source box satisfies

\[
y_i<H<P(C_B(y)).
\tag{10.3}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
-\log\frac{\prod_iw_{y_i}}{\prod_iw_{T(y)_i}}
\ge
\Delta_{J(C_B(y))}-E(D(y)),
}
\tag{10.4}
\]

where `P(C_B)=2^{J(C_B)}` and `Delta_J` is given by (6.2).

#### Proof

By Lemma 7.1,

\[
\sum_{i\in B(y)}\varphi(y_i)
