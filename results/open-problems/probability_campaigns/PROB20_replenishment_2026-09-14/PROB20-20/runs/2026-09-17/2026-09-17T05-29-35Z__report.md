## 2. Exact source target and fixed-law setup

Primary source, rechecked 2026-09-17:

Svante Janson, *Simply generated trees, conditioned Galton--Watson trees, random allocations and condensation*, Probability Surveys 9 (2012), 103--252, arXiv:1112.0510v1, Section 18.6, Problem 18.39, PDF p. 122.

The source asks:

> “Is \(Y_{(1)}/n\to_p0\) possible when \(0<\nu<\lambda\)?”

The rest of Problem 18.39 distinguishes two quantifiers. Example 18.36 already gives vanishing of the largest box on a selected subsequence for one fixed law. Janson conjectures that such vanishing cannot persist on the full sequence and states the stronger expected alternative: some fixed positive epsilon and some subsequence should have a macroscopic largest box with probability tending to one. Problem 18.40 is separately the case \(\nu=0\).

The balls-in-boxes model uses one fixed weight sequence \(w=(w_k)_{k\ge0}\). Conditional on total mass m in n boxes, a vector y has probability proportional to

\[
\prod_{i=1}^n w_{y_i}.
\]

When the boundary weights are normalized to a probability law \(\pi\), this is exactly the iid law conditioned on its sum:

\[
(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)\overset d=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\mid\{S_n=m_n\},
\qquad S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i.
\tag{2.1}
\]

Throughout this report there is one fixed law, one full admissible sequence \(m_n/n\to\lambda\), and

\[
0<\nu<\lambda<\infty,
\qquad
\delta:=\lambda-\nu>0.
\tag{2.2}
\]

Janson Lemma 18.31 gives the source-side ceiling: for every deterministic cutoff tending to infinity, the mass above it is at most \(\delta n+o_p(n)\). In particular

\[
\limsup_n E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\le\delta.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Thus any class on which we prove the matching lower limit \(\delta\) has the optimal frozen metric.

## 4. Mathematical idea before proof

### B7 — compare the scaling limit of the landing map

The preceding proof maps q large fragments of total mass c to

\[
(g(c),\,c-g(c),\,0,\ldots,0).
\tag{4.1}
\]

With fixed gap L, the second coordinate has fixed positive cost. But the exact fiber count only needs the first landing coordinate to remain a positive fraction of c. Therefore fixed L is far stronger than necessary.

The right large-scale condition is

\[
\liminf_{c\to\infty}\frac{g(c)}c>0.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The residual \(d(c)=c-g(c)\) may grow without bound. Its effect enters only through its weight \(w_{d(c)}\). If that weight is no worse than polynomial in c, the earlier exponent calculation survives after adding one new exponent \(\kappa\).

### B23 — try to build the opposite behavior first

A natural counterexample attempt is to place polynomially heavy atoms on a sparse set and suppress every intermediate integer super-polynomially. The bounded-gap theorem does not cover unbounded gaps, so at first this appears promising.

The attempt fails whenever the gap is small enough that the residual suppression is still only \(c^{-o(1)}\) or, more generally, \(c^{-\kappa+o(1)}\) for finite \(\kappa\). The explicit logarithmic-gap law in Section 9 has unbounded gaps and super-polynomial suppression at almost every non-backbone site, yet the residual needed to hit the previous backbone point is only logarithmic, so its cost is sub-polynomial in the macroscopic scale. Full one-giant condensation still follows.

This does **not** show that all sparse backbones condense. It identifies what an opposite construction must exploit: the residual landing cost has to remain expensive enough, on every usable scale, to defeat every fixed-q coalescence.

### B30 — localize the gluing obstruction

For a polynomial global upper envelope with exponent \(\beta_->2\), a heavy landing exponent \(\beta_+\), and residual-cost exponent \(\kappa\), the exact gluing inequality becomes

\[
\boxed{
q(\beta_--2)>\beta_++\kappa-2.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

If (4.3) holds for some finite q, q large fragments are asymptotically too expensive compared with one preferred landing box plus one residual box. Hence at most q-1 boxes can carry the missing mass and a macroscopic maximum is forced.

Because \(\beta_->2\), every **finite** \(\beta_++\kappa\) admits some finite q. Thus, inside this envelope scheme, a true full-dust counterexample must make the effective landing-plus-reservoir exponent unbounded, or destroy the positive-fraction landing property, or leave the global polynomial upper-envelope regime altogether.

## 5. Exact variable-gap coalescence lemma

Fix q>=2 and integer cutoff K. Define

\[
A_{q,K}(c)
:=
\sum_{\substack{a_1+\cdots+a_q=c\\a_i>K}}
\prod_{i=1}^q w_{a_i},
\tag{5.1}
\]

and let

\[
R_K:=\#\{i:Y_i>K\}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

For each sufficiently large c, choose integers

\[
0\le g(c)\le c,
\qquad
d(c):=c-g(c).
\tag{5.3}
\]

Assume there is a fixed \(\alpha>0\) such that eventually

\[
g(c)\ge\alpha c.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Define

\[
\widetilde Q_{q,K}(m)
:=
\sup_{q(K+1)\le c\le m}
\frac{A_{q,K}(c)}
{w_{g(c)}w_{d(c)}w_0^{q-2}}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

### Lemma 5.1 — exact two-coordinate target count

For all sufficiently large K,

\[
\boxed{
E(R_K)_q
\le
\frac{m\,n^{q-1}}{\alpha q(K+1)}
\widetilde Q_{q,K}(m).
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

#### Proof

Expand the falling factorial over ordered q-tuples of distinct box indices. Fix such a tuple and fixed occupancies \(a_1,\ldots,a_q>K\), and put \(c=\sum_ja_j\). Keeping every unselected coordinate unchanged, replace the selected q-block by

\[
(g(c),d(c),0,\ldots,0).
\tag{5.7}
\]

Both blocks have total c. The ratio of their unnormalized allocation weights is exactly

\[
\frac{\prod_{j=1}^q w_{a_j}}
{w_{g(c)}w_{d(c)}w_0^{q-2}}.
\tag{5.8}
\]

Summing over every composition of c with all parts greater than K produces the ratio in (5.5).

It remains to count how many selected tuples can map into one target allocation. In every target,

\[
Y_{i_1}=g(c)\ge\alpha c\ge\alpha q(K+1).
\tag{5.9}
\]

Since total mass is m, there are at most

\[
\frac{m}{\alpha q(K+1)}
\tag{5.10}
\]

possible first target indices. The remaining ordered selected indices have at most \(n^{q-1}\) choices. Once the first two target values are fixed, c is fixed because \(c=g(c)+d(c)\); counting all possible selected indices as above only overcounts. Combining the exact weight comparison with this deterministic fiber bound proves (5.6). \(\square\)

No local limit theorem and no pointwise record-rate comparison is used in this lemma.

## 6. New theorem: polynomial reservoir cost permits unbounded gaps

### Definition 6.1 — polynomial landing-reservoir scheme

A fixed full-support weight sequence \(w\), with \(w_0>0\), has parameters

\[
(\beta_-,\beta_+,\kappa,\alpha)
\]

if

\[
2<\beta_-\le\beta_+<\infty,
\qquad
0\le\kappa<\infty,
\qquad
\alpha>0,
\tag{6.1}
\]

and the following hold eventually.

First, a global polynomial upper envelope:

\[
w_k\le c_+k^{-\beta_-}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Second, for every sufficiently large integer c there is a deterministic preferred landing site \(g(c)\in\{0,\ldots,c\}\), with residual \(d(c)=c-g(c)\), such that

\[
g(c)\ge\alpha c
\tag{6.3}
\]

and

\[
w_{g(c)}\ge c_-c^{-\beta_+}.
\tag{6.4}
\]

Third, the residual has polynomial cost exponent at most \(\kappa\): for every \(\eta>0\), there is \(C_\eta>0\) such that, for all sufficiently large c,

\[
w_{d(c)}\ge C_\eta c^{-(\kappa+\eta)}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

The fixed-gap theorem is the special case \(\kappa=0\), because a residual taking values in a finite set has fixed positive weight. The new definition allows \(d(c)\to\infty\) and hence allows unbounded gaps between preferred landing sites.

### Theorem 6.2 — variable-gap persistence

Let one fixed full-support weight sequence satisfy Definition 6.1. Normalize at the radius to obtain boundary mean \(\nu\), and let one full admissible sequence satisfy

\[
\frac{m_n}{n}\to\lambda>\nu>0.
\]

Put \(\delta=\lambda-\nu\), and define

\[
\boxed{
q_*
:=
\left\lfloor
\frac{\beta_++\kappa-2}{\beta_--2}
\right\rfloor+1.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

Then, along the **full admissible sequence**, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[
\boxed{
P\left(
\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge
\frac{\delta-\varepsilon}{q_*-1}
\right)\to1.
}
\tag{6.7}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
\liminf_{n\to\infty}E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n
\ge
\frac{\lambda-\nu}{q_*-1}.
}
\tag{6.8}
\]

In particular the frozen limsup metric has the same lower bound.

If

\[
\boxed{
\beta_++\kappa<2\beta_--2,
}
\tag{6.9}
\]

then \(q_*=2\), and the full sequence has a unique macroscopic box:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\to_p\delta,
\qquad
\frac{Y_{(2)}}n\to_p0,
\qquad
E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\to\delta.
}
\tag{6.10}
\]

#### Proof

##### Step 1: radius one and finite positive boundary mean

From (6.2),

\[
\limsup_{k\to\infty}w_k^{1/k}\le1.
\]

The landing sites tend to infinity because \(g(c)\ge\alpha c\), and (6.4) gives along them

\[
\limsup w_{g(c)}^{1/g(c)}\ge1.
\]

Hence the radius of \(\Phi(z)=\sum_kw_kz^k\) is exactly one. Since \(\beta_->2\),

\[
\Phi(1)<\infty,
\qquad
\sum_k k w_k<\infty.
\]

Full support implies the normalized boundary law

\[
\pi_k=\frac{w_k}{\Phi(1)}
\]

has finite positive mean \(\nu\). Multiplying every weight by the same normalization constant changes neither the allocation law nor any ratio argument below, so we freely use the normalized law in probability estimates.

##### Step 2: polynomial lower bound on the exact conditioning event

By the ordinary law of large numbers for \(S_{n-2}\), choose deterministic \(\eta_n\downarrow0\) such that

\[
P\bigl(|S_{n-2}-(n-2)\nu|\le\eta_n n\bigr)\to1.
\tag{6.11}
\]

For all large n the probability in (6.11) is at least 1/2. The interval contains at most \(2n+3\) integers after taking \(\eta_n\le1\), so some integer \(r_n\) in it satisfies

\[
P(S_{n-2}=r_n)\ge\frac1{2(2n+3)}.
\tag{6.12}
\]

Put

\[
c_n:=m_n-r_n.
\]

Then

\[
c_n=\delta n+o(n).
\tag{6.13}
\]

Use the deterministic landing decomposition

\[
c_n=g(c_n)+d(c_n).
\]

For any fixed \(\eta>0\), (6.4)--(6.5) and normalization yield

\[
\pi_{g(c_n)}\ge C n^{-\beta_+},
\qquad
\pi_{d(c_n)}\ge C_\eta n^{-(\kappa+\eta)}.
\tag{6.14}
\]

The explicit event

\[
\{X_1=g(c_n),\ X_2=d(c_n),\ S_{n-2}=r_n\}
\subseteq\{S_n=m_n\}
\]

therefore gives

\[
\boxed{
P(S_n=m_n)
\ge
C_\eta n^{-(\beta_++\kappa+1+\eta)}.
}
\tag{6.15}
\]

Only a polynomial lower bound is needed below.

##### Step 3: every sublinear power cutoff captures exactly the missing mass

Fix \(0<\gamma<1\), let

\[
K_n=\lfloor n^\gamma\rfloor,
\]

and set

\[
Z_i=X_i\mathbf1_{\{X_i\le K_n\}}.
\]

Then

\[
0\le Z_i\le K_n,
\qquad EZ_i\le\nu,
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Z_i)\le EZ_i^2\le\nu K_n.
\tag{6.16}
\]

Bernstein's inequality gives, for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[
P\left(\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i>(\nu+\varepsilon)n\right)
\le
\exp\{-c_\varepsilon n/K_n\}.
\tag{6.17}
\]

Divide by the polynomial conditioning lower bound (6.15). Because \(n/K_n=n^{1-\gamma+o(1)}\), the exponential term dominates every polynomial, so under \(S_n=m_n\),

\[
P\left(\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}>(\nu+\varepsilon)n\right)\to0.
\tag{6.18}
\]

Janson Lemma 18.31(i), which applies to any deterministic \(K_n\to\infty\), supplies the matching lower bound

\[
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}
\ge\nu n+o_p(n).
\tag{6.19}
\]

Hence

\[
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i\le K_n\}}
=\nu n+o_p(n),
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
T_n:=\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{Y_i>K_n\}}
=\delta n+o_p(n).
}
\tag{6.20}
\]

This is an unconditional statement under the conditioned allocation law and holds for every fixed \(\gamma\in(0,1)\).

##### Step 4: bound the q-fold fragmentation defect

Fix q>=2. If

\[
a_1+\cdots+a_q=c,
\qquad a_i>K,
\]

then at least one coordinate is at least c/q. Using (6.2) on that coordinate, summing over its q possible positions, and dropping the exact-sum constraint on the other q-1 coordinates gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
A_{q,K}(c)
&\le C_q c^{-\beta_-}
\left(\sum_{a>K}a^{-\beta_-}\right)^{q-1}\\
&\le C_q c^{-\beta_-}
K^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.21}
\]

For every fixed \(\eta>0\), the target lower bounds (6.4)--(6.5) yield

\[
w_{g(c)}w_{d(c)}
\ge
C_\eta c^{-(\beta_++\kappa+\eta)}.
\tag{6.22}
\]

Therefore, for \(c\le m_n=O(n)\),

\[
\boxed{
\widetilde Q_{q,K}(m_n)
\le
C_{q,\eta}
 n^{\beta_++\kappa+\eta-\beta_-}
 K^{-(q-1)(\beta_--1)}.
}
\tag{6.23}
\]

##### Step 5: the finite-q threshold

Apply Lemma 5.1 at \(K=K_n\). Since \(m_n=O(n)\),

\[
E(R_{K_n})_q
\le
C_{q,\eta}
 n^{q+\beta_++\kappa+\eta-\beta_-}
 K_n^{-D_q},
\tag{6.24}
\]

where

\[
D_q
:=1+(q-1)(\beta_--1)
=(q-1)\beta_- -q+2.
\tag{6.25}
\]

With \(K_n=n^{\gamma+o(1)}\),

\[
E(R_{K_n})_q
\le
n^{q+\beta_++\kappa+\eta-\beta_--\gamma D_q+o(1)}.
\tag{6.26}
\]

There exist sufficiently small \(\eta>0\) and some \(\gamma<1\) making the exponent negative exactly when

\[
q+\beta_++\kappa-\beta_-<D_q,
\]

which simplifies to

\[
\boxed{
q(\beta_--2)>\beta_++\kappa-2.
}
\tag{6.27}
\]

The least integer q satisfying the strict inequality is exactly \(q_*\) in (6.6). Choose \(\eta>0\) small enough that the strict inequality remains true and then choose \(\gamma<1\) sufficiently close to one. It follows that

\[
E(R_{K_n})_{q_*}\to0.
\tag{6.28}
\]

##### Step 6: finite fragmentation complexity forces a macroscopic maximum

Markov's inequality for the falling factorial gives

\[
P(R_{K_n}\ge q_*)
\le
\frac{E(R_{K_n})_{q_*}}{q_*!}
\to0.
\tag{6.29}
\]

By (6.20), asymptotically \(\delta n\) mass lies above \(K_n\), while (6.29) says that this mass occupies at most \(q_*-1\) boxes w.h.p. Hence for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[
P\left(
Y_{(1)}\ge\frac{(\delta-\varepsilon)n}{q_*-1}
\right)\to1.
\]

This proves (6.7). Since \(Y_{(1)}/n\) is uniformly bounded by \(m_n/n=O(1)\), (6.8) follows.

If (6.9) holds, q=2 satisfies (6.27). Then w.h.p. there is at most one box above \(K_n\); (6.20) forces exactly one such box carrying \(\delta n+o_p(n)\), while every other box is at most \(K_n=o(n)\). This proves (6.10). \(\square\)

## 7. What the theorem adds

Theorem 6.2 simultaneously contains two previously separate branches.

1. **Global asymmetric polynomial envelope.** Choose \(g(c)=c\), \(d(c)=0\), \(\alpha=1\), \(\kappa=0\). The theorem reduces to the prior asymmetric-envelope threshold.
2. **Bounded-gap heavy backbone.** If \(c-g(c)\le L\), full support makes the residual weight bounded below on a fixed finite set, again giving \(\kappa=0\).

But Theorem 6.2 also permits

\[
d(c)\to\infty
\]

and therefore permits genuinely unbounded gaps between preferred landing sites. What matters is not the number of missing integers but the asymptotic reservoir price required to hit a preferred site without changing the total mass.

The useful effective exponent is

\[
\boxed{\beta_{\rm eff}:=\beta_++\kappa.}
\tag{7.1}
\]

The q-fragment route is killed whenever

\[
q(\beta_--2)>\beta_{\rm eff}-2.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Because \(\beta_->2\), every finite \(\beta_{\rm eff}\) admits a finite q. This is the main B30 obstruction to gluing sparse scales into full dust within this class.

## 8. Counterexample checks against over-transfer

### 8.1 A positive-fraction landing site is actually used

The proof of Lemma 5.1 uses \(g(c)\ge\alpha c\) to retain the decisive target-count factor \(1/K\). If every usable heavy landing site can be arbitrarily small compared with c, this exact counting argument does not provide (5.6) with a uniform constant. The theorem does not claim otherwise.

### 8.2 Finite residual exponent is actually used

If along all usable landing maps

\[
-\log w_{d(c)}\gg\log c,
\]

then no finite \(\kappa\) satisfies (6.5). The denominator route and coalescence ratio may then lose super-polynomially. The theorem gives no conclusion in that regime.

For example, if gaps are of order \(c^a\), \(a>0\), and off-backbone residual weights are \(e^{-\sqrt{k}}\), then the reservoir price is \(e^{-c^{a/2}}\), far smaller than any polynomial. This is precisely outside the new theorem and remains a plausible arena for a counterexample attack.

### 8.3 The 18.41 monotone-cliff counterexample remains compatible

The neighboring Janson 18.41 construction is a fixed monotone law for which unique one-box condensation fails along cliff subsequences: at least two macroscopic boxes persist. That example is a stress test against accidentally proving too much. The present theorem only promises a finite number of macroscopic boxes unless q=2 meets the exponent inequality. There is no contradiction.

### 8.4 Example 18.36 remains the quantifier warning

Janson's own fixed law in Example 18.36 has a subsequence on which the largest box is o(n), but another subsequence with macroscopic boxes. Nothing in this report promotes a selected-subsequence estimate to a full-sequence statement without proving the factorial-moment bound on the full chosen \(m_n\) sequence.

## 9. Explicit new stress law: polynomial sites separated by logarithmically growing gaps

Define an increasing deterministic sequence of preferred sites by

\[
a_1=3,
\qquad
a_{j+1}=a_j+\left\lceil\log(a_j+2)\right\rceil,
\tag{9.1}
\]

and put

\[
G:=\{a_j:j\ge1\}.
\]

The gaps

\[
a_{j+1}-a_j\to\infty,
\]

so G is **not** a bounded-gap backbone.

Define one fixed full-support weight sequence by

\[
w_0=1,
\]

and, for k>=1,

\[
\boxed{
w_k=
\begin{cases}
k^{-3},&k\in G,\\
e^{-\sqrt{k}},&k\notin G.
\end{cases}}
\tag{9.2}
\]

For all sufficiently large k,

\[
e^{-\sqrt{k}}\le k^{-3},
\]

so the global upper envelope holds with

\[
\beta_-=3.
\]

For every sufficiently large c, let \(g(c)\) be the largest element of G not exceeding c. Then

\[
d(c):=c-g(c)
\le
\left\lceil\log(g(c)+2)\right\rceil
=O(\log c),
\tag{9.3}
\]

and therefore \(g(c)/c\to1\). On the preferred site,

\[
w_{g(c)}=g(c)^{-3}\ge Cc^{-3},
\]

so

\[
\beta_+=3.
\]

It remains to compute the reservoir exponent. If \(d(c)\in G\), then

\[
-\log w_{d(c)}=3\log d(c)=O(\log\log c)=o(\log c).
\]

If \(d(c)\notin G\), then

\[
-\log w_{d(c)}=\sqrt{d(c)}=O(\sqrt{\log c})=o(\log c).
\]

Hence for every \(\eta>0\), eventually

\[
w_{d(c)}\ge c^{-\eta}.
\]

Thus

\[
\kappa=0.
\]

Theorem 6.2 has \(\beta_-=\beta_+=3\), \(\kappa=0\), so
