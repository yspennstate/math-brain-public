## 1. Exact target and primary-source verification

Janson's Problem 18.39 asks, for one fixed weighted-allocation law and an admissible sequence with
\[
0<\nu<\lambda,\qquad m_n/n\to\lambda,
\]
whether
\[
Y_{(1)}/n\to_p0
\]
can hold along the **full** sequence. The primary source states explicitly that Example 18.36 gives this only on a subsequence, and conjectures both that full-sequence convergence to zero is impossible and, more strongly, that there are a fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) and a subsequence with \(Y_{(1)}\ge\varepsilon n\) w.h.p.

Primary source, checked directly in the current arXiv HTML:
- S. Janson, arXiv:1112.0510, Problem 18.39, lines 3539–3541 of the current HTML rendering.
- Theorem 17.1, lines 2009–2059: partition-function exponential rate.
- Lemma 18.31, lines 3206–3235: the exact small/large-box mass statements and the important distinction between part (i), valid for every \(K_n\to\infty\), and part (ii), valid only below an existentially slow \(\Omega_n\).

When the finite-mean boundary normalization is valid, write
\[
\pi_k=\frac{w_k\rho^k}{\Phi(\rho)},\qquad
\sum_k\pi_k=1,\qquad
\sum_k k\pi_k=\nu,
\]
so the normalized radius is one and
\[
(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)\overset d=(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)\mid\{S_n=m_n\},
\]
with iid \(\xi_i\sim\pi\).

A fresh bounded literature search found no exact later resolution of Problem 18.39. Standard big-jump theory such as Denisov–Dieker–Shneer, *Annals of Probability* 36 (2008), arXiv:math/0703265, assumes subexponential or comparable hypotheses and therefore is not a universal black box for Janson's arbitrary irregular boundary law.

# 4. New exact entropy identity for the conditioned marginal

Let \(\mathbb Q_n\) denote the conditioned law
\[
\mathbb Q_n=\mathbb P_\pi^{\otimes n}(\cdot\mid S_n=m_n),
\]
and let \(\mu_n\) be its one-coordinate marginal, the law of \(Y_1\).

## Lemma 4.1 — exact KL budget
\[
\boxed{
D(\mathbb Q_n\Vert\pi^{\otimes n})=b_n
}
\tag{4.1}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
b_n\ge nD(\mu_n\Vert\pi).}
\tag{4.2}
\]

### Proof
On \(\{S_n=m_n\}\),
\[
\frac{d\mathbb Q_n}{d\pi^{\otimes n}}=p_n^{-1},
\]
so (4.1) is immediate. Since \(\mathbb Q_n\) is exchangeable with marginal \(\mu_n\),
\[
D(\mathbb Q_n\Vert\pi^{\otimes n})
=
D(\mathbb Q_n\Vert\mu_n^{\otimes n})
+nD(\mu_n\Vert\pi)
\ge nD(\mu_n\Vert\pi).
\]
\(\square\)

This converts the exact conditioning cost into a budget for how far a single conditioned box can tilt the fixed boundary law.

# 5. New band-mean entropy inequality

Fix integers
\[
0\le K<L.
\]
For an arbitrary probability law \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), define its mean mass in the band
\[
a=a(\mu;K,L):=
\sum_{K<k\le L}k\mu_k,
\tag{5.1}
\]
and define the corresponding boundary-law band mass
\[
Q=Q(K,L)=\sum_{K<k\le L}k\pi_k.
\tag{5.2}
\]
Let
\[
\Phi(a,Q):=
\begin{cases}
a\log(a/Q)-a+Q,&a>Q,\\0,&a\le Q.
\end{cases}
\tag{5.3}
\]

## Theorem 5.1 — band-mean entropy cost
For every \(\mu\),
\[
\boxed{
D(\mu\Vert\pi)
\ge
\frac{1}{L}\Phi(a,Q).
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

### Proof
For \(h\ge0\), the Gibbs variational inequality gives
\[
D(\mu\Vert\pi)
\ge
h a-\log\mathbb E_\pi
\exp\{hX\mathbf1_{\{K<X\le L\}}\}.
\tag{5.5}
\]
For \(0\le k\le L\), convexity of \(x\mapsto e^{hx}\) gives the chord bound
\[
e^{hk}-1
\le
\frac{k}{L}(e^{hL}-1).
\tag{5.6}
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_\pi e^{hX\mathbf1_{\{K<X\le L\}}}
&=1+\sum_{K<k\le L}\pi_k(e^{hk}-1)\\
&\le1+\frac{Q}{L}(e^{hL}-1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.7}
\]
Using \(\log(1+x)\le x\),
\[
D(\mu\Vert\pi)
\ge
h a-rac{Q}{L}(e^{hL}-1).
\tag{5.8}
\]
Set \(z=hL\). Then
\[
LD(\mu\Vert\pi)
\ge
az-Q(e^z-1).
\]
The supremum over \(z\ge0\) is zero when \(a\le Q\), and when \(a>Q\) is attained at
\[
z=\log(a/Q),
\]
with value \(a\log(a/Q)-a+Q\). \(\square\)

This is an exact, one-line variational estimate. Its key feature is the factor \(1/L\), which converts a constant amount of conditioned mass in a band capped at \(L\) into a KL cost proportional to the logarithmic scarcity of the **size-biased** boundary mass in that band.

# 6. New conditioned cap theorem

For \(L>K_n\), define the conditioned band mass per coordinate
\[
a_n(L):=
\frac1n\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q_n}
\sum_iY_i\mathbf1_{\{K_n<Y_i\le L\}}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

## Lemma 6.1 — high-probability caps force constant band mean
Let
\[
\varepsilon_n(L):=
\mathbb Q_n(Y_{(1)}>L).
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
a_n(L)
\ge
c-o(1)-\left(\frac{m_n}{n}\right)\varepsilon_n(L).}
\tag{6.2}
\]

### Proof
By (3.3),
\[
\frac1n\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q_n}T_n
\ge c-o(1).
\]
The mass in boxes above \(L\) is at most \(m_n\mathbf1_{\{Y_{(1)}>L\}}\). Subtracting gives (6.2). \(\square\)

Combining Lemmas 4.1, 6.1, and Theorem 5.1 yields the main new estimate.

## Theorem 6.2 — entropy-band cap inequality
Suppose \(L_n>K_n\) and
\[
\varepsilon_n:=\mathbb Q_n(Y_{(1)}>L_n)\to0.
\]
Put
\[
Q_n^{\rm band}:=Q(K_n,L_n).
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
b_n
\ge
\frac{n}{L_n}
\left[
(c-o(1))\log\frac{c-o(1)}{Q_n^{\rm band}}
-c+o(1)+Q_n^{\rm band}
\right].}
\tag{6.3}
\]
In particular, since \(Q_n^{\rm band}\to0\),
\[
\boxed{
\log\frac1{Q(K_n,L_n)}
\le
\left(\frac{L_n}{cn}+o\left(\frac{L_n}{n}\right)\right)b_n+O(1).
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

A robust version requiring only a fixed high-probability cap is useful in applications: if for all large \(n\)
\[
\mathbb Q_n(Y_{(1)}>L_n)\le\frac{c}{4\lambda}
\]
and the \(o(1)\) in (3.3) is sufficiently small, then \(a_n(L_n)\ge c/2\), hence
\[
\boxed{
Q(K_n,L_n)
\ge
\frac c2
\exp\left\{-1-rac{2L_nb_n}{cn}\right\}.
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

### Proof
Apply Theorem 5.1 to \(\mu_n\), then Lemma 4.1:
\[
b_n\ge nD(\mu_n\Vert\pi)
\ge\frac n{L_n}\Phi(a_n(L_n),Q_n^{\rm band}).
\]
Lemma 6.1 gives \(a_n(L_n)=c-o(1)\) from below when \(\varepsilon_n\to0\). Since \(K_n\to\infty\), finite first moment implies \(Q_n^{\rm band}\le Q(K_n)\to0\), so eventually \(a_n>Q_n^{\rm band}\). This gives (6.3) and (6.4). The robust bound (6.5) follows by replacing \(a_n\) by \(c/2\) and using monotonicity of \(a\mapsto\Phi(a,Q)\) for \(a>Q\). \(\square\)

# 7. Consequences for any hypothetical full-sequence counterexample

Assume hypothetically
\[
Y_{(1)}/n\to_p0.
\tag{7.1}
\]
Choose any deterministic capture scale \(L_n=o(n)\) such that
\[
\mathbb Q_n(Y_{(1)}\le L_n)\to1.
\tag{7.2}
\]
Define the dimensionless scale ratio
\[
R_n:=\frac{L_nb_n}{n}
=
\frac{L_n}{n/b_n}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

## Corollary 7.1 — tail mass must be exponentially large on the scale ratio
Every such capture scale satisfies
\[
\boxed{
Q(K_n,L_n)
\ge
\frac c2 e^{-1-(2/c)R_n}
}
\tag{7.4}
\]
for all large \(n\).

Because \(K_n\to\infty\), the left side tends to zero. Hence necessarily
\[
\boxed{R_n\to\infty.}
\tag{7.5}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{L_n\gg n/b_n.}
\tag{7.6}
\]
More importantly than (7.6), (7.4) quantifies exactly how much fixed-law size-biased mass must be present between the self-consistent lower scale \(n/b_n\) and the actual high-probability maximum scale.

## Corollary 7.2 — fixed macroscopic cap, linear-in-\(\eta\) exponent
Under the full no-condensation hypothesis, for every fixed sufficiently small \(\eta>0\), take \(L_n=\eta n\). Then
\[
\boxed{
Q(K_n,\eta n)
\ge
\frac c2
\exp\left\{-1-\frac{2\eta}{c}b_n\right\}
}
\tag{7.7}
\]
for all large \(n\).

This strictly improves the recovered factorial-band constraint, whose exponent was only of order \(\sqrt\eta\,b_n\). The improvement comes from using the exact total KL budget rather than a fixed-order factorial moment.

Thus any genuine counterexample must have large size-biased mass in **every** band
\[
(n/b_n,\eta n]
\]
at a strength exponential only in \(\eta b_n\), for every fixed small \(\eta\).

# 8. An unconditional quantile formulation

Let \(L_n^{(\varepsilon)}\) be any \((1-\varepsilon)\)-upper quantile of \(Y_{(1)}\) under \(\mathbb Q_n\), with fixed
\[
0<\varepsilon<c/(4\lambda).
\]
Then the robust version of Theorem 6.2 gives
\[
\boxed{
\frac{L_n^{(\varepsilon)}b_n}{n}
\ge
\frac c2
\left[
\log\frac{c/2}{Q(K_n,L_n^{(\varepsilon)})}-1
\right].
}
\tag{8.1}
\]
This is unconditional: if the right side diverges, the high quantiles of the maximum must separate from \(n/b_n\) by a correspondingly large factor.

The inequality is implicit because the band tail itself depends on the quantile, but it converts the global conditioned maximum problem into a deterministic comparison between an exact conditioning cost and a fixed-law tail profile.

# 9. Why this still does not close Problem 18.39

The new inequality is substantially sharper but leaves a real obstruction. A finite-mean boundary law can have a size-biased tail that decays arbitrarily slowly. For example, a smooth summable sequence of the rough size
\[
q_k\asymp\frac{1}{k(\log k)^2}
\]
has very large integrated tail mass even though the corresponding allocation is in a standard subexponential one-big-jump regime. The band mass \(Q(K,L)\) is therefore still too coarse to detect the local convolution structure that makes one large jump win.

This adversarial calibration is important: the new theorem does **not** falsely subsume the classical one-big-jump result for all smooth heavy tails. It instead isolates the next missing ingredient as a local/convolution refinement of the size-biased band mass.

# 12. Next concrete attack

The next object should replace the integrated band mass \(Q(K,L)\) by a constrained convolution quantity that remembers the **sum of the selected mesoscopic occupancies**.

A natural candidate is, for fixed \(r\),
\[
C_r(s;K,L)
:=
\sum_{\substack{k_1+\cdots+k_r=s\\K<k_i\le L}}
\prod_{i=1}^r q_{k_i}.
\]
The exact factorial expansion before the crude residual bound already contains these coefficients. The next goal is a subsequential theorem, valid for an arbitrary summable radius-one \(q\), showing that at a suitable strong record scale the total constrained convolution mass of all \(r\)-part macroscopic/mesoscopic splits is smaller than the one-record contribution by enough to beat the exact conditioning budget.

The new entropy theorem helps because it has already forced any hypothetical counterexample to keep substantial q-mass in every relevant band. Therefore a successful constrained-convolution estimate may be attacked under an additional **large band-mass assumption**, rather than for an arbitrary sparse sequence. This is a stronger and more structured starting point than the original record-gap problem.
