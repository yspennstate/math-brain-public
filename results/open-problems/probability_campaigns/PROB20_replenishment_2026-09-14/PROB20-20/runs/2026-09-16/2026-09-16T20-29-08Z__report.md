\mathcal B_{m,n}=\{(y_1,\dots,y_n)\in\mathbb N_0^n:\sum_i y_i=m\},

\Phi(t)=\sum_{k\ge0}w_k t^k,

## 4. B2 — attack the opposite answer at the near-exponential boundary

A plausible place to seek a fixed-law counterexample is just inside the heavy-tail side of an exponential law: the tail is so light that a collective moderate deviation might compete with a macroscopic jump, yet the boundary mean is still finite. I therefore stress-tested

\[
h_\gamma(x)=\frac{c x}{(\log x)^\gamma},
\qquad \gamma>0.
\tag{4.1}
\]

As `gamma` decreases, this approaches an exponential cost without ever becoming exponential. The first-order proof from the previous run fails for every `gamma` because the cap-chord advantage is only

\[
\asymp \frac{n}{(\log n)^{\gamma+1}}.
\]

If one keeps the other `n-1` boxes exactly at their ordinary mean, the truncated mgf contributes a quadratic bulk cost of order

\[
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2\gamma}},
\]

which dominates that advantage when `0<gamma<1`. This looks at first like a serious candidate mechanism for distributed condensation.

The candidate fails for a structural reason: keeping the background exactly at mean is not the cheapest one-giant denominator. The background can move upward by `o(n)` under a tiny positive tilt, allowing the giant to move downward by the same amount. At the correct tangent tilt, the entire bulk convex cost cancels from the cap comparison to first order. What remains is a Bregman divergence between two nearby tilts, which is much smaller than the chord advantage for **every** `gamma>0`.

Thus B2 did not find a counterexample. Instead it exposed why the previous proof was paying an artificial cost.

## 5. New theorem: full condensation for almost-exponential logarithmic hazards

### Theorem 5.1

Fix `c>0`, `gamma>0`. Let `w_k>0` for every `k`, and suppose that for all sufficiently large integers `k`,

\[
w_k=\exp\!\left\{-h(k)\right\},
\qquad
h(x)=\frac{c x}{(\log x)^\gamma}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Let `rho`, `nu` be Janson's parameters. Then `rho=1`, `0<nu<infinity`, the boundary law

\[
\pi_k=\frac{w_k}{\Phi(1)}
\]

has finite moments of every order, positive variance, and span one.

For every `lambda>nu` and every integer sequence `m_n` with

\[
\frac{m_n}{n}\to\lambda,
\]

the fixed-law allocation satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\xrightarrow{p}\lambda-\nu
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
E\frac{Y_{(1)}}n\to\lambda-\nu.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Consequently the frozen metric on this class is exactly

\[
\boxed{b(w,\lambda)=\lambda-\nu.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

### Proof overview

The proof has four ingredients, all verified below:

1. a capped exponential-dual upper bound for the event `Y_(1)<=tn`;
2. an explicit sublinear truncation on which a tiny positive background tilt has a uniform lattice local limit theorem;
3. a denominator configuration whose giant size and background tilt satisfy a tangent matching equation;
4. an exact convex Bregman identity. The old quadratic bulk term appears on both sides and cancels, leaving a penalty `O(n(s-theta)^2)`.

The argument is along the **full** sequence `m_n/n->lambda`; no sparse subsequence is selected.

## 6. Boundary law and notation

Since

\[
w_k^{1/k}=\exp\{-c/(\log k)^\gamma\}\to1,
\]

the radius of convergence is `rho=1`. Moreover

\[
\frac{h(k)}{\log k}
=\frac{c k}{(\log k)^{\gamma+1}}\to\infty,
\]

so `Phi(1)<infinity` and every polynomial moment under `pi` is finite. Full support implies span one and nonzero variance. Write

\[
EX=\nu,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}X=\sigma^2>0,
\]

for `X~pi`, and let

\[
S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n,
\qquad
M_n=\max_i X_i.
\]

The conditional law of `(X_1,...,X_n)` given `S_n=m_n` is exactly `B_{m_n,n}`. Put

\[
\lambda_n:=m_n/n,
\qquad
\delta_n:=\lambda_n-\nu\to\delta:=\lambda-\nu>0,
\]

and

\[
B_n:=-\log P(S_n=m_n).
\]

For the tail formula (5.1),

\[
h'(x)
=
\frac{c(\log x-\gamma)}{(\log x)^{\gamma+1}},
\tag{6.1}
\]

and

\[
h''(x)
=
\frac{c\gamma(\gamma+1-\log x)}{x(\log x)^{\gamma+2}}<0
\tag{6.2}
\]

for all sufficiently large `x`. Hence `h` is eventually increasing and concave.

## 7. Capped dual inequality

For an integer cap `K` define

\[
F_K(s):=\sum_{k\le K}\pi_k e^{sk},
\qquad s\ge0.
\]

Coefficient positivity gives, for every `s>=0`,

\[
P(S_n=m_n,M_n\le K)
\le e^{-s m_n}F_K(s)^n.
\]

Thus

\[
P(M_n\le K\mid S_n=m_n)
\le
\exp\left\{
B_n-n[\lambda_n s-\log F_K(s)]
\right\}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

We will show that for each fixed `t in (0,delta)`, with `K_n=floor(tn)`, there exists a test tilt `s_n` for which the bracketed capped cost exceeds `B_n` by a quantity tending to infinity.

## 8. A polylogarithmic background truncation

Put

\[
L_n:=\log n,
\qquad
\ell_n:=\log\log n.
\]

Choose a fixed constant `A` so large that

\[
cA>10\gamma+30,
\]

and define, for all sufficiently large `n`,

\[
R_n
:=
\left\lceil
A\,\ell_n(\log\ell_n)^\gamma
\right\rceil.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Then

\[
h(R_n)=(cA+o(1))\ell_n,
\qquad
\frac{R_n}{L_n^\gamma}\to0,
\qquad
R_n=o(\sqrt n).
\tag{8.2}
\]

For every fixed integer `p>=0`,

\[
E[X^p;X>R_n]
\le
C_p R_n^{p+1}e^{-h(R_n)}
=
L_n^{-cA+o(1)}.
\tag{8.3}
\]

To justify the first inequality, note that for large `x`,

\[
\frac{h(2x)}{h(x)}
=2\left(\frac{\log x}{\log(2x)}\right)^\gamma
\ge\frac32.
\]

Splitting the tail into dyadic blocks `[2^jR,2^{j+1}R)` bounds the `j`th block by a polynomial factor times `exp(-(3/2)^j h(R))`; the resulting series is bounded by the first block up to a constant factor. Equation (8.3) follows.

Define

\[
\psi_n(u):=\log F_{R_n}(u).
\tag{8.4}
\]

Uniformly for

\[
0\le u\le 3cL_n^{-\gamma},
\tag{8.5}
\]

we have `uR_n->0`. Hence (8.3), Taylor expansion on the bounded support, and convergence of the first three truncated moments imply

\[
\psi_n'(u)=\nu+O(u)+o(L_n^{-M}),
\tag{8.6}
\]

for arbitrarily large fixed `M` after increasing `A`, and

\[
0<c_0\le\psi_n''(u)\le C_0<\infty
\tag{8.7}
\]

for all large `n`, where `c_0,C_0` are fixed. In fact `psi_n''(u)->sigma^2` uniformly on (8.5), but boundedness above and below is all that will be needed.

## 9. Cap transform can be replaced by the background transform

Fix once and for all

\[
0<t<\delta,
\qquad
K_n:=\lfloor tn\rfloor.
\]

Set

\[
a_n:=\frac{h(K_n)}{K_n}
=\frac{c}{(\log K_n)^\gamma}
\tag{9.1}
\]

and choose the near-chord cap tilt

\[
s_n:=a_n-\varepsilon_n,
\qquad
\varepsilon_n:=L_n^{-(\gamma+2)}.
\tag{9.2}
\]

Then `s_n>0` and `s_n=O(L_n^-gamma)`.

We claim

\[
\log F_{K_n}(s_n)
=
\psi_n(s_n)+o(L_n^{-(\gamma+1)}).
\tag{9.3}
\]

Indeed, on `[R_n,K_n]` define

\[
\varphi_n(x):=-h(x)+s_nx.
\]

By (6.2), `varphi_n` is convex there for large `n`. At the two endpoints,

\[
\varphi_n(R_n)
=-(cA+o(1))\ell_n,
\tag{9.4}
\]

because `s_nR_n=o(1)`, while

\[
\varphi_n(K_n)
=-\varepsilon_nK_n
=-\Theta(nL_n^{-(\gamma+2)}).
\tag{9.5}
\]

A convex function lies below its secant. The secant slope from `R_n` to `K_n` is at most `-epsilon_n/2` for large `n`, by (9.4)-(9.5). Consequently

\[
\sum_{R_n<k\le K_n}e^{\varphi_n(k)}
\le
C\varepsilon_n^{-1}e^{\varphi_n(R_n)}
\le
L_n^{\gamma+2-cA+o(1)}.
\tag{9.6}
\]

Our choice of `A` makes the last quantity `o(L_n^{-(gamma+1)})`. Since `F_{R_n}(s_n)->1`, (9.3) follows.

This is the point where eventual concavity is used. No exact one-big-jump local asymptotic is assumed.

## 10. B12 — tangent-compensated background saddle

The central idea is to pay for a slightly elevated background instead of artificially forcing the background sum to equal `(n-1)nu`.

Let

\[
x_n:=\delta_n n.
\]

For large `n`, `x_n` is linear in `n` and

\[
d_n:=h'(x_n)\sim cL_n^{-\gamma}.
\tag{10.1}
\]

For `theta` in the interval `[d_n/2,2d_n]`, put

\[
\mu_n(\theta):=\psi_n'(\theta),
\qquad
z_n(\theta):=m_n-(n-1)\mu_n(\theta).
\tag{10.2}
\]

By (8.6), uniformly on this interval,

\[
\mu_n(\theta)=\nu+O(L_n^{-\gamma}),
\qquad
z_n(\theta)=\delta_n n+O(nL_n^{-\gamma}).
\tag{10.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{h'(z_n(\theta))}{d_n}\to1
\tag{10.4}
\]

uniformly there. The continuous function

\[
f_n(\theta)=\theta-h'(z_n(\theta))
\]

is negative at `d_n/2` and positive at `2d_n` for all large `n`. Hence there is a `theta_n^*` such that

\[
\theta_n^*=h'(z_n(\theta_n^*)).
\tag{10.5}
\]

Now let `r_n` be the nearest integer to

\[
(n-1)\mu_n(\theta_n^*).
\]

Since (8.7) gives a uniform positive derivative for `mu_n`, there exists a unique `hat theta_n` within `O(1/n)` of `theta_n^*` such that

\[
\psi_n'(\widehat\theta_n)=\frac{r_n}{n-1}=:\widehat\mu_n.
\tag{10.6}
\]

Set

\[
k_n:=m_n-r_n.
\tag{10.7}
\]

Then

\[
\frac{k_n}{n}\to\delta,
\qquad
k_n=z_n(\theta_n^*)+O(1),
\tag{10.8}
\]

and, by (6.2), (10.5) and `hat theta_n-theta_n^*=O(1/n)`, 

\[
\widehat\theta_n
=h'(k_n)+o(L_n^{-(\gamma+1)}).
\tag{10.9}
\]

In particular

\[
\widehat\theta_n
=
\frac{c}{(\log k_n)^\gamma}
\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{\log k_n}\right)
+o(L_n^{-(\gamma+1)}).
\tag{10.10}
\]

### Uniform local probability at the tilted background mean

Define the triangular probability law

\[
q_n(j)
:=
\frac{\pi_j e^{\widehat\theta_n j}}{F_{R_n}(\widehat\theta_n)},
\qquad 0\le j\le R_n.
\tag{10.11}
\]

It has mean `hat mu_n` by (10.6). Moreover `hat theta_n R_n->0`, `R_n=o(sqrt n)`, its variance tends to `sigma^2`, its third absolute centered moment is uniformly bounded, and `q_n(0),q_n(1)` stay bounded below by positive constants.

These facts give the lattice triangular-array local CLT at its mean:

\[
q_n^{*(n-1)}(r_n)\ge\frac{c_1}{\sqrt n}
\tag{10.12}
\]

for all large `n`.

For completeness, here is the verification rather than an invocation with unchecked hypotheses. Fourier inversion is split into `|u|<=eta`, `eta<|u|<=pi`. On the first region, the uniformly bounded third centered moments give

\[
\log E_{q_n}e^{iu(X-\widehat\mu_n)}
=-\frac12\sigma_n^2u^2+O(|u|^3)
\]

uniformly, with `sigma_n^2->sigma^2>0`; scaling `u=v/sqrt n` gives the Gaussian integral. On every fixed annulus away from zero modulo `2pi`, the two fixed atoms 0 and 1 give a uniform characteristic-function bound strictly below one:

\[
|\phi_n(u)|^2
\le
1-2q_n(0)q_n(1)(1-\cos u).
\]

The intermediate shrinking annulus is bounded by `exp(-cnu^2)` using the same variance expansion. Thus

\[
\sqrt n\,q_n^{*(n-1)}(r_n)
\to(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2},
\]

which proves (10.12).

Returning from the tilt to the original law,

\[
P(S_{n-1}=r_n,\ \max_{i\le n-1}X_i\le R_n)
=
 e^{-\widehat\theta_n r_n}
F_{R_n}(\widehat\theta_n)^{n-1}
q_n^{*(n-1)}(r_n).
\tag{10.13}
\]

Fixing the first coordinate at `k_n` and using (10.12) therefore yields

\[
B_n
\le
h(k_n)+\log\Phi(1)
+(n-1)
[\widehat\theta_n\widehat\mu_n-\psi_n(\widehat\theta_n)]
+\frac12\log n+O(1).
\tag{10.14}
\]

This denominator is the key improvement over the preceding run's background-at-mean lower bound.

## 11. Exact chord–tangent Bregman cancellation

Define the convex Bregman divergence

\[
D_{\psi_n}(s,\theta)
:=
\psi_n(s)-\psi_n(\theta)-\psi_n'(\theta)(s-\theta)
\ge0.
\tag{11.1}
\]

The exact decomposition

\[
m_n=k_n+r_n
=k_n+(n-1)\widehat\mu_n
\tag{11.2}
\]

and (10.6) give

\[
\begin{aligned}
&m_ns_n-n\psi_n(s_n)
-
\Bigl[
 h(k_n)
 +(n-1)(\widehat\theta_n\widehat\mu_n-\psi_n(\widehat\theta_n))
\Bigr]
\\
&\qquad=
 k_ns_n-h(k_n)
 -(n-1)D_{\psi_n}(s_n,\widehat\theta_n)
 -\psi_n(s_n).
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.3}
\]

This identity is the main mathematical output of B12. It shows exactly what was wrong with the previous near-linear comparison. Expanding each ensemble separately creates an apparently dangerous term of size `n sigma^2 s_n^2/2`. In (11.3) the common bulk convex cost cancels. The only remaining background penalty is the Bregman divergence between the cap chord slope `s_n` and the denominator tangent slope `hat theta_n`.

By (8.7),

\[
D_{\psi_n}(s_n,\widehat\theta_n)
\le
C(s_n-\widehat\theta_n)^2.
\tag{11.4}
\]

Furthermore (9.1)-(9.2) and (10.9)-(10.10), together with `K_n/n->t`, `k_n/n->delta`, give

\[
s_n-\widehat\theta_n
=O(L_n^{-(\gamma+1)}).
\tag{11.5}
\]

Hence

\[
(n-1)D_{\psi_n}(s_n,\widehat\theta_n)
=O\left(\frac{n}{L_n^{2\gamma+2}}\right).
\tag{11.6}
\]

This is smaller than the macroscopic chord advantage for every `gamma>0`.

## 12. The macroscopic chord advantage

Using (5.1), (9.1), `K_n/n->t`, and `k_n/n->delta`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
k_n\frac{h(K_n)}{K_n}-h(k_n)
&=
c k_n\left[
(\log K_n)^{-\gamma}
-(\log k_n)^{-\gamma}
\right]
\\
&=
\left[
c\gamma\delta\log(\delta/t)+o(1)
\right]
\frac{n}{L_n^{\gamma+1}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.1}
\]

Because `t<delta`, the coefficient is strictly positive. The small subtraction in (9.2) costs only

\[
k_n\varepsilon_n
=O\left(\frac{n}{L_n^{\gamma+2}}\right),
\tag{12.2}
\]

so

\[
k_ns_n-h(k_n)
=
\left[
c\gamma\delta\log(\delta/t)+o(1)
\right]
\frac{n}{L_n^{\gamma+1}}.
\tag{12.3}
\]

Now compare scales:

\[
\frac{n/L_n^{2\gamma+2}}{n/L_n^{\gamma+1}}
=L_n^{-(\gamma+1)}\to0,
\]

and `log n=o(n/L_n^(gamma+1))`. Also `psi_n(s_n)=O(L_n^-gamma)`.

Finally (9.3) gives

\[
n[\log F_{K_n}(s_n)-\psi_n(s_n)]
=o\left(\frac{n}{L_n^{\gamma+1}}\right).
\tag{12.4}
\]

Combining (10.14), (11.3), (11.6), (12.3), and (12.4), we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
&n[\lambda_ns_n-\log F_{K_n}(s_n)]-B_n
\\
&\qquad\ge
\left[
c\gamma\delta\log(\delta/t)+o(1)
\right]
\frac{n}{L_n^{\gamma+1}}
\longrightarrow+\infty.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.5}
\]

The cap inequality (7.1) therefore gives

\[
\boxed{
P(M_n\le tn\mid S_n=m_n)\to0
}
\tag{12.6}
\]

for **every fixed `t<delta`**.

Thus

\[
\liminf\frac{M_n}{n}\ge\delta
\quad\text{in probability under the conditioning.}
\tag{12.7}
\]

## 13. Matching upper bound

Fix `epsilon>0`. If

\[
M_n>(\delta+\epsilon)n
\]

and `S_n=m_n`, then after deleting the coordinate attaining the maximum, the other `n-1` variables have sum at most

\[
(\nu-\epsilon/2)(n-1)
\]

for all sufficiently large `n`. Since `X>=0`, a standard Chernoff bound using `E exp(-aX)<infinity` gives

\[
P\left(S_{n-1}\le(\nu-\epsilon/2)(n-1)\right)
\le e^{-c_\epsilon n}.
\tag{13.1}
\]

A union bound over the possible maximal coordinate gives

\[
P(S_n=m_n,M_n>(\delta+\epsilon)n)
\le n e^{-c_\epsilon n}.
\tag{13.2}
\]

For the denominator it already suffices to use one giant plus an ordinary local-CLT background at mean: since `h(delta n)=o(n)`,

\[
P(S_n=m_n)\ge e^{-o(n)}.
\tag{13.3}
\]

(Equation (10.14) also implies this.) Dividing (13.2) by (13.3),

\[
\boxed{
P(M_n>(\delta+\epsilon)n\mid S_n=m_n)\to0.
}
\tag{13.4}
\]

Together with (12.6), this proves

\[
M_n/n\xrightarrow{p}\delta.
\]

Under the conditional law `M_n=Y_(1)`. Since

\[
0\le Y_{(1)}/n\le m_n/n=O(1),
\]

the family is uniformly bounded; convergence in probability therefore implies convergence of expectations. This proves (5.2)-(5.4). `square`
