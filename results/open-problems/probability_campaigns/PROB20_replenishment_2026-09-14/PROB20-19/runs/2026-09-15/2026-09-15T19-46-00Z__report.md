## 3. Rank-two Parseval-frame coordinates

Let `P` be a rank-two orthogonal projection. Choose a `p x 2` matrix `Q` with orthonormal columns and `P=QQ^T`. Write the `i`th row of `Q` as
\[
(a_i,b_i),
\]
and define
\[
z_i=a_i+ib_i,
\qquad
w_i=z_i^2,
\qquad
d_i=|z_i|^2=P_{ii}.
\tag{3.1}
\]
Column orthonormality gives
\[
\sum_i a_i^2=\sum_i b_i^2=1,
\qquad
\sum_i a_ib_i=0.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\sum_i d_i=2,
\qquad
\sum_iw_i=0,
\qquad
|w_i|=d_i.
}
\tag{3.2}
\]
Define the two complex frame moments
\[
A=\sum_i d_iw_i,
\qquad
B=\sum_iw_i^2.
\tag{3.3}
\]
The exact rank-two reduction from the recovered checkpoint is
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_2
=16-18S_2^2+30S_4-16|A|^2-|B|^2.
}
\tag{3.4}
\]
The task is to control the two phase-sensitive terms using only (3.2), and then solve the remaining scalar hypersimplex inequality.

## 4. Two sharp-enough zero-sum complex inequalities

### Lemma 4.1

Under (3.2),
\[
\boxed{|A|^2\le 2S_3-S_2^2.}
\tag{4.1}
\]

### Proof

Write `w_i=d_i u_i` with `|u_i|=1` whenever `d_i>0` (the zero terms are irrelevant). Since `sum_i w_i=0`, for any real `c`,
\[
A=\sum_i(d_i-c)w_i
 =\sum_i d_i(d_i-c)u_i.
\]
Weighted Cauchy gives
\[
|A|^2
\le
\left(\sum_i d_i\right)
\left(\sum_i d_i(d_i-c)^2\right).
\]
Because `sum d_i=2`, the second factor is minimized at `c=S_2/2`. Hence
\[
|A|^2
\le2\left(S_3-\frac{S_2^2}{2}\right)
=2S_3-S_2^2.
\]
QED.

### Lemma 4.2

Under (3.2),
\[
\boxed{|B|^2\le2S_3-|A|^2.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

### Proof

Again use `sum_i w_i=0`. For any complex `c`,
\[
B=\sum_iw_i(w_i-c)
 =\sum_i d_i u_i(w_i-c).
\]
Weighted Cauchy yields
\[
|B|^2
\le2\sum_i d_i|w_i-c|^2.
\]
The weighted least-squares minimizer is
\[
c=\frac{\sum_i d_iw_i}{\sum_i d_i}=\frac A2.
\]
Since `|w_i|=d_i`, the minimized weighted variance is
\[
\sum_i d_i|w_i|^2-\frac{|A|^2}{2}
=S_3-\frac{|A|^2}{2}.
\]
Thus (4.2) follows. QED.

Combining the two lemmas,
\[
\begin{aligned}
16|A|^2+|B|^2
&\le15|A|^2+2S_3\\
&\le15(2S_3-S_2^2)+2S_3\\
&=32S_3-15S_2^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3}
\]
Substitution in (3.4) gives the purely scalar lower bound
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_2\ge
F(d):=16-3S_2^2-32S_3+30S_4.
}
\tag{4.4}
\]
It remains to prove `F(d)>=0` on the rank-two hypersimplex
\[
0\le d_i\le1,
\qquad
\sum_i d_i=2.
\tag{4.5}
\]

## 5. Scalar hypersimplex theorem

### Theorem 5.1

For every finite vector `x=(x_i)` satisfying
\[
0\le x_i\le1,
\qquad
\sum_i x_i=2,
\]
we have
\[
\boxed{
16-3\left(\sum_i x_i^2\right)^2
-32\sum_i x_i^3
+30\sum_i x_i^4
\ge0.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]
Equality occurs only for a coordinate vector `(1,1,0,...)` or, up to permutation and appended zeros, `(2/3,2/3,2/3)`.

### 5.1 Boundary with a coordinate equal to one

Zeros can be removed. If two coordinates equal one, the vector is `(1,1,0,...)` and (5.1) is equality.

Suppose exactly one coordinate equals one. Let the remaining nonnegative coordinates be `y_i`, with `sum y_i=1`, and let `e_k` denote their elementary symmetric functions. Direct Newton-identity simplification gives
\[
F(1,y)=24\left(2e_2^2+e_3-5e_4\right).
\tag{5.2}
\]
For nonnegative `y` with `e_1=1`, elementary expansion gives
\[
e_2^2\ge e_1e_3=e_3,
\tag{5.3}
\]
and
\[
e_1e_3\ge4e_4,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad e_3\ge4e_4.
\tag{5.4}
\]
Therefore
\[
2e_2^2+e_3-5e_4
\ge2e_3+e_3-\frac54e_3
=\frac74e_3\ge0.
\tag{5.5}
\]
So every boundary face containing a `1` is settled.

### 5.2 Interior minima have at most two distinct values

Now remove zero coordinates and assume no coordinate is one. At an interior minimum of `F` under `sum x_i=2`, Lagrange multipliers give, for every active coordinate,
\[
120x_i^3-96x_i^2-12S_2x_i=\lambda,
\qquad
S_2=\sum_jx_j^2.
\tag{5.6}
\]
Let
\[
g(t)=120t^3-96t^2-12S_2t.
\]
Then
\[
g'(t)=360t^2-192t-12S_2.
\]
Because the product of the two roots of `g'` is negative, `g'` has exactly one positive root and one negative root. Thus on `(0,1)` the function `g` first decreases and then increases. A horizontal line therefore intersects its graph in at most two points. Hence an interior minimizer has at most two distinct coordinate values.

If all `N` active coordinates are equal, then `x_i=2/N` with `N>=3`, and direct substitution gives
\[
\boxed{
F=\frac{16(N-3)(N-2)(N+5)}{N^3}\ge0.
}
\tag{5.7}
\]
The only noncoordinate equality is `N=3`, namely `(2/3,2/3,2/3)`.

### 5.3 At a two-level interior minimum, one multiplicity is one

Suppose the two distinct values are `a,b in (0,1)` with multiplicities `m,n`. Equality of the two Lagrange equations and division by `a-b` gives
\[
S_2=10(a^2+ab+b^2)-8(a+b).
\tag{5.8}
\]
If both `m,n>=2`, then the constraint `ma+nb=2` implies
\[
a+b\le1,
\]
and
\[
S_2\ge2(a^2+b^2).
\]
But the right side of (5.8) minus `2(a^2+b^2)` equals
\[
8(a+b)(a+b-1)-6ab<0,
\]
a contradiction. Thus one multiplicity is exactly one.

Without loss of generality, the candidate has one coordinate `a` and `n>=2` repeated coordinates
\[
b=\frac{2-a}{n},
\qquad 0\le a\le1.
\tag{5.9}
\]
It remains only to prove `F_n(a)>=0` for this one-parameter family. In fact the inequality holds on the whole family, not only at its stationary points.

### 5.4 The one-plus-`n` family

For `n=2`, direct factorization gives
\[
\boxed{
F_2(a)=3a^2(3a-2)^2\ge0.
}
\tag{5.10}
\]

For `n=3`,
\[
\boxed{
F_3(a)=\frac{8a}{9}
\left(29a^3-30a^2-12a+20\right).
}
\tag{5.11}
\]
Let
\[
h(a)=29a^3-30a^2-12a+20.
\]
Its only critical point in `[0,1]` is
\[
a_*=rac{10+6\sqrt6}{29},
\]
and
\[
h(a_*)=
\frac{11340-2592\sqrt6}{841}>0.
\]
Also `h(0)=20`, `h(1)=7`. Hence `F_3>=0`.

For `n>=4`, set `x=1/n<=1/4`. Exact algebra gives
\[
F_n(a)
=f_\infty(a)-(2-a)^2H_a(x),
\tag{5.12}
\]
where
\[
f_\infty(a)=16-32a^3+27a^4,
\]
\[
H_a(x)=
6a^2x+(3a^2-44a+76)x^2
-30(2-a)^2x^3.
\tag{5.13}
\]
For `0<=a<=1`, `0<=x<=1/4`,
\[
\begin{aligned}
H_a'(x)
&=6a^2+2(3a^2-44a+76)x
-90(2-a)^2x^2\\
&\ge6a^2+x\left(62+2a-\frac{33}{2}a^2\right)\\
&\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.14}
\]
Here we used `x^2<=x/4`; the final quadratic is concave on `[0,1]` and is positive at both endpoints. Thus `H_a(x)` is increasing in `x`, so `F_n(a)` is increasing in `n`. Therefore
\[
F_n(a)\ge F_4(a),
\qquad n\ge4.
\tag{5.15}
\]
Finally
\[
F_4(a)=
\frac3{32}
\left(275a^4-280a^3-120a^2+160a+48\right),
\tag{5.16}
\]
with derivative
\[
F_4'(a)=\frac{15}{8}(5a-2)(11a^2-4a-4).
\]
The only critical points in `[0,1]` are
\[
a=\frac25,
\qquad
a=\frac{2+4\sqrt3}{11}.
\]
At the endpoints and these two critical points,
\[
F_4(0)=\frac92,
\qquad
F_4(1)=\frac{249}{32},
\]
\[
F_4(2/5)=\frac{192}{25},
\]
\[
F_4\!\left(\frac{2+4\sqrt3}{11}\right)
=
\frac{3552+2880\sqrt3}{1331}>0.
\tag{5.17}
\]
So `F_n>0` for every `n>=4`.

Sections 5.1--5.4 exhaust every global-minimum possibility and prove Theorem 5.1.

## 6. Exact closure of `lambda_2` at rank two

Combining (4.4) with Theorem 5.1 gives immediately
\[
\boxed{\lambda_2\ge0}
\tag{6.1}
\]
for every rank-two orthogonal projection.

The inequality is strict for every noncoordinate projection. If `F(d)>0`, this follows already from (4.4). The only noncoordinate equality case of Theorem 5.1 is, up to zeros and permutation,
\[
d=(2/3,2/3,2/3).
\]
Then all three nonzero `w_i` have equal modulus and sum to zero, so they form an equilateral triple. Hence
\[
A=\frac23\sum_iw_i=0,
\qquad
B=\sum_iw_i^2=0.
\]
Substitution in (3.4) gives
\[
\lambda_2
=16-18\left(\frac43\right)^2
+30\left(\frac{16}{27}\right)
=\frac{16}{9}>0.
\tag{6.2}
\]
Thus:

### Theorem 6.1 — rank-two quartic `lambda_2`

For every rank-two orthogonal projection `P`,
\[
\boxed{\lambda_2(P)\ge0,}
\]
and equality holds iff `P` is a coordinate projection.

This closes one of the final two unresolved geometric coefficients at quartic order.

## 7. New exact form of the sole remaining quartic obstruction

Only `lambda_1` remains at rank two. Let
\[
q_{ij}=P_{ij}^2,
\qquad
Q=\sum_{i<j}q_{ij},
\qquad
E=\sum_{i<j}q_{ij}^2.
\]
Because `P^2=P`,
\[
2Q=2-S_2.
\]
Substituting the projection identities into the exact general formula for `lambda_1` gives the new edge representation
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_1
=
\sum_{i<j}q_{ij}\,c_1(d_i,d_j)
+36Q^2+48E,
}
\tag{7.1}
\]
where
\[
\boxed{
c_1(a,b)
=92-206(a+b)+114(a^2+b^2)+144ab.}
\tag{7.2}
\]
The Hessian of `c_1` is positive definite. Its unique global minimizer on `[0,1]^2` is
\[
a=b=\frac{103}{186},
\]
where
\[
\min c_1=-\frac{2053}{93}.
\tag{7.3}
\]
Therefore, dropping the positive `48E` term,
\[
\lambda_1
\ge
-\frac{2053}{93}Q+36Q^2.
\]
If
\[
W:=2-S_2=2Q,
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_1
\ge
W\left(9W-\frac{2053}{186}\right).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]
Thus the remaining coefficient is already settled in the high-mixing regime
\[
\boxed{
W\ge\frac{2053}{1674}\approx1.2264
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lambda_1\ge0.
}
\tag{7.5}
The unresolved rank-two quartic region is therefore confined to `0<W<2053/1674`. A sharper argument must exploit that an edge `q_ij` is small when either endpoint diagonal is close to `0` or `1`; the pointwise minimum (7.3) discards that projection-degree coupling.

## 9. Failed / rejected shortcuts

1. **Using only `|A|<=S_2` or `|B|<=S_2`.** These lose too much. The zero-sum weighted-variance bounds (4.1)--(4.2) are essential.
2. **Assuming the scalar inequality (5.1) from numerics.** It is proved here by a complete compact-minimum/KKT reduction and explicit one-parameter analysis.
3. **Claiming quartic rank two is solved after `lambda_2`.** False: `I_1>0`, and `lambda_1` remains sign-unresolved for part of the rank-two projection space.
4. **Using the global minimum of `c_1` to claim all `lambda_1>=0`.** It only proves the high-mixing regime (7.5). Near coordinate projections, one must couple `q_ij` to the endpoint variances rather than minimize `c_1` independently.
5. **Summing the positive-spike series from four coefficients.** Even complete quartic control would not settle finite spike strength because all `M_k`, `k>=5`, remain.

## 10. Next attack

The next exact obligation is now singular: prove `lambda_1>=0` for every rank-two projection.

The most promising route is to combine the edge representation (7.1) with the exact degree identity
\[
\sum_{j\ne i}q_{ij}=d_i(1-d_i).
\tag{10.1}
\]
The negative region of `c_1(a,b)` occurs only away from the corners, while the edge weight `q_ij` is bounded by each incident degree `d_i(1-d_i)`. A useful target is a pair inequality of the form
\[
q_{ij}\,c_1(d_i,d_j)
\ge
-\Phi_i(q_{ij})-\Phi_j(q_{ij})
\]
whose vertex sums can be absorbed by the positive global terms `36Q^2+48E`.

A second route is the rank-two Plücker representation. If `delta_ij` is the squared `2x2` minor of rows `i,j`, then
\[
q_{ij}+\delta_{ij}=d_id_j,
\qquad
\sum_{j\ne i}\delta_{ij}=d_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}=1.
\]
Rewriting `lambda_1` in the nonnegative edge variables `delta_ij` may expose a four-index sum-of-squares structure; the fact that `lambda_1` vanishes identically for rank two in ambient dimension three strongly suggests that its true obstruction is intrinsically four-index / Plücker in nature.

If `lambda_1` closes, then `M_4` is exactly coordinate-maximized for **every projection rank**, providing the first four complete moment coefficients (`M_0` through `M_4`) in the positive two-eigenvalue expansion.
