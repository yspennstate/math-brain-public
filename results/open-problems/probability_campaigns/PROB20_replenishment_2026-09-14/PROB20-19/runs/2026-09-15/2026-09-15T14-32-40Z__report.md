## 3. Positive two-eigenvalue expansion

\[
C=aI+cP,
\qquad a>0,\ c\ge0,
\qquad P^2=P=P^T,
\qquad \operatorname{rank}P=r,
\]
the exact sphere representation from the archived work gives, up to a positive orientation-independent factor,
\[
J_\kappa(P)
=\mathbb E_\theta\left[
 f_d(\theta)(1-\kappa\theta^TP\theta)^{-\alpha}
\right],
\qquad
\alpha=\frac{p+2}{2},
\quad
\kappa=\frac{c}{a+c}.
\]
The binomial expansion has positive coefficients:
\[
J_\kappa(P)
=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(\alpha)_k}{k!}\kappa^kM_k(P),
\qquad
M_k(P)=\mathbb E[f_d(\theta)(\theta^TP\theta)^k].
\]
Cumulative exact status before this run:

- `M_3`: coordinate projection uniquely maximizes for every rank.

## 4. Quartic moment types

Put
\[
X_i=\theta_i^2.

For `p>=4`, permutation symmetry leaves five degree-four moment types:
\[
A=\mathbb E[f_dX_1^4],
\qquad

C=\mathbb E[f_dX_1^2X_2^2],
\qquad
D=\mathbb E[f_dX_1^2X_2X_3],

d_i=P_{ii},

S_k=\sum_i d_i^k,

\[

\qquad
T_4=\sum_{i<j}P_{ij}^4.
\tag{4.2}

## 5. Exact sign-parity expansion of `M_4`

Write
\[
Q=\theta^TP\theta
=\sum_i d_iX_i
+2\sum_{i<j}P_{ij}\theta_i\theta_j.
\]
Because `f_d` is invariant under independent coordinate sign flips, only monomials in which every coordinate appears with even total degree survive in `E[f_d Q^4]`.

A complete expansion gives
\[
\boxed{
M_4(P)=A W_4+B W_{31}+C W_{22}+D W_{211}+E W_{1111},
}
\tag{5.1}
\]
where
\[
W_4=S_4,
\tag{5.2}
\]
\[
W_{31}=4(r+6)S_3-28S_4,
\tag{5.3}
\]
\[
W_{22}=3(S_2^2-S_4)+48U+16T_4,
\tag{5.4}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
W_{211}={}&-18S_2^2+6r^2S_2+60rS_2+144S_2\\
&-60rS_3-360S_3+228S_4-288U-96T_4,
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.5}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
W_{1111}={}&r^4+12r^3+44r^2+48r\\
&-18r^2S_2-180rS_2-432S_2+27S_2^2\\
&+120rS_3+720S_3-342S_4\\
&+432U+144T_4.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{5.6}
\]

### 5.1 Derivation details

Let
\[
D_0=\sum_i d_iX_i,
\qquad
O_0=2\sum_{i<j}P_{ij}\theta_i\theta_j.
\]
Then after sign averaging,
\[
Q^4=D_0^4+6D_0^2\,\mathbb E_{\rm signs}O_0^2
+4D_0\,\mathbb E_{\rm signs}O_0^3
+\mathbb E_{\rm signs}O_0^4.
\]
The off-diagonal sign averages are
\[
\mathbb E_{\rm signs}O_0^2
=4\sum_{i<j}P_{ij}^2X_iX_j,
\]
\[
\mathbb E_{\rm signs}O_0^3
=48\sum_{i<j<k}P_{ij}P_{jk}P_{ki}X_iX_jX_k,
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{\rm signs}O_0^4
={}&16\sum_e P_e^4X_e^2
+96\sum_{e<f}P_e^2P_f^2X_eX_f\\
&+384\sum_{\text{undirected 4-cycles}}
P_{ij}P_{jk}P_{k\ell}P_{\ell i}X_iX_jX_kX_\ell.
\end{aligned}
\]
The projection identities used to reduce these graph sums are
\[
\sum_{j\ne i}P_{ij}^2=d_i-d_i^2,
\tag{5.7}
\]
\[
\sum_{i<j}(d_i+d_j)P_{ij}^2=S_2-S_3,
\tag{5.8}
\]
\[
\sum_{i<j}(d_i^2+d_j^2)P_{ij}^2=S_3-S_4,
\tag{5.9}
\]
\[
\sum_{i<j<k}(d_i+d_j+d_k)P_{ij}P_{jk}P_{ki}
=\frac{S_2-2S_3+S_4}{2}-U,
\tag{5.10}
\]
and, for the off-diagonal matrix `O=P-diag(d)`,
\[
\operatorname{tr}(O^4)
=r-4S_2+4S_3-S_4+4U.
\tag{5.11}
\]
Equations (5.2)–(5.6) follow by collecting the five exponent partitions `4`, `3+1`, `2+2`, `2+1+1`, and `1+1+1+1`.

A symbolic/numeric checker is archived separately in this run.

## 6. Four strict `Top_d` moment contrasts

The quartic gap simplifies dramatically in the following basis:
\[
I_1=D-3E,
\qquad
I_2=C-3D,
\qquad
I_3=B-5D,
\qquad
I_4=A-4B-5C.
\tag{6.1}
\]

### Lemma 6.1

For every `1<=d<p` in the relevant dimensions,
\[
\boxed{I_1>0,\quad I_2>0,\quad I_3>0,\quad I_4>0.}
\tag{6.2}
\]

### Proof

The same pair-conditioning mechanism proves all four inequalities.

For any pair of squared coordinates `x,y`, let
\[
S=x+y,
\qquad
q=\frac{|x-y|}{S}=|\cos2\phi|.
\]
Conditional on `S` and all outside squared coordinates, the angle `phi` is uniform and
\[
\mathbb E[q^2]=\frac12.
\]
For fixed conditioning data, `f_d` is a nondecreasing function of `q`: making two coordinates more unequal at fixed sum cannot decrease the sum of the largest `d` coordinates. Hence for every nonnegative outside weight `W` independent of the pair angle,
\[
\mathbb E\left[f_d W S^m(q^2-1/2)\right]\ge0,
\tag{6.3}
\]
with strict inequality whenever `W>0` on a positive-probability region where `f_d` actually changes with `q`.

The four contrasts are exactly of this form:
\[
D-3E
=\mathbb E\left[f_dX_3X_4S^2(q^2-1/2)\right],
\tag{6.4}
\]
using the pair `(X_1,X_2)`;
\[
C-3D
=\mathbb E\left[f_dX_2^2S^2(q^2-1/2)\right],
\tag{6.5}
\]
using the pair `(X_1,X_3)`;
\[
B-5D
=\mathbb E\left[f_dX_2S^3(q^2-1/2)\right],
\tag{6.6}
\]
and
\[
A-4B-5C
=\mathbb E\left[f_dS^4(q^2-1/2)\right].
\tag{6.7}
\]
For (6.7), the elementary identity is
\[
\frac{x^4+y^4}{2}
-4\frac{x^3y+xy^3}{2}
-5x^2y^2
=S^4(q^2-1/2).
\]
Strictness follows by restricting to an open region where exactly `d-1` outside squared coordinates are above the active pair and the remaining outside coordinates are below it, so that `f_d=constant+max(x,y)`.

## 7. Exact positive-contrast decomposition of the quartic gap

For a coordinate rank-`r` projection, `S_2=S_3=S_4=r` and `U=T_4=0`. Define
\[
\Delta_k=r-S_k,
\qquad k=2,3,4.
\]
Subtracting (5.1) from the coordinate value and changing basis from `(A,B,C,D,E)` to `(I_1,I_2,I_3,I_4)` gives the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
M_4(P_{coord})-M_4(P)
=\lambda_1I_1+\lambda_2I_2+\lambda_3I_3+\lambda_4I_4,
}
\tag{7.1}
\]
where
\[
\boxed{\lambda_4=\Delta_4,}
\tag{7.2}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_3=4\left[r^2-(r+6)S_3+6S_4\right]
=4\left[(r+6)\Delta_3-6\Delta_4\right],
}
\tag{7.3}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_2
=3(r^2-S_2^2)+2\Delta_4-16(T_4+3U),
}
\tag{7.4}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_1={}&6(r+4)(r+6)\Delta_2
-40(r+6)\Delta_3+114\Delta_4\\
&-9(r^2-S_2^2)+48(T_4+3U).
\end{aligned}}
\tag{7.5}
\]

This decomposition is the central new structural fact of the run. At quartic order a genuine non-diagonal projection invariant appears through `T_4+3U`; nevertheless it enters only the two geometric coefficients `lambda_1,lambda_2`, while every analytic `Top_d` coefficient is strictly positive.

## 8. Geometric positivity for sufficiently large rank

We now prove
\[
\lambda_j\ge0
\]
for all four coefficients once `r>=9`.

### 8.1 `lambda_4`

Since `0<=d_i<=1`,
\[
\Delta_4=\sum_i(d_i-d_i^4)\ge0,
\]
with equality iff every `d_i` is `0` or `1`, i.e. iff the projection is coordinate.

### 8.2 `lambda_3` for `r>=3`

Using `sum_i d_i=r`,
\[
\frac{\lambda_3}{4}
=\sum_i\left[rd_i-(r+6)d_i^3+6d_i^4\right]
\]
\[
=\sum_i d_i(1-d_i)\left[r(1+d_i)-6d_i^2\right].
\tag{8.1}
\]
For `r>=3`, the bracket is nonnegative on `[0,1]`: it is a concave quadratic in `d`, so its minimum is at an endpoint, where the values are `r` and `2r-6`. Thus
\[
\lambda_3\ge0.
\tag{8.2}
\]

### 8.3 `lambda_2` for `r>=6`

Let
\[
s_i=\sum_{j\ne i}P_{ij}^2=d_i-d_i^2.
\]
Then
\[
\sum_i s_i^2
=S_2-2S_3+S_4
=2T_4+2\sum_i\sum_{j<k\atop j,k\ne i}P_{ij}^2P_{ik}^2,
\]
so
\[
T_4\le\frac{S_2-2S_3+S_4}{2}.
\tag{8.3}
\]
Also, since `d_id_j<=(d_i+d_j)/2`,
\[
U\le\frac12\sum_{i<j}(d_i+d_j)P_{ij}^2
=\frac{S_2-S_3}{2}.
\tag{8.4}
\]
Therefore
\[
16(T_4+3U)
\le32S_2-40S_3+8S_4.
\tag{8.5}
\]
Substituting into (7.4),
\[
\lambda_2\ge
3(r^2-S_2^2)+2r-32S_2+40S_3-10S_4.
\tag{8.6}
\]
Since `r^2-S_2^2=Delta_2(r+S_2)`, the right side equals
\[
\sum_i d_i(1-d_i)
\left[
3(r+S_2)+2(5d_i^2-15d_i+1)
\right].
\tag{8.7}
\]
The quadratic `2(5x^2-15x+1)` is decreasing on `[0,1]` and has minimum `-18`. Thus for `r>=6`, every bracket in (8.7) is nonnegative, so
\[
\lambda_2\ge0.
\tag{8.8}
\]

### 8.4 `lambda_1` for `r>=9`

Set
\[
V=T_4+3U\ge0.
\]
From (7.5), drop the positive term `48V`, use
\[
\Delta_4\ge\Delta_3,
\qquad
\Delta_3\le2\Delta_2,
\qquad
r^2-S_2^2=\Delta_2(r+S_2)\le2r\Delta_2.
\]
Because the coefficient of `Delta_3` after replacing `114Delta_4` by `114Delta_3` is negative,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_1
&\ge6(r+4)(r+6)\Delta_2
-(40r+126)\Delta_3
-18r\Delta_2\\
&\ge
\left[6(r+4)(r+6)-2(40r+126)-18r\right]\Delta_2\\
&=
(6r^2-38r-108)\Delta_2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.9}
\]
For every integer `r>=9`,
\[
6r^2-38r-108>0.
\]
Hence
\[
\lambda_1\ge0
\]
for `r>=9`, strictly for every noncoordinate projection.

## 9. New quartic theorem

Combining Lemma 6.1 and Section 8:

### Theorem 9.1 — quartic KL alignment for all projection ranks `r>=9`

For every `p>r>=9`, every `1<=d<p`, and every rank-`r` orthogonal projection `P`,
\[
\boxed{
M_4(P)\le M_4(P_{coord}).
}
\tag{9.1}
\]
If `P` is noncoordinate, the inequality is strict.

The rank-one case is already stronger in the cumulative archive: the positive one-spike theorem gives exact finite-strength KL optimality and therefore all moment coefficients. Thus the cumulative quartic status is:

- rank `r=1`: solved to all orders by the positive one-spike theorem;
- ranks `r>=9`: `M_4` solved by Theorem 9.1;
- ranks `2<=r<=8`: quartic coefficient still open.

This does not imply finite-strength two-eigenvalue optimality for `r>=9`, because `M_k`, `k>=5`, are not controlled.

## 10. What the quartic calculation reveals

The quartic order is the first place where the moment coefficient does **not** collapse to diagonal power sums alone. The new invariant
\[
T_4+3U
=\sum_{i<j}P_{ij}^4
+3\sum_{i<j}d_id_jP_{ij}^2
\]
survives after all projection identities are used.

This is important for strategy selection:

- a naive induction using only Schur–Horn majorization of the diagonal cannot control all higher coefficients;
- nevertheless, the quartic gap still admits a positive analytic contrast basis, reducing the problem to geometric inequalities for projection invariants;
- the high-rank theorem shows that these geometric inequalities can dominate the new cycle/edge information in a substantial regime.

The remaining low ranks `2,...,8` are finite geometric cases for the quartic coefficient and are now sharply isolated.

## 11. Failed / rejected shortcuts

1. **Assume the new invariant has a favorable coefficient.** Not valid. In the raw quartic formula the coefficient of `T_4+3U` is proportional to `C-6D+9E`, whose sign is not established and need not be assumed.

2. **Use only positivity of `D-3E` and `C-3D` separately.** The exact change of basis in (7.1) is required; otherwise the uncertain sign above obscures the gap.

3. **Claim all ranks from the large-r bounds.** The estimates in Section 8 deliberately lose information. They prove `r>=9`; they do not prove ranks `2,...,8`.

4. **Treat quartic coefficient optimality as finite-spike optimality.** Invalid. The positive binomial expansion has infinitely many higher coefficients.
