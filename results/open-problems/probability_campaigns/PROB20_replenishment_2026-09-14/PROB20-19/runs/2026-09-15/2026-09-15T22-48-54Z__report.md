## 3. Parseval-frame occupancy law recalled

Let `P` be a rank-`r` orthogonal projection in `R^p`. Write

\[
P=QQ^T,
\qquad Q^TQ=I_r,
\]

and let `a_i in R^r` be the rows of `Q`. Then

\[
\sum_i a_i a_i^T=I_r,
\qquad
 d_i:=\|a_i\|^2=P_{ii},
\qquad
0\le d_i\le1,
\qquad
\sum_i d_i=r.
\tag{3.1}
\]

If `U` is uniform on `S^{r-1}`, define

\[
p_i(U)=(a_i\cdot U)^2.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Parseval gives

\[
\sum_i p_i(U)=1.
\]

The preceding checkpoint proved that, if `k` iid categorical samples are drawn conditional on the random probability vector `p(U)`, then the sorted occupancy-count partition has exactly the geometric partition law `pi_P` arising in the spherical moment `M_k(P)`.

For `k=5`, the partitions in dominance order are

\[
(5)\succ(4,1)\succ(3,2)\succ(3,1,1)
\succ(2,2,1)\succ(2,1,1,1)\succ(1^5).
\tag{3.3}
\]

## 4. The top-four quintic cumulative event has a one-coordinate formula

Let `N_i` denote the occupancy count of category `i` among five iid samples.

The first four partitions in (3.3) are exactly those for which

\[
\max_i N_i\ge3.
\tag{4.1}
\]

For five samples, the events `{N_i>=3}` are pairwise disjoint: two distinct categories cannot each occur at least three times because that would require at least six samples.

Therefore, conditional on a deterministic probability vector `p`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr\{\max_iN_i\ge3\mid p\}
&=\sum_i\Pr\{N_i\ge3\mid p\}\\
&=\sum_i\left[
10p_i^3(1-p_i)^2+5p_i^4(1-p_i)+p_i^5
\right]\\
&=\boxed{\sum_i\left(10p_i^3-15p_i^4+6p_i^5\right).}
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This is the key simplification: the middle cumulative probability contains no cross-coordinate terms after the occupancy interpretation is used.

## 6. Scalar chord inequality

Divide (5.2) by `15` and define

\[
\Phi_r(x)
:=10(r+6)(r+8)x^3
-105(r+8)x^4
+378x^5.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Then

\[
\Phi_r(1)=10r^2+35r+18.
\tag{6.2}
\]

A direct factorization gives

\[
\boxed{
 x\Phi_r(1)-\Phi_r(x)
=x(1-x)Q_r(x),
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

where

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
Q_r(x)={}&10r^2(1+x)
+r(35+35x-105x^2)\\
&+18+18x-462x^2+378x^3.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

We now prove `Q_r(x)>=27` for every `r>=3` and `0<=x<=1`.

First,

\[
\frac{\partial Q_r(x)}{\partial r}
=20r(1+x)+35+35x-105x^2.
\tag{6.5}
\]

For `r>=3`, this is at least

\[
95+95x-105x^2.
\]

The latter is concave on `[0,1]`, so its minimum is at an endpoint; the endpoint values are `95` and `85`. Hence `Q_r(x)` is strictly increasing in `r` on the entire relevant domain.

It therefore suffices to check `r=3`. In that case

\[
Q_3(x)=3(126x^3-259x^2+71x+71).
\tag{6.6}
\]

Moreover

\[
126x^3-259x^2+71x+71-9
=(x-1)(126x^2-133x-62).
\tag{6.7}
\]

The quadratic `126x^2-133x-62` is convex and has endpoint values `-62` and `-69`, hence is negative throughout `[0,1]`. Because `x-1<=0`, (6.7) is nonnegative. Therefore

\[
126x^3-259x^2+71x+71\ge9,
\]
so

\[
\boxed{Q_r(x)\ge Q_3(x)\ge27.}
\tag{6.8}
\]

Combining (6.3) and (6.8),

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_r(x)\le x\Phi_r(1)-27x(1-x)
\qquad(r\ge3).
}
\tag{6.9}
\]

## 7. Universal quintic top-four stochastic facet

Apply (6.9) to the projection diagonal entries and use `sum_i d_i=r`:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i\Phi_r(d_i)
&\le
\Phi_r(1)\sum_i d_i
-27\sum_i d_i(1-d_i)\\
&=
r\Phi_r(1)
-27(r-S_2).
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Multiplying by `15` and dividing by `T_{r,5}` gives the quantitative probability gap

\[
\boxed{
\pi_{\rm coord}\{(5),(4,1),(3,2),(3,1,1)\}
-
\pi_P\{(5),(4,1),(3,2),(3,1,1)\}
\ge
\frac{405(r-S_2)}{T_{r,5}}
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

for every `r>=3`.

Since

\[
r-S_2=\sum_i d_i(1-d_i)\ge0,
\]
we obtain:

### Theorem 7.1 — universal middle quintic occupancy facet

For every rank `r>=3` orthogonal projection `P`,

\[
\boxed{
\pi_P\{(5),(4,1),(3,2),(3,1,1)\}
\le
\pi_{\rm coord}\{(5),(4,1),(3,2),(3,1,1)\}.
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

The inequality is strict for every noncoordinate projection.

Indeed `r-S_2=0` implies every `d_i` is `0` or `1`; an orthogonal projection with a `0/1` diagonal is diagonal, hence coordinate up to permutation.

For rank `r=2`, the coordinate law has only two categories, so with five draws the pigeonhole principle forces some occupancy count to be at least three; its top-four cumulative probability is exactly one. Thus (7.3) is trivially true also for `r=2`.

Therefore the top-four quintic cumulative facet is proved for **every nontrivial projection rank**.

## 8. All-orders theorem: coordinate measurement minimizes expected support size

The occupancy representation also gives a simple all-orders statistic that was not recorded in the preceding checkpoint.

Let `K_k` be the number of distinct categories observed in `k` iid samples from `p(U)`.

Conditional on `U`,

\[
\mathbb E[K_k\mid U]
=\sum_i\left[1-(1-p_i(U))^k\right].
\tag{8.1}
\]

For a fixed row of squared norm `d`, rotational invariance gives

\[
p_i(U)\stackrel{d}=dX,
\qquad
X=U_1^2.
\]

Define

\[
g_{r,k}(d)
:=1-\mathbb E(1-dX)^k.
\tag{8.2}
\]

For every `k>=2`,

\[
g_{r,k}''(d)
=-k(k-1)\mathbb E\left[X^2(1-dX)^{k-2}\right]\le0.
\tag{8.3}
\]

Thus `g_{r,k}` is concave on `[0,1]` and `g_{r,k}(0)=0`. Concavity implies the chord inequality

\[
g_{r,k}(d)\ge d\,g_{r,k}(1).
\tag{8.4}
\]

Summing over the projection diagonal,

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E K_k(P)
\ge
r\,g_{r,k}(1)
=
\mathbb E K_k(P_{\rm coord}).
}
\tag{8.5}
\]

For `r>=2`, since `X~Beta(1/2,(r-1)/2)`,

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E K_k(P_{\rm coord})
=r\left[
1-
\frac{((r-1)/2)_k}{(r/2)_k}
\right].
}
\tag{8.6}
\]

For `k>=2`, strict concavity gives equality only when every diagonal entry is `0` or `1`, hence only for coordinate projections.

### Theorem 8.1 — support-size first moment

For every `k>=2` and every projection rank, the coordinate/projective Parseval frame uniquely minimizes the expected number of distinct outcomes in the exact occupancy law `pi_P`.

This theorem does **not** by itself imply stochastic dominance of the full support count; it is recorded as a new all-orders constraint that future attacks on the remaining quintic facets can use.
