## 4. Exact `S_4` invariant reduction

On the sum-zero hyperplane `E`, define the basic power-sum invariants
\[
q_2=\sum_i z_i^2,\qquad q_3=\sum_i z_i^3,\qquad q_4=\sum_i z_i^4.
\tag{4.1}
\]

The symmetric polynomial ring restricted to `E` is generated by `q_2,q_3,q_4`. Indeed Newton's identities give
\[
e_2=-\frac12q_2,\qquad e_3=\frac13q_3,\qquad e_4=\frac18q_2^2-\frac14q_4.
\tag{4.2}
\]

For a finite reflection group, an invariant analytic germ is an analytic function of a system of basic polynomial invariants. Therefore, after shrinking the neighborhood if necessary,
\[
\boxed{\Phi(z,\lambda)=\Psi(q_2(z),q_3(z),q_4(z),\lambda)}
\tag{4.3}
\]
for a real-analytic `Psi`.

This can also be seen coefficientwise: the convergent Taylor series of `Phi` is termwise symmetric, and each homogeneous symmetric polynomial on `E` is a polynomial in `q_2,q_3,q_4`.

Write
\[
\Psi_2=\partial_{q_2}\Psi,\qquad \Psi_3=\partial_{q_3}\Psi,\qquad \Psi_4=\partial_{q_4}\Psi.
\tag{4.4}
\]

Then an ambient representative of the gradient is
\[
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z_i}=2\Psi_2 z_i+3\Psi_3 z_i^2+4\Psi_4 z_i^3.
\tag{4.5}
\]

Because the optimization is constrained to `E`, `z` is critical iff these four components are all equal. Equivalently there exists a scalar `eta` such that every coordinate `z_i` is a root of
\[
\boxed{P(t)=4\Psi_4 t^3+3\Psi_3 t^2+2\Psi_2 t-\eta.}
\tag{4.6}
\]

The coefficients in (4.6) are evaluated at the common invariant triple `(q_2,q_3,q_4)` of the critical point, so they are the same for all four coordinates.

This cubic-root observation is the key to excluding hidden symmetry types.

## 6. New theorem: no three-level or four-level critical points nearby

### Theorem 6.1

After shrinking `rho_0,epsilon_0` if necessary, every nonzero critical point of `Phi` in
\[
\|z\|<\rho_0,\qquad |\lambda-\lambda_*|<\varepsilon_0
\tag{6.1}
\]
has exactly two distinct coordinate values.

### Proof

Let
\[
A=2\Psi_2,\qquad B=3\Psi_3,\qquad C=4\Psi_4.
\tag{6.2}
\]
At a critical point all coordinates solve
\[
Ct^3+Bt^2+At-\eta=0.
\tag{6.3}
\]

Four distinct coordinate values are impossible because (6.3) has degree at most three.

Suppose there are three distinct coordinate values `r,s,t`.

If `C=0`, then (6.3) is at most quadratic. Since `B>=b>0`, it cannot have three distinct roots. Thus `C!=0`.

For three distinct roots of the cubic, Vieta gives
\[
r+s+t=-\frac{B}{C}.
\tag{6.4}
\]
Equivalently, subtracting the pairwise criticality factors gives the same identity:
\[
Q(r,s)-Q(r,t)=(s-t)\,[B+C(r+s+t)],
\tag{6.5}
\]
where
\[
Q(x,y)=A+B(x+y)+C(x^2+xy+y^2).
\]

By (5.7)-(5.8),
\[
\left|\frac{B}{C}\right|\ge \frac{b}{M}
\tag{6.6}
\]
whenever `C!=0`.

Choose
\[
\rho<\frac{b}{3M}
\tag{6.7}
\]
when `M>0` (if `M=0`, the quadratic case already finishes the proof). If every coordinate has magnitude below `rho`, then
\[
|r+s+t|<3\rho<\frac{b}{M},
\tag{6.8}
\]
contradicting (6.4)-(6.6).

Therefore no critical point in the sufficiently small neighborhood can have three distinct coordinate values. Together with the cubic degree bound, it has at most two.

A nonzero sum-zero vector cannot have only one coordinate value, so every nonzero critical point has exactly two distinct values. QED.

## 7. Two coordinate values force the `3+1` or `2+2` symmetry types

Let a nonzero critical point have values `a` and `b`, with multiplicities `k` and `4-k`.

The constraint
\[
ka+(4-k)b=0
\tag{7.1}
\]
leaves only
\[
k=1,2,3.
\]

- `k=1` or `k=3` is the `3+1` fixed-point axis, up to permutation.

\boxed{\text{Every sufficiently small nonzero critical point is symmetry-equivalent to a point on the `3+1` or `2+2` axis.}}

This is stronger than merely invoking the equivariant branching lemma: it excludes all lower-symmetry local critical branches directly from the exact invariant gradient equation.

In particular, there is no nearby `2+1+1` branch and no all-distinct branch.

## 9. Complete local critical-set theorem

Combining Sections 6-8 gives the main result of this run.

### Theorem 9.1 — local critical-set completeness

There exist `rho>0` and `epsilon>0` such that, whenever
\[
\|z\|<\rho,\qquad |\lambda-\lambda_*|<\epsilon,
\tag{9.1}
\]
every critical point of
\[
z\mapsto \Phi(z,\lambda)
\]
is one of the following:

1. the balanced point `z=0`;
2. one of the permutation images of the unique small `3+1` branch;
3. when `kappa(lambda)<0`, one of the permutation images of the unique small `2+2` branch.

There are no other critical points in this neighborhood.

### Morse-type consequences

- If `kappa(lambda)<0`, the balanced point has negative-definite Hessian and is a strict local maximum.
- If `kappa(lambda)>0`, the balanced point has positive-definite Hessian and is a strict local minimum.
- At `lambda=lambda_*`, the balanced point is a degenerate saddle because the cubic invariant is nonzero.
- Every nearby nonzero `3+1` critical point is a saddle.
- Every nearby nonzero `2+2` critical point is a saddle.

Therefore:
\[
\boxed{\text{For }\lambda>\lambda_*\text{ sufficiently close to }\lambda_*,\text{ there is no local maximum at all in a fixed neighborhood of }u_*.}
\tag{9.2}
\]

And:
\[
\boxed{\text{For }\lambda<\lambda_*\text{ sufficiently close to }\lambda_*,\text{ the balanced point is the only local maximum in that neighborhood.}}
\tag{9.3}
\]

So the balanced non-KL local-max branch does not hand off to an infinitesimally nearby symmetry-breaking local-max branch when it loses stability.

## 10. Why this matters for the universal conjecture

The earlier landscape work showed that non-KL stationary points and even non-KL local maxima exist. Thus a proof of the universal conjecture cannot rely on stationarity uniqueness, generic gradient ascent, or the assertion that every non-KL critical point has an ascent direction.

The present theorem removes the most obvious local escape mechanism at the first balanced bifurcation:

- `3+1` branch: saddle;
- `2+2` branch: saddle;
- `2+1+1` branch: cannot occur sufficiently close;
- all-distinct branch: cannot occur sufficiently close.

Any non-KL local maximum surviving for `lambda>lambda_*` must therefore lie a **finite distance away from the balanced point** rather than being born locally at its first loss of stability.

This converts the next search from a local normal-form problem into a genuinely global orbit problem.
