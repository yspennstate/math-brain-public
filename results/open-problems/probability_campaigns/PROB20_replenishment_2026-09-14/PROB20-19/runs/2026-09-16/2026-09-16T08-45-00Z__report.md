# PROB20-19 — Full nonlinear Gaussian PCA conjecture

**SOLUTION STATUS: PARTIAL.** The universal Mallat–Zeitouni/Bandeira conjecture is not solved in this run. The new result is a rigorous landscape obstruction that is directly relevant to proof strategy: for `p=4,d=2`, one-small-eigenvalue spectra have **strict suboptimal non-KL local maxima** once the small eigenvalue is sufficiently close to zero. Thus the stronger hoped-for statement “every non-KL stationary point has an ascent direction” is false, even for positive-definite Gaussian covariances. This does **not** refute KL global optimality; the local maxima constructed here are rigorously below the KL value for sufficiently small positive fourth eigenvalue.

## 3. The negative one-spike orbit used here

Scale the large eigenvalue to one. For `0 <= lambda < 1` and a unit vector `u in S^3`, define

\[
C_{\lambda,u}
=I_4-(1-\lambda)uu^T.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Its spectrum is

\[
(1,1,1,\lambda).
\]

For `lambda>0` this is positive definite. A KL covariance on this orbit is obtained when `u` is a coordinate vector. The orbit is naturally parameterized by projective spike directions because `u` and `-u` give the same covariance.

This run studies

\[
\mathcal F_\lambda(u)
:=F_2(C_{\lambda,u})
=\mathbb E\operatorname{Top}_2(X_1^2,X_2^2,X_3^2,X_4^2).
\tag{3.2}
\]

The singular endpoint `lambda=0` is not itself part of the positive-definite target, but it is an analytically tractable boundary point from which positive-definite local maxima can be forced by uniform perturbation.

Let

\[
u_*:=\frac12(1,1,1,1)^T.
\tag{3.3}
\]

The central theorem below is that `u_*` is a strict local maximum at `lambda=0` and therefore positive-definite non-KL local maxima persist nearby for all sufficiently small `lambda>0`.

## 4. A Hadamard identity for `p=4,d=2`

Introduce the orthogonal Hadamard coordinates

\[
\begin{aligned}
D&=\frac{x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4}{2},\\
A&=\frac{x_1+x_2-x_3-x_4}{2},\\
B&=\frac{x_1-x_2+x_3-x_4}{2},\\
C&=\frac{x_1-x_2-x_3+x_4}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Then

\[
T:=x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2+x_4^2=D^2+A^2+B^2+C^2.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Direct expansion gives the six pair energies in three complementary pairs:

\[
\begin{aligned}
x_1^2+x_2^2 &= \frac T2 +(DA+BC),\\
x_3^2+x_4^2 &= \frac T2 -(DA+BC),\\
x_1^2+x_3^2 &= \frac T2 +(DB+AC),\\
x_2^2+x_4^2 &= \frac T2 -(DB+AC),\\
x_1^2+x_4^2 &= \frac T2 +(DC+AB),\\
x_2^2+x_3^2 &= \frac T2 -(DC+AB).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Therefore the exact top-two identity is

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Top}_2(x_1^2,x_2^2,x_3^2,x_4^2)
=
\frac T2+
\max\{|DA+BC|,|DB+AC|,|DC+AB|\}.
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

This identity is the key simplification at the singular balanced hyperplane.

## 5. Exact curvature at the balanced singular hyperplane

At `lambda=0` and `u=u_*`, the Gaussian is standard isotropic Gaussian restricted to the hyperplane

\[
D=0.
\]

Choose the unit tangent vector

\[
h:=\frac12(1,1,-1,-1)^T,
\qquad h\perp u_*.
\tag{5.1}
\]

This is precisely the `A` Hadamard axis. Consider the geodesic

\[
u_\theta=u_*\cos\theta+h\sin\theta.
\tag{5.2}
\]

An orthonormal basis for `u_\theta^\perp` is

\[
v_\theta=h\cos\theta-u_*\sin\theta,
\quad B\text{-axis},
\quad C\text{-axis}.
\]

Hence, for independent standard normals `X,Y,Z`, a sample from `N(0,C_{0,u_\theta})` has fixed Hadamard coordinates

\[
D=-Y\sin\theta,
\qquad
A=Y\cos\theta,
\qquad
B=X,
\qquad
C=Z.
\tag{5.3}
\]

The total energy

\[
T=X^2+Y^2+Z^2
\]

is independent of `theta`. Thus all orientation dependence in (4.4) is in

\[
m_\theta
:=\max\{|q_1(\theta)|,|q_2(\theta)|,|q_3(\theta)|\},
\tag{5.4}
\]

where

\[
\begin{aligned}
q_1(\theta)&=XZ-Y^2\sin\theta\cos\theta,\\
q_2(\theta)&=Y(Z\cos\theta-X\sin\theta),\\
q_3(\theta)&=Y(X\cos\theta-Z\sin\theta).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.5}
\]

At `theta=0`,

\[
|q_1|=|XZ|,
\qquad
|q_2|=|YZ|,
\qquad
|q_3|=|YX|.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Thus the active branch is exactly the product of the two largest among `|X|,|Y|,|Z|`.

Define

\[
M:=\max\{|XY|,|XZ|,|YZ|\}.
\tag{5.7}
\]

### 5.1 Interior second derivatives

Away from zeroes and ties, differentiation of the active absolute-value branch gives

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{active branch at }\theta=0
&\text{equivalent smallest absolute variable}
&\text{second derivative at }0\\
\hline
|q_1|=|XZ| & |Y|\text{ smallest} & 0,\\
|q_2|=|YZ| & |X|\text{ smallest} & -|YZ|,\\
|q_3|=|YX| & |Z|\text{ smallest} & -|YX|.
\end{array}
\tag{5.8}
\]

Indeed `q_1''(0)=0`, while `q_2''(0)=-q_2(0)` and `q_3''(0)=-q_3(0)`.

### 5.2 The switching-boundary terms vanish exactly

For an expectation of a maximum of smooth branches, the second derivative generally has coarea/switching contributions supported on tie surfaces. Here they vanish because the first `theta` derivatives of the tied active branches agree pointwise on every active tie surface.

At `theta=0`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{d\theta}|q_1|\Big|_0
&=-Y^2\,\operatorname{sgn}(XZ),\\
\frac d{d\theta}|q_2|\Big|_0
&=-XY\,\operatorname{sgn}(YZ),\\
\frac d{d\theta}|q_3|\Big|_0
&=-YZ\,\operatorname{sgn}(YX).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.9}
\]

There are three generic active tie surfaces.

1. `|q_1|=|q_2| >= |q_3|` means `|X|=|Y|` and `|Z|>=|X|`. Writing `Y=epsilon X`, `epsilon in {+1,-1}`, the first two expressions in (5.9) are identical.
2. `|q_1|=|q_3| >= |q_2|` means `|Z|=|Y|` and `|X|>=|Y|`. Writing `Z=epsilon Y`, the first and third expressions in (5.9) are identical.
3. `|q_2|=|q_3| >= |q_1|` means `|Z|=|X|` and `|Y|>=|X|`. Writing `Z=epsilon X`, the second and third expressions in (5.9) are identical.

Triple ties have Gaussian codimension at least two and do not contribute a surface term. The zero sets of the active products have measure zero.

Equivalently, in the standard second-variation formula for an integral of `max_k g_k(theta,z)`, every switching surface carries a nonnegative term proportional to the square of the jump of the first `theta` derivative. Every such jump is exactly zero here. Therefore the expectation's second derivative is just the expectation of the interior branch second derivatives in (5.8).

This can also be justified by partitioning Gaussian space away from an `eta`-neighborhood of the tie surfaces, differentiating there, applying the coarea estimate on the `eta`-neighborhood, and sending `eta -> 0`; (5.9) removes the possible first-order switching singularity.

### 5.3 Exact negative curvature

By exchangeability, each of `|X|,|Y|,|Z|` is the unique smallest with probability `1/3`, and on each such event the active product equals `M`. Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}
\mathcal F_0(u_\theta)\Big|_{\theta=0}
&=
-\mathbb E\big[|YZ|\,\mathbf 1_{\{|X|\text{ smallest}\}}\big]
-\mathbb E\big[|YX|\,\mathbf 1_{\{|Z|\text{ smallest}\}}\big]\\
&=
\boxed{-\frac23\,\mathbb E M}<0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.10}
\]

This is an exact analytic curvature formula, not a numerical observation.

## 6. From one direction to a strict local maximum

The stabilizer of `u_*` under coordinate permutations is the full symmetric group `S_4`. It acts on the tangent space

\[
T_{u_*}S^3
=\left\{v\in\mathbb R^4:\sum_i v_i=0\right\}
\tag{6.1}
\]

by its three-dimensional standard irreducible representation.

Because `\mathcal F_0` is permutation invariant and `u_*` is fixed by every permutation, its Hessian at `u_*` is an `S_4`-invariant symmetric bilinear form on this irreducible tangent representation. Therefore it is a scalar multiple of the Euclidean metric.

Equation (5.10) computes that scalar and shows it is strictly negative. Consequently

\[
\boxed{
u_*\text{ is a nondegenerate strict local maximum of }\mathcal F_0.}
\tag{6.2}
\]

This is already an important landscape fact at the singular endpoint: the completely balanced one-small-eigenvector orientation is not a saddle there; it is a genuine local maximum, even though it is not KL.

## 7. Uniform positive-definite perturbation

The singular local maximum forces positive-definite non-KL local maxima nearby without requiring any unproved Hessian-continuity assertion.

Couple the entire orbit with a single `Z~N(0,I_4)` using the exact square root

\[
C_{\lambda,u}^{1/2}
=P_u+\sqrt\lambda\,uu^T,
\qquad
P_u=I-uu^T.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Set

\[
X_{\lambda,u}
=(P_u+\sqrt\lambda\,uu^T)Z,
\qquad
X_{0,u}=P_uZ.
\]

Then uniformly over all unit `u`,

\[
\|X_{\lambda,u}-X_{0,u}\|
=\sqrt\lambda\,|u^TZ|
\le\sqrt\lambda\,\|Z\|,
\tag{7.2}
\]

and both `\|X_{\lambda,u}\|` and `\|X_{0,u}\|` are at most `\|Z\|`.

For

\[
f_2(x)=\max_{|S|=2}\|P_Sx\|^2,
\]

the elementary max-of-quadratics bound gives

\[
|f_2(x)-f_2(y)|
\le(\|x\|+\|y\|)\|x-y\|.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\sup_{u\in S^3}
|\mathcal F_\lambda(u)-\mathcal F_0(u)|
\le
2\sqrt\lambda\,\mathbb E\|Z\|^2
=8\sqrt\lambda.
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Thus `\mathcal F_\lambda -> \mathcal F_0` uniformly on the entire orbit as `lambda downarrow 0`.

Because `u_*` is a strict local maximum of `\mathcal F_0`, choose a sufficiently small closed geodesic ball `B` around `u_*`, containing no coordinate axis, such that

\[
\delta
:=\mathcal F_0(u_*)-\max_{u\in\partial B}\mathcal F_0(u)>0.
\tag{7.5}
\]

For all sufficiently small positive `lambda`, (7.4) gives

\[
\mathcal F_\lambda(u_*)
>
\max_{u\in\partial B}\mathcal F_\lambda(u).
\tag{7.6}
\]

Hence the continuous function `\mathcal F_\lambda` attains a maximum over `B` at an interior point `u_\lambda`. That point is a local maximum on the full rank-one orbit. Since `B` contains no coordinate axis, it is non-KL.

We have proved:

### Theorem 7.1 — positive-definite non-KL local maxima exist

There is `lambda_0>0` such that for every

\[
0<\lambda<\lambda_0,
\]

the spectrum

\[
(1,1,1,\lambda)
\]

with `p=4,d=2` has at least one **non-KL local maximum** of the exact nonlinear retained-energy objective.

No numerical optimization is used in this existence proof.

## 8. The local maxima are rigorously suboptimal for small lambda

The preceding theorem by itself does not say whether these local maxima could defeat KL globally. At the singular endpoint we can prove an explicit strict KL gap.

### 8.1 Balanced singular value

At `u_*`, `D=0` and `A,B,C` are iid standard normals. From (4.4),

\[
\mathcal F_0(u_*)
=\frac32+\mathbb E M,
\qquad
M=\max\{|AB|,|AC|,|BC|\}.
\tag{8.1}
\]

If `R_1<=R_2<=R_3` are the order statistics of three iid half-normal variables, then `M=R_2R_3`. With half-normal density

\[
f(r)=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}e^{-r^2/2},
\qquad
F(r)=\int_0^r f(s)\,ds,
\]

order-statistic integration gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E M
&=6\int_0^\infty r f(r)F(r)
\left(\int_r^\infty s f(s)\,ds\right)dr\\
&=6\int_0^\infty r f(r)^2F(r)\,dr\\
&=\boxed{\frac{2\sqrt3}{\pi}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.2}
\]

For the final equality, use `f(r)^2=(2/\pi)e^{-r^2}` and integrate by parts against `F(r)=erf(r/\sqrt2)`.

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal F_0(u_*)=\frac32+\frac{2\sqrt3}{\pi}.
}
\tag{8.3}
\]

### 8.2 Coordinate/KL singular value and a strict lower gap

At a coordinate spike, say `u=e_1`, the first coordinate vanishes and the remaining three are iid standard normals. Thus

\[
\mathcal F_0(e_1)
=3-\mathbb E R_1^2,
\tag{8.4}
\]

where `R_1` is the minimum of three iid half-normal variables.

A simple two-variable bound is enough. Since

\[
R_1^2\le\min\{G_1^2,G_2^2\}
\]

for iid standard normals `G_1,G_2`, and

\[
\mathbb E\min\{G_1^2,G_2^2\}
=1-\frac2\pi,
\tag{8.5}
\]

we obtain

\[
\mathcal F_0(e_1)
\ge2+\frac2\pi.
\tag{8.6}
\]

For completeness, (8.5) follows from

\[
\min(G_1^2,G_2^2)
=\frac{G_1^2+G_2^2-|G_1^2-G_2^2|}{2}
\]

and

\[
G_1^2-G_2^2=(G_1-G_2)(G_1+G_2),
\]

where `G_1-G_2` and `G_1+G_2` are independent `N(0,2)` variables, so

\[
\mathbb E|G_1^2-G_2^2|=\frac4\pi.
\]

Combining (8.3) and (8.6),

\[
\mathcal F_0(e_1)-\mathcal F_0(u_*)
\ge
\boxed{
\gamma_0:=\frac12-\frac{2(\sqrt3-1)}{\pi}>0.
}
\tag{8.7}
\]

The positivity is elementary: `sqrt(3)<7/4` and `pi>3` imply

\[
\frac{2(\sqrt3-1)}\pi<\frac12.
\]

Thus the balanced singular local maximum is rigorously below KL.

### 8.3 Suboptimality persists for the positive-definite local maxima

Shrink the ball `B` from Section 7 if necessary so that

\[
\sup_{u\in B}\mathcal F_0(u)
\le\mathcal F_0(u_*).
\tag{8.8}
\]

This is possible because `u_*` is a strict local maximum.

For the interior local maximizer `u_\lambda in B`, uniform convergence gives

\[
\mathcal F_\lambda(u_\lambda)
\le
\mathcal F_0(u_*)+8\sqrt\lambda,
\]

while at a KL coordinate point

\[
\mathcal F_\lambda(e_1)
\ge
\mathcal F_0(e_1)-8\sqrt\lambda.
\]

Therefore, whenever `lambda` is also small enough that

\[
16\sqrt\lambda<\gamma_0,
\tag{8.9}
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal F_\lambda(e_1)
>
\mathcal F_\lambda(u_\lambda).
}
\tag{8.10}
\]

So the positive-definite non-KL local maxima forced by Theorem 7.1 are **strictly suboptimal** for all sufficiently small `lambda>0`.

This is important: the construction is a landscape obstruction, not a counterexample to the Mallat–Zeitouni global conjecture.

## 9. Consequences for proof strategy

The preceding checkpoint had sharpened the gradient-flow program to the hoped-for statement

> every non-KL stationary point should have an ascent direction.

Theorem 7.1 shows that statement is false. In fact, the exact nonlinear objective can have non-KL **local maxima** for positive-definite Gaussian covariances.

Therefore none of the following can prove the full conjecture without an additional global invariant:

1. “all stationary points are KL” — already refuted by balanced-support stationary points;
2. “all non-KL stationary points are saddles/minima” — refuted in this run;
3. “the monotone double-bracket flow converges from every initialization to KL” — impossible, because non-KL local maxima are stable candidates for ascent dynamics;
4. pairwise Jacobi monotonicity — already refuted in the earlier `05-36-01Z` checkpoint.

The surviving target is genuinely global:

> **Global value separation problem.** Prove that every non-KL local maximum has objective value strictly below the KL value, or construct a non-KL local maximum whose value exceeds KL.

The family `(p,d)=(4,2)`, spectrum `(1,1,1,lambda)` with small `lambda`, is now a concrete laboratory in which suboptimal local maxima are known to exist analytically. Tracking these branches as `lambda` increases is a targeted route to either a certified counterexample or a global separation theorem.
