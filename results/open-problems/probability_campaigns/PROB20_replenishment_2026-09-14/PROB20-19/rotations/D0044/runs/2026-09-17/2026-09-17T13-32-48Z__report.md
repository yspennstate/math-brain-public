2. **Forrester, arXiv:0711.1914v1, Theorem 2.** With `k=r+1`, the circular identity includes
   \[
   D_k\!\left(CE^0_{2/k,kN}\right)=CE^0_{2k,N}.
   \]
   The paper defines `CE^b_{beta,N}` by ordered angles `0<theta_1<...<theta_N<2pi`; `b=0` is the ordinary circular beta ensemble. Thus the decimation is genuinely anchored at the deterministic cut `0`, not at a randomly selected eigenvalue.
3. **Valkó–Virág, Sine-beta operator / circular-beta operator limit.** The standard Sine operator is the bulk operator limit, and the finite circular operators admit a precise coupling to it.

## 3. The candidate operator transform being audited

Let

\[
T_-\sim \mathrm{Sine}_{2/k},\qquad k\ge2.
\]

Index its simple spectrum relative to the fixed origin by

\[
\cdots<\xi^-_0<0<\xi^-_1<\xi^-_2<\cdots,
\]

with the evident continuation to all integer indices. Let the normalized left spectral measure be

\[
\Phi(T_-)=\mu_-:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}W_n^-\,\delta_{\xi_n^-}.
\]

By Valkó–Virág Proposition 3 plus Corollary 40,

\[
(W_n^-)_{n\in\mathbb Z}\ \text{are iid }\Gamma(1/k,\text{ mean }2)
\]

and are independent of the entire Sine-`2/k` support.

Let `F_-` and `F_+` be the CDFs of `Gamma(1/k, mean 2)` and `Gamma(k, mean 2)`, respectively, and define the deterministic monotone quantile map

\[
Q_k(x)=F_+^{-1}(F_-(x)),\qquad x>0.
\]

Define the marked decimation map

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal T_k\!\left(\sum_n w_n\delta_{\xi_n}\right)
=
\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z}Q_k(w_{km})\,\delta_{\xi_{km}/k}.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

The candidate operator map is

\[
\boxed{
\mathfrak D_k^{\rm op}(T)
:=\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal T_k(\Phi(T)))
}
\tag{3.2}
\]

on the full-measure normalized Sine domain on which the spectral data are simple and the normalized inverse is defined.

The preceding run claimed

\[
T_-\sim\mathrm{Sine}_{2/k}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathfrak D_k^{\rm op}(T_-)\sim\mathrm{Sine}_{2k}
\tag{3.3}
\]

and pathwise

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{spec}(\mathfrak D_k^{\rm op}(T_-))
=
\{\xi^-_{km}/k:m\in\mathbb Z\}.
}
\tag{3.4}
\]

This run's job was to try hard to break (3.1)--(3.4).

## 4. B2 — attack the negation: exact anchor and `1/k` scaling audit

The most plausible first failure is an off-by-one/residue error when the finite circular decimation is lifted across the cut at zero. This can be eliminated exactly, before taking any limit.

Take a source sample from `CE^0_{2/k,kN}` with ordered angles

\[
0<\theta_1<\cdots<\theta_{kN}<2\pi.
\]

Lift the circular configuration periodically and put it in the **source bulk scale** by defining, for `q in Z` and `1<=j<=kN`,

\[
X_{qkN+j}^{-,N}=kN(\theta_j+2\pi q).
\tag{4.1}
\]

Forrester's decimation keeps exactly `j=km`, `m=1,...,N`. The retained angles have the law of `CE^0_{2k,N}`. Lift the retained configuration in the **target bulk scale**:

\[
X_{qN+m}^{+,N}=N(\theta_{km}+2\pi q).
\tag{4.2}
\]

Now there is an exact pathwise identity, for every integer `q` and `m=1,...,N`,

\[
\boxed{
X_{qN+m}^{+,N}
=
\frac1k X_{k(qN+m)}^{-,N}.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Indeed,

\[
\frac1k X_{k(qN+m)}^{-,N}
=\frac1k\,kN(\theta_{km}+2\pi q)
=N(\theta_{km}+2\pi q).
\]

This formula handles positive and negative lifts simultaneously. In particular there is no free random residue at the origin: the deterministic finite cut in Forrester's definition selects the residue, and the two-sided lift preserves it.

Under the Valkó–Virág circular-to-Sine coupling, fixed ordered lifted coordinates converge locally almost surely; the spectrum is simple and almost surely has no point at the deterministic origin. Therefore (4.3) passes coordinatewise to the bulk limit and gives

\[
\boxed{
(\xi^+_m)_{m\in\mathbb Z}
=\left(\frac{\xi^-_{km}}{k}\right)_{m\in\mathbb Z}
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

under the induced coupling, while the target marginal is Sine-`2k` by Forrester's exact finite identity plus the ordinary circular bulk limit.

The factor `1/k` is also forced by intensity: every-`k`th thinning changes Sine intensity by `1/k`; compressing space by `1/k` restores the beta-independent Sine intensity.

**B2 outcome:** the strongest indexing/scaling counterattack fails. Equation (4.3) gives the exact finite origin-anchored identity from which (4.4) follows; there is no hidden residue or off-by-one defect.

### k=2 sporadic check

The potentially dangerous classical case is `k=2`, finite `beta=1 -> beta=4`. Equations (4.1)--(4.3) specialize to

\[
D_2(CE^0_{1,2N})=CE^0_{4,N},\qquad
X_{qN+m}^{+,N}=\frac12X_{2(qN+m)}^{-,N}.
\]

The last negative retained point and first positive retained point obey the same rule, so the classical case does not exhibit a special anchor exception.

## 5. B26 — hunt a sporadic mark-law failure

The second plausible failure is that retaining every `k`th eigenvalue could introduce dependence into the retained source norming constants, or that the Gamma-shape conversion could have the wrong parameters.

For standard `Sine_beta`, the relevant left spectral measure has, in distribution,

\[
W_n\stackrel{\rm iid}{\sim}\Gamma(\beta/2,\text{ mean }2),
\]

independently of the full support. Thus for `beta_-=2/k`,

\[
W_n^-\sim\Gamma(1/k,\text{ mean }2),
\]

and for `beta_+=2k`,

\[
W_m^+\sim\Gamma(k,\text{ mean }2).
\]

Ordering the support relative to zero is a measurable operation on the support alone. Since the **whole** source weight sequence is independent of the **whole** support, taking the deterministic subsequence `(W^-_{km})` preserves iid Gamma law and independence from the decimated support.

Because the two Gamma CDFs are continuous and strictly increasing on `(0,infinity)`, the probability-integral transform gives exactly

\[
Q_k(W^-_{km})\stackrel{\rm iid}{\sim}\Gamma(k,\text{ mean }2),
\]

still independently of `(\xi^-_{km}/k)_m`.

Together with (4.4), this proves the marked-measure identity in law

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal T_k(\Phi(T_-))
\ \stackrel d=\
\Phi(T_+),\qquad T_+\sim\mathrm{Sine}_{2k}.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

No independence-replacement inequality is used: this is the actual spectral-mark independence theorem of Valkó–Virág.

**B26 outcome:** no sporadic `k=2` or general-`k` mark obstruction appears. The exact shapes are `1/k -> k`, and the coordinatewise quantile transform gives the target marked law without fresh randomness.

## 6. B23 — assume the opposite: can the spectral measure fail to determine the normalized operator?

The third and most serious attack is against the inverse step. A bare spectrum certainly does **not** determine the Sine operator. Even a scalar spectral measure determines a general weighted Dirac system only up to the gauge/reparameterization freedom of inverse spectral theory. So the prior construction is valid only if the **standard Sine normalization kills all of that freedom**.

### 6.1 First bridge the two spectral-measure conventions

Valkó–Virág define the left spectral measure of a Dirac operator by

\[
\mu_{\rm left}=\sum_{\lambda}|\varphi_\lambda(0)|^2\,\delta_\lambda,
\tag{6.1}
\]

where `varphi_lambda` is the `L^2_R`-normalized eigenfunction.

At the regular left endpoint of standard Sine, fix the entire solution `Phi(z,t)` by

\[
\Phi(z,0)=e_1.
\]

At an eigenvalue `lambda`, the normalized eigenfunction is

\[
\varphi_\lambda(t)=\frac{\Phi(\lambda,t)}{\|\Phi(\lambda,\cdot)\|_{L^2_R}},
\]

so, since `|e_1|=1`,

\[
|\varphi_\lambda(0)|^2
=\frac1{\|\Phi(\lambda,\cdot)\|_{L^2_R}^2}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

But (6.2) is exactly the atom of the scalar spectral measure associated with the normalized entire solution in singular Weyl/de Branges theory. Therefore the marked measure in (5.1) is the scalar measure to which the Eckhardt–Kostenko–Teschl uniqueness theorem applies; there is no hidden convention mismatch.

### 6.2 Gauge/reparameterization elimination

Consider two standard-normalized zero-potential Sine-type Dirac systems on `[0,1)` with the same left spectral measure. Their Hamiltonians satisfy

\[
\det R(t)=\det\widetilde R(t)=\frac14,
\qquad
R(0)=\widetilde R(0)=\frac12I,
\tag{6.3}
\]

and both use the fixed left boundary line `span(e_1)`.

Eckhardt–Kostenko–Teschl Corollary 4.6 gives a locally absolutely continuous bijection `eta` and a **constant** matrix `Gamma_0 in SL(2,R)` such that

\[
\eta'(t)\widetilde R(\eta(t))
=\Gamma_0^T R(t)\Gamma_0
\quad\text{a.e.}
\tag{6.4}
\]

The bounded-type hypothesis is not being silently assumed: the left endpoint here is regular (hence limit-circle), and EKT explicitly records that the required Cartwright/bounded-type normalization is available in the limit-circle case.

Taking determinants in (6.4) and using (6.3) gives

\[
\eta'(t)^2\frac14=\frac14.
\]

Since `eta` is increasing,

\[
\eta'(t)=1\quad\text{a.e.}
\]

and because it maps the same interval `(0,1)` onto itself,

\[
\eta(t)=t.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Letting `t downarrow 0` in (6.4), using continuity of the Sine path at its regular endpoint and (6.3), yields

\[
\Gamma_0^T\Gamma_0=I.
\]

Together with `det Gamma_0=1`, this gives `Gamma_0 in SO(2)`. Intertwining the two self-adjoint operators also maps the fixed left boundary line to itself, so

\[
\Gamma_0e_1\parallel e_1.
\]

The only rotations with this property are

\[
\Gamma_0=\pm I.
\tag{6.6}
\]

The sign acts trivially on the operator/boundary lines. Hence the two normalized operators coincide.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{within the standard Sine normalization, the left spectral measure determines the operator uniquely.}}
\tag{6.7}
\]

### 6.3 Measurable inverse, not merely set-theoretic uniqueness

For a coupling one needs an operator-valued random variable, not only uniqueness.

Use the coefficient space consisting of locally integrable positive Hamiltonians with determinant `1/4`, the fixed regular normalization at `0`, and the right boundary parameter; with the usual local-`L^1` / boundary-angle coordinates this is a standard Borel space. The normalized spectral map `Phi` is Borel: on compact subintervals fundamental solutions depend measurably (indeed continuously in the usual ODE topology) on the coefficients, the singular Weyl function is obtained as the limit of truncated Weyl functions, and the scalar spectral measure is recovered from the Herglotz function by Stieltjes inversion. Equation (6.7) says `Phi` is injective on the normalized class. Lusin–Souslin therefore gives a Borel inverse on `Phi`'s image.

Since the random measure in (5.1) has exactly the law of a target Sine spectral measure, it belongs to that image almost surely, and (3.2) defines a genuine measurable deterministic coupling.

**B23 outcome:** the strongest inverse-spectral negation also fails. The residual EKT gauge and time change are killed by determinant normalization, the regular endpoint value, and the fixed left boundary line; the inverse can be chosen measurably on the full-measure target spectral-measure image.

## 7. What “explicit” means — the one remaining scope risk, not a mathematical gap

There is one semantic/source-scope issue that an outside audit must adjudicate carefully.

The construction is explicit at the operator/spectral-transform level:

\[
\boxed{
\mathfrak D_k^{\rm op}
=\Phi^{-1}\circ
\left[
\sum_n w_n\delta_{\xi_n}
\mapsto
\sum_m F_{\Gamma(k,\,\mathrm{mean}\,2)}^{-1}
\!\left(F_{\Gamma(1/k,\,\mathrm{mean}\,2)}(w_{km})\right)
\delta_{\xi_{km}/k}
\right]
\circ\Phi.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

It is deterministic from the source operator, has the correct target operator law, and realizes the requested spectral decimation pathwise. It does **not** give a local closed-form transformation of the underlying hyperbolic Brownian path/coefficient field. The old `D0044-A001` attempt shows why the most naive such local phase/time-change formula fails.

Virág's printed Question 6 asks whether the limiting **operators** can be coupled explicitly “in this way”; it does not formally require a local coefficientwise Brownian map. Under the natural operator-theoretic reading, (7.1) is a source-valid explicit coupling. Under a stronger unstated reading “write the target coefficient path directly as a local formula of the source Brownian path,” the current construction would be PARTIAL.
