## 2. Pinned theorem under attack, not re-proved

Let `D_v` be the degree of vertex `v` in `LK(n,k(n))`, and let

\[
\mu_n=\mathbb E[D_v].
\]

The existing owner-track theorem states that if

\[
1\le k(n)\le n-1,
\qquad
\frac{k(n)}{\log n}\to\infty,
\]

then

\[
\frac{\max_{1\le v\le n}|D_v-\mu_n|}{\mu_n}
\xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0.
\]

The historical threshold argument sets

\[
\delta_n=\sqrt{6k\log n},
\qquad
t_n=\frac{k-\delta_n}{n-1},
\]

and lets `B_v` be the degree in the threshold graph of edges with weight at most `t_n`. For sufficiently large `n`, `k>2\delta_n`, and with probability at least `1-2/n^2`, simultaneously

\[
k-2\delta_n\le B_v\le k
\quad\text{for all }v.
\]

On that event every threshold edge is among the `k` cheapest edges at both endpoints, hence the threshold graph is a subgraph of `LK(n,k)`, giving

\[
k-2\delta_n\le D_v\le k
\quad\text{for all }v.
\]

The later P0459 bridge uses only `D_v\ge0` and `D_v\le k` to obtain

\[
\mu_n\ge(k-2\delta_n)(1-2/n^2).
\]

This run tried to falsify these interfaces rather than polishing them.

## 3. B15 — BREAK THE SYMMETRY ON PURPOSE

### Attack

Try to condition on extreme, highly asymmetric local neighborhoods or threshold-degree configurations and break either:

1. the implication “threshold edge => bilateral `k`-preference edge”; or
2. the common mean/mean-centering bridge.

The dependence in `LK(n,k)` is strongest precisely in atypical neighborhoods, so a hidden symmetry assumption here would be fatal.

### Deterministic stress test of the subgraph implication

Fix an arbitrary realization of all edge weights and suppose the global threshold event has `B_v\le k` for every vertex. If edge `{u,v}` has weight at most `t_n`, then it is counted among the `B_u` threshold edges incident to `u` and among the `B_v` threshold edges incident to `v`. Because all weights are continuous, ties occur with probability zero; even allowing a deterministic tie-breaking rule would not hurt the rank inequality. Since `B_u,B_v\le k`, the rank of `{u,v}` at each endpoint is at most `k`. Therefore the edge belongs to `LK(n,k)`.

This implication is pointwise and survives arbitrary asymmetry, clustering, or dependence elsewhere in the graph. No independence of vertex degrees is used.

### Stress test of the mean bridge

Let `A_n` denote the global threshold event and `p_n=P(A_n^c)\le2/n^2`. Exchangeability gives a common deterministic vertex mean `\mu_n`, but the lower bound does not even need independence between `D_v` and `A_n`:

\[
\mu_n
=\mathbb E[D_v1_{A_n}]+\mathbb E[D_v1_{A_n^c}]
\ge(k-2\delta_n)P(A_n).
\]

On the bad event, the contribution to the mean can be adversarially arranged, but the deterministic ceiling `D_v\le k` caps its effect by at most `kp_n`. Thus conditioning on an atypical vertex or neighborhood cannot create a mean shift large enough to defeat the stated bound.

### B15 outcome

**REFUTATION FAILED.** The two key implications are deterministic after conditioning on the global threshold event; breaking exchangeability locally does not expose a defect.

## 4. B24 — FIND THE EXTREMAL OBJECT

### Attack

Within the admissible class `k/log n -> infinity`, identify the growth sequence making the explicit normalized error bound as large as possible, and see whether a slow admissible sequence prevents convergence.

Let

\[
x_n:=\frac{2\delta_n}{k}=2\sqrt{\frac{6\log n}{k}},
\qquad
p_n:=\frac{2}{n^2}.
\]

From the existing proof, on `A_n`,

\[
\max_v|D_v-\mu_n|
\le 2\delta_n+kp_n,
\]

and

\[
\mu_n\ge(k-2\delta_n)(1-p_n).
\]

Hence the explicit relative bound is

\[
R_n(k)
:=
\frac{x_n+p_n}{(1-x_n)(1-p_n)}.
\]

For fixed `n`, `x_n` is strictly decreasing in `k`, and `R_n` is strictly increasing in `x_n` on `0\le x_n<1`; therefore the most adverse `k` is the smallest admissible one. But admissibility itself says

\[
\frac{k}{\log n}\to\infty,
\]

which is exactly equivalent to `x_n->0`. Also `p_n->0`. Therefore every admissible sequence, including arbitrarily slowly diverging examples such as

\[
k(n)=\left\lceil \log n\,\log\log\log n\right\rceil
\]

(where defined and then patched on finitely many small `n`), still satisfies `R_n(k)->0`.

There is no hidden uniformity demand over *all* admissible sequences at a fixed `n`; the theorem is a statement for each asymptotic sequence satisfying the displayed hypothesis. A diagonal sequence with infinitely many returns to `k\asymp C\log n` would fail the hypothesis itself.

### B24 outcome

**REFUTATION FAILED.** The explicit error ratio exposes the extremal direction and still tends to zero for every sequence in the theorem's domain.

## 5. B26 — HUNT THE SPORADIC CASE

This attack inspected integer rounding, the threshold condition, near-complete graphs, and asymptotically sparse-but-valid sequences.

### 5.1 The `k>24 log n` threshold

Since

\[
\delta_n^2=6k\log n,
\]

`k>2\delta_n` is equivalent to `k>24\log n`. The theorem's hypothesis `k/\log n->\infty` guarantees this eventually. Finitely many failures do not affect convergence in probability.

An attempted sporadic counterexample with infinitely many `n` satisfying only `k\approx25\log n` cannot coexist with `k/\log n->\infty`, because that subsequence would keep the ratio bounded.

### 5.2 Integer rounding in Chernoff events

`B_v` is integer-valued while `k-2\delta_n` need not be. The lower-tail event `B_v<k-2\delta_n` is contained in the usual real-threshold lower-tail event at distance `\delta_n` below the mean `k-\delta_n`; using a noninteger threshold does not reverse the Chernoff inequality. Likewise `B_v>k` is a subset of the standard upper-tail event `B_v\ge k=\mathbb E B_v+\delta_n`. Thus integer rounding can only make the quoted estimates safer, not invalidate them.

### 5.3 Positivity and range of the threshold probability

For all sufficiently large `n`, `k>2\delta_n`, hence `k-\delta_n>0` and

\[
0<t_n=\frac{k-\delta_n}{n-1}<1
\]

whenever `k\le n-1`. So the threshold graph is well-defined throughout the asymptotic regime where the proof is invoked.

### 5.4 Near-complete regime `k\approx n-1`

If `k=n-1`, `LK(n,k)=K_n` deterministically, so `D_v=\mu_n=n-1` and the theorem is trivial. For `k` close to `n-1`, `t_n<1` remains valid and the same binomial lower-tail comparison works. The upper-tail event `B_v>k` may become impossible, which only improves the global event.

### 5.5 Bad-event contribution

A rare global failure event could in principle contain pathological configurations. But `0\le D_v\le k` deterministically, so its total possible effect on `\mu_n` is at most `kp_n=2k/n^2`, already included in the numerator. No sporadic high-degree outlier is possible because `k` is a hard ceiling.

### B26 outcome

**REFUTATION FAILED.** No rounding, endpoint, finite-prefix, or near-complete exceptional family violates the theorem.
