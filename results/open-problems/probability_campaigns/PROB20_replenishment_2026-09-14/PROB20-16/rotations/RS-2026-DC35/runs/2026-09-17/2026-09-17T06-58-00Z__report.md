## 2. Recovered starting reductions

The previous RS-2026-DC35 report proved two reductions that remain in force.

First, with `V=span(D)`, the hull decomposes by affine `V`-cosets, so Problem 35 can be reduced to spanning cones.

Second, for a spanning cone the real-valued `D`-convex functions depend only on the closed projective direction set

`Omega_D = closure{[v]:v in D\{0}}`.

Thus the classification is naturally projective, not a classification of raw presentations of the cone. In particular, a dense nonsemialgebraic raw cone can still induce ordinary convexity and ordinary polytopal hulls.

The previous report also introduced the finite Jensen-weight set. For `P={p_1,...,p_N}` and a point `x`, define

\[
\Lambda_D(P,x)=\left\{\lambda\in\Delta_{N-1}:
 f(x)\le\sum_{i=1}^N\lambda_i f(p_i)
 \text{ for every real-valued }D\text{-convex }f\right\}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

By finite-dimensional separation of the cone of evaluation differences from the open negative orthant,

\[
 x\in\operatorname{conv}_D(P)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \Lambda_D(P,x)\ne\varnothing.
\tag{2.2}
\]

This run pushes (2.1) much further.

## 3. B11 — DUALIZE: exact Jensen lifting through a full linear factor

The source's most prominent suspected wild cone — finitely many planes in `R^3` sharing one line — has a common full linear factor. The following theorem removes that factor exactly.

### Theorem 3.1 (cylindrical Jensen lifting)

Let `V` and `W` be finite-dimensional real vector spaces. Let `D_0 subset V` be a spanning cone and set

\[
D=D_0\times W\subset V\times W.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Let

\[
\widetilde P=\{(p_i,q_i):1\le i\le N\}\subset V\times W,
\]

and let `(x,q) in V x W`. Then for every `lambda in Delta_{N-1}`,

\[
\boxed{
\lambda\in\Lambda_D(\widetilde P,(x,q))
\iff
\left[
\lambda\in\Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)
\ \text{and}\
q=\sum_i\lambda_i q_i
\right].}
\tag{3.2}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{conv}_{D_0\times W}(\widetilde P)
=
\left\{\left(x,\sum_i\lambda_iq_i\right):
\lambda\in\Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)\right\}.}
\tag{3.3}
\]

This is an equality for the **functional** hull, not merely the lamination hull.

### Proof: forward implication

Assume `lambda` is a `D`-Jensen weight at `(x,q)`.

For every real-valued `D_0`-convex `f:V->R`, the function

\[
F(v,w)=f(v)
\]

is `D`-convex. Hence

\[
f(x)\le\sum_i\lambda_i f(p_i),
\]

so `lambda in Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)`.

For every linear functional `ell in W^*`, both

\[
F_+(v,w)=\ell(w),\qquad F_-(v,w)=-\ell(w)
\]

are `D`-convex. The two Jensen inequalities force

\[
\ell(q)=\sum_i\lambda_i\ell(q_i)
\]

for every `ell`, hence

\[
q=\sum_i\lambda_iq_i.
\tag{3.4}
\]

### Proof: reverse implication

Assume now that `lambda in Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)` and (3.4) holds. Let

\[
F:V\times W\to\mathbb R
\]

be an arbitrary real-valued `D`-convex function. Because vertical directions belong to `D`, the section `w -> F(x,w)` is an ordinary finite convex function on `W`. Choose

\[
\alpha\in\partial_wF(x,q)\subset W^*.
\tag{3.5}
\]

For `epsilon>0`, fix any Euclidean norm on `W` and define the regularized partial infimum

\[
g_{\alpha,\epsilon}(v)
=
\inf_{w\in W}
\left[F(v,w)-\langle\alpha,w\rangle
      +\epsilon\|w\|^2\right].
\tag{3.6}
\]

#### Claim 3.2
`g_{alpha,epsilon}` is a finite real-valued `D_0`-convex function.

Fix `v_0 in V` and `y in D_0`. On the affine subspace

\[
\{(v_0+s y,w):s\in\mathbb R,\ w\in W\},
\]

all line directions have the form `(t y,w')` up to sign, hence belong to the projective directions of `D=D_0 x W`. Therefore the finite restriction

\[
(s,w)\mapsto F(v_0+s y,w)
\]

is an ordinary jointly convex function of `(s,w)`. Subtracting the linear form `<alpha,w>` and adding `epsilon||w||^2` preserves joint convexity. Partial minimization over `w` therefore gives a convex function of `s`.

It is finite: the upper bound follows by evaluating at `w=0`; for each fixed `v`, the finite convex section `w -> F(v,w)-<alpha,w>` admits an affine lower support, while `epsilon||w||^2` is coercive. Thus the infimum is a finite real number. This proves the claim.

Since `lambda` is a base Jensen weight,

\[
g_{\alpha,\epsilon}(x)
\le
\sum_i\lambda_i g_{\alpha,\epsilon}(p_i).
\tag{3.7}
\]

At each support point, simply evaluate the infimum at `q_i`:

\[
g_{\alpha,\epsilon}(p_i)
\le
F(p_i,q_i)-\langle\alpha,q_i\rangle
+\epsilon\|q_i\|^2.
\tag{3.8}
\]

At `(x,q)`, (3.5) says that `q` minimizes

\[
w\mapsto F(x,w)-\langle\alpha,w\rangle.
\]

Hence, as `epsilon downarrow 0`,

\[
g_{\alpha,\epsilon}(x)
\longrightarrow
F(x,q)-\langle\alpha,q\rangle.
\tag{3.9}
\]

Indeed the unregularized minimum gives a lower bound, while evaluation at `w=q` gives the matching upper bound up to `epsilon||q||^2`.

Combining (3.7)–(3.9), letting `epsilon downarrow 0`, and using (3.4), gives

\[
F(x,q)-\langle\alpha,q\rangle
\le
\sum_i\lambda_i
\left(F(p_i,q_i)-\langle\alpha,q_i\rangle\right),
\]

so the linear terms cancel and

\[
F(x,q)\le\sum_i\lambda_iF(p_i,q_i).
\tag{3.10}
\]

This holds for every real-valued `D`-convex `F`, proving the reverse implication in (3.2). Equation (3.3) follows from the Jensen-weight characterization (2.2). QED.

### Corollary 3.3 (semialgebraic positive-transfer criterion)

For finite base data `P`, let

\[
\mathcal J_{D_0}(P)
=
\{(x,\lambda):\lambda\in\Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)\}.
\tag{3.11}
\]

If `mathcal J_{D_0}(P)` is semialgebraic, then for **every** choice of fiber labels `q_i in W`, the cylindrical functional hull of the lifted points is semialgebraic, because (3.3) is the projection of the semialgebraic set

\[
\{(x,\lambda,q):
(x,\lambda)\in\mathcal J_{D_0}(P),
q=\sum_i\lambda_iq_i\}
\]

under a polynomial/linear map. Thus a cone class for which every finite base Jensen relation is semialgebraic remains positive after adjoining a common full linear factor.

This is only a **sufficient transfer principle** for Problem 35. I am not claiming here that Franěk–Matoušek's planar membership algorithm automatically proves semialgebraicity of the full Jensen relation (3.11); that stronger parametric statement is exactly one of the remaining points that needs proof rather than inference.

## 4. Consequence for the source's planes-through-a-line geometry

Let

\[
D=P_1\cup\cdots\cup P_k\subset\mathbb R^3
\]

where the distinct planes `P_j` share a common line `L`. Passing to the quotient

\[
V=\mathbb R^3/L\cong\mathbb R^2,
\]

each plane becomes a projective base direction `ell_j subset V`. After choosing a linear splitting of the quotient,

\[
D\cong D_0\times L,
\qquad
D_0=\ell_1\cup\cdots\cup\ell_k\subset V.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Therefore Theorem 3.1 applies **exactly** to the source-suspected cone family. If

\[
K=\{(p_i,h_i)\}\subset V\times L,
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{conv}_{D}(K)
=
\left\{\left(x,\sum_i\lambda_i h_i\right):
\lambda\in\Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)\right\}.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

The three-dimensional difficulty is therefore not an independent vertical compatible-grid phenomenon: it is the geometry, as `x` varies, of the **planar finite-direction Jensen-weight set** `Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)`. Meroni–Raiţă's infinite compatible grid is one way this planar Jensen geometry can become difficult, but (4.2) is an exact functional-hull reduction and does not require the grid to terminate.

This is the main mathematical advance of the run.

## 5. B5 — ADD A PARAMETER AND DIFFERENTIATE: plane-pencil moduli

The source specifically singles out three distinct planes sharing one line. Projective normalization shows that there is actually **no intrinsic continuous cone parameter** in that case.

### Proposition 5.1 (all three-plane pencils are linearly equivalent)

Let `P_1,P_2,P_3` and `P'_1,P'_2,P'_3` be ordered triples of distinct planes in `R^3`, each triple sharing one common line. Then there is an invertible linear map taking the first ordered pencil to the second.

### Proof

Quotient each ambient space by its common line. An ordered triple of planes becomes an ordered triple of distinct lines in a two-dimensional quotient, hence three distinct points of `RP^1`. The projective group `PGL_2(R)` is triply transitive on ordered distinct triples.

In an affine slope chart `r`, for three distinct slopes `r_1,r_2,r_3`, the Möbius map

\[
T(r)=
\frac{(r-r_1)(r_2-r_3)}{(r-r_3)(r_2-r_1)}
\tag{5.1}
\]

sends

\[
r_1\mapsto0,\qquad r_2\mapsto1,\qquad r_3\mapsto\infty.
\]

A representative `GL_2` map on the quotient lifts to `GL_3` after choosing the common line as the third coordinate. QED.

Thus the source phrase “any cone consisting of three distinct planes which all intersect in a line” describes one linear-equivalence class. Angles between the three planes cannot be the missing classification invariant.

### First genuine deformation: four planes

For four distinct quotient slopes `r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4`, after normalizing the first three, the fourth becomes the cross-ratio parameter

\[
\tau=T(r_4).
\tag{5.2}
\]

Differentiating gives

\[
\boxed{
\frac{d\tau}{dr_4}
=
\frac{(r_2-r_3)(r_1-r_3)}
{(r_2-r_1)(r_4-r_3)^2},}
\tag{5.3}
\]

which is nonzero away from collisions. Hence a pencil of four planes is the first member of this source geometry with a genuine one-dimensional local moduli parameter (up to the usual relabeling transformations of the cross-ratio). For `k>4`, one obtains the expected `k-3` projective moduli generically.

The exact algebra in (5.1)–(5.3) was self-checked symbolically; see Section 8.

**B5 outcome.** Deforming the three-plane cone itself cannot reveal a hidden threshold: every such deformation is projective gauge. The next cone-parameter experiment, if pursued, should start at four planes and vary the cross-ratio, while the fixed three-plane case must be attacked through the Jensen relation / point configuration instead.

## 6. B24 — FIND THE EXTREMAL OBJECT: where can the first genuinely new complexity live?

The cylindrical formula (4.2) gives a sharper way to count degrees of freedom.

Let `P={p_1,...,p_N}` affinely span `R^m`. Every Jensen weight satisfies the affine barycenter equations

\[
\sum_i\lambda_i=1,
\qquad
\sum_i\lambda_i p_i=x.
\tag{6.1}
\]

For fixed `x`, the ambient affine space of candidate weights therefore has dimension

\[
N-m-1.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The `D_0`-Jensen set is a compact convex subset of that affine space.

### Three base points in the planar quotient

For `m=2,N=3`, the barycentric weight is unique whenever it exists. Formula (4.2) then forces every lift to lie in one affine plane over the planar functional hull. This is exactly the mechanism behind Meroni–Raiţă Lemma 5.2 for a `T_3` configuration: there is no free Jensen-weight geometry to create a nonlinear vertical fiber.

### Four base points

For `m=2,N=4`, there is exactly one free barycentric coordinate. In the standard rectangle notation

\[
(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1),
\]

all candidate weights with barycenter `(s,t)` are

\[
\boxed{
(\lambda_{00},\lambda_{10},\lambda_{01},\lambda_{11})
=(1-s-t+u,\ s-u,\ t-u,\ u).}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Hence `Lambda_{D_0}(P,(s,t))` is a closed interval in the single parameter `u`.

This explains, without re-proving C29, why the vertical-height gauge in the four-point source candidate collapses so strongly. Modulo an affine function on the base, the four heights have only one nonaffine degree of freedom, and the one-dimensional fiber coordinate probes exactly the one-dimensional Jensen interval. C29's exact canonical hull can therefore be read as an exact computation of the two endpoint functions of this Jensen interval for that irrational-parallelogram configuration.

The symbolic artifact checks (6.3) exactly.

### Five base points are the first multi-parameter Jensen laboratory in the plane

For `m=2,N=5`, the affine candidate-weight space is two-dimensional. This is the first cardinality at which a planar point configuration can carry a genuinely two-parameter Jensen body `Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)` rather than a point or interval.

This is **not** a proof that four points are always semialgebraic, and it is **not** a proof that five points are wild. A different four-point projection could still have nonsemialgebraic endpoint functions. The precise conclusion is only the structural one: `N=5` is the first size at which the hidden Jensen geometry itself can be two-dimensional, so a one-dimensional vertical lift sees only a support direction of a higher-dimensional convex set rather than the whole set.

That is also why the C29 five-point experiments are the correct next laboratory rather than another perturbation of the already-solved four-corner vertical heights.

**B24 outcome.** For the source three-plane pencil, the extremal object to understand is not “the most irrational compatible grid” by itself. It is the smallest point configuration for which the planar Jensen-weight relation acquires genuinely multi-parameter geometry and its scalar support functions can fail finite semialgebraic closure.

## 7. A classification criterion exposed by the reduction

Theorem 3.1 suggests separating Problem 35 into two layers.

For a spanning cone `D_0 subset V`, define the **Jensen-semialgebraicity property**

\[
\mathrm{JSA}(D_0):
\quad
\mathcal J_{D_0}(P)
=\{(x,\lambda):\lambda\in\Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)\}
\text{ is semialgebraic for every finite }P.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\mathrm{JSA}(D_0)
\Longrightarrow
P(D_0\times W)
\text{ for every finite-dimensional }W,}
\tag{7.2}
\]

where `P(D)` denotes the source property “every finite functional `D`-convex hull is semialgebraic.”

This gives an exact permanence mechanism for one important family of cones and converts the three-plane source conjecture to a concrete planar question:

> Is the full Jensen relation `mathcal J_{D_0}(P)` semialgebraic for every finite planar `P` when `D_0` consists of three directions?

The existing Franěk–Matoušek theorem proves semialgebraicity/computability of **membership in the planar functional hull**, i.e. nonemptiness of `Lambda_{D_0}(P,x)`. It does not, from the material recovered in this run, automatically supply the stronger parametric statement (7.1). Treating those as equivalent would be an unjustified leap. This distinction is now the cleanest remaining bridge between known planar algorithms and the source's three-dimensional wildness question.
