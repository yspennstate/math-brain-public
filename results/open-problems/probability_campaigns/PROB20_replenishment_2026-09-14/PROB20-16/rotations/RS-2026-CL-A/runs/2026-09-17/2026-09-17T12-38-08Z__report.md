## 3. B11 — DUALIZE: replace the global planted entropy budget by a high-pass observation operator

### 3.1 Abstract setup

Let `(Omega,Q)` be a probability space. Let `V_D` be the closed `L^2(Q)` subspace generated by polynomials of degree at most `D`, with constants included, and let

\[
\Pi_D:L^2(Q)\to V_D
\]

be orthogonal projection.

Let `T` be a `Q`-stationary self-adjoint Markov operator. Think of `T` as the noise kernel: independent Boolean resampling, Gaussian Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise, or another reversible smoothing operator.

Let

\[
\Phi:\Omega\to\mathsf Y
\]

be a measurable statistic map and write

\[
Q_\Phi=\Phi_\#Q.
\]

Define the conditional-expectation operator

\[
\mathcal C_\Phi h(z)
=
\mathbb E_Q[h(X)\mid \Phi(X)=z].
\tag{3.1}
\]

It is a contraction from `L^2(Q)` to `L^2(Q_Phi)`.

Optionally, let a compact or finite group `G` act measurably on `Omega`, preserving `Q`, and let

\[
\mathcal R_G h(x)=\int_G h(gx)\,d\mu_G(g)
\tag{3.2}
\]

be the Reynolds projection. For a finite group this is the ordinary group average. If `P` is `G`-invariant and `P<<Q`, then its likelihood ratio `L=dP/dQ` satisfies

\[
\mathcal R_G L=L.
\tag{3.3}
\]

The group is optional; with no symmetry take `R_G=I`.

### 3.2 The high-pass smoothing coefficient

Define

\[
\boxed{
\Gamma_D(\Phi;Q,T,G)
:=
\left\|
\mathcal C_\Phi T(I-\Pi_D)\mathcal R_G
\right\|_{L^1(Q)\to L^2(Q_\Phi)}.
}
\tag{3.4}
\]

This coefficient depends on the **null law, noise, statistic class and symmetry**, but not on the planted law `P`. It asks how much an arbitrary unit-`L^1` input can survive the sequence

\[
\text{symmetrize}
\to
\text{remove all degree-}\le D\text{ information}
\to
\text{apply noise}
\to
\text{observe only }\Phi.
\]

This is the new route: rather than controlling the entire high-degree `L^2` mass of `L=dP/dQ`, control only the high-degree mass that can survive through the specified observation channel.

## 4. B4 — complete theorem: low-degree advantage plus high-pass smoothing blocks the whole statistic class

### Theorem 4.1 (dual high-pass smoothing criterion)

Assume the setup of Section 3. Let `P<<Q` be `G`-invariant, let `L=dP/dQ`, and let `TP` denote the law obtained by applying the Markov kernel `T` to a sample from `P`. Then

\[
\boxed{
\sqrt{
\chi^2\!\left(\Phi_\#(TP)\,\middle\|\,\Phi_\#Q\right)
}
\le
\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D}(P,Q)
+
\Gamma_D(\Phi;Q,T,G).
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Consequently, if along a sequence of problems

\[
\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D_n}(P_n,Q_n)\le A
\quad\text{and}\quad
\Gamma_{D_n}(\Phi_n;Q_n,T_n,G_n)\le B
\tag{4.2}
\]

with constants `A,B<infinity`, then no sequence of tests measurable with respect to `Phi_n` can strongly distinguish `T_nP_n` from `Q_n`.

More quantitatively,

\[
\boxed{
\left\|\Phi_{n\#}(T_nP_n)-\Phi_{n\#}Q_n\right\|_{TV}
\le
1-
\frac{1}{2+(A+B)^2}
<1.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

If both terms on the right side of `(4.1)` tend to zero, then

\[
\left\|\Phi_{n\#}(T_nP_n)-\Phi_{n\#}Q_n\right\|_{TV}\to0.
\tag{4.4}
\]

#### Proof

We prove every step because this is the theorem-sized output of the run.

**Step 1: density after reversible noise.**
For every bounded measurable `f`, stationarity and self-adjointness give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{TP}[f]
&=\mathbb E_P[Tf]
=\langle L,Tf\rangle_{L^2(Q)}\\
&=\langle TL,f\rangle_{L^2(Q)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.5}
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{d(TP)}{dQ}=TL.
\tag{4.6}
\]

**Step 2: density after observing `Phi`.**
For every bounded measurable `g` on `Y`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{\Phi_\#(TP)}[g]
&=\mathbb E_Q[g(\Phi(X))\,TL(X)]\\
&=\mathbb E_{Q_\Phi}
\left[
 g(Z)\,\mathbb E_Q[TL(X)\mid \Phi(X)=Z]
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.7}
\]

Thus

\[
\frac{d\Phi_\#(TP)}{dQ_\Phi}
=\mathcal C_\Phi TL.
\tag{4.8}
\]

Because `T1=1` and conditional expectation preserves constants,

\[
\frac{d\Phi_\#(TP)}{dQ_\Phi}-1
=\mathcal C_\Phi T(L-1).
\tag{4.9}
\]

**Step 3: low/high degree decomposition.**
Since constants lie in `V_D`,

\[
(I-\Pi_D)(L-1)=(I-\Pi_D)L.
\tag{4.10}
\]

Since `P` and `Q` are `G`-invariant, `(3.3)` gives `R_G L=L`. Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal C_\Phi T(L-1)
&=
\mathcal C_\Phi T\Pi_D(L-1)
+
\mathcal C_\Phi T(I-\Pi_D)\mathcal R_G L.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.11}
\]

Take `L^2(Q_Phi)` norms and use the triangle inequality.

**Step 4: low-degree term.**
Both `T` and `C_Phi` are `L^2` contractions, so

\[
\|\mathcal C_\Phi T\Pi_D(L-1)\|_2
\le
\|\Pi_D(L-1)\|_2.
\tag{4.12}
\]

The Riesz representation of the source advantage gives

\[
\|\Pi_D(L-1)\|_2
=
\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D}(P,Q).
\tag{4.13}
\]

Indeed, for every centered `f in V_D`,

\[
\mathbb E_P f-\mathbb E_Q f
=\langle L-1,f\rangle_Q
=\langle \Pi_D(L-1),f\rangle_Q,
\]

and the supremum after dividing by `||f||_2=sqrt(Var_Q f)` is precisely the norm of the representing vector.

**Step 5: high-degree term.**
By definition `(3.4)` and because `L` is a probability density,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathcal C_\Phi T(I-\Pi_D)\mathcal R_G L\|_2
&\le
\Gamma_D(\Phi;Q,T,G)\,\|L\|_{L^1(Q)}\\
&=
\Gamma_D(\Phi;Q,T,G).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.14}
\]

Combining `(4.9)`–`(4.14)` proves `(4.1)` because the `L^2(Q_Phi)` norm of the centered output density is exactly the square root of its chi-square divergence.

**Step 6: convert bounded chi-square to failure of strong testing.**
Let `R<<S` have density `r=dR/dS`. Since

\[
1-\|R-S\|_{TV}
=\mathbb E_S[\min(r,1)]
\ge
\mathbb E_S\left[\frac{r}{1+r}\right],
\tag{4.15}
\]

Cauchy–Schwarz applied to

\[
r=\sqrt{\frac{r}{1+r}}\,\sqrt{r(1+r)}
\]

gives

\[
1=(\mathbb E_Sr)^2
\le
\mathbb E_S\left[\frac{r}{1+r}\right]
\mathbb E_S[r(1+r)].
\tag{4.16}
\]

But

\[
\mathbb E_S[r(1+r)]
=1+\mathbb E_S[r^2]
=2+\chi^2(R\|S).
\tag{4.17}
\]

Therefore

\[
1-\|R-S\|_{TV}
\ge
\frac{1}{2+\chi^2(R\|S)}.
\tag{4.18}
\]

Apply `(4.18)` to the two pushforward laws and `(4.1)` to obtain `(4.3)`. If the right side of `(4.1)` tends to zero, the standard inequality `TV <= (1/2)sqrt(chi^2)` gives `(4.4)`. This completes the proof. `square`

## 5. Exact finite version: `Gamma_D` is a maximum column norm

The criterion is not merely an existential condition. On a finite state space it has an exact finite representation.

### Proposition 5.1

Assume `Omega` is finite and `Q(x)>0` for every `x`. Put

\[
A_D=\mathcal C_\Phi T(I-\Pi_D)\mathcal R_G.
\tag{5.1}
\]

For each `x in Omega`, define the point-mass density

\[
e_x(y)=\frac{\mathbf 1_{\{y=x\}}}{Q(x)}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\Gamma_D(\Phi;Q,T,G)
=
\max_{x\in\Omega}
\|A_De_x\|_{L^2(Q_\Phi)}.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

#### Proof

Every `h in L^1(Q)` has the exact decomposition

\[
h=\sum_{x\in\Omega} c_xe_x,
\qquad
c_x=Q(x)h(x),
\tag{5.4}
\]

with

\[
\|h\|_{L^1(Q)}=\sum_x|c_x|.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Therefore

\[
\|A_Dh\|_2
\le
\sum_x|c_x|\,\|A_De_x\|_2
\le
\left(\max_x\|A_De_x\|_2\right)\|h\|_1.
\tag{5.6}
\]

This proves the upper bound in `(5.3)`. The reverse inequality follows by taking `h=e_x` at a maximizing state; `||e_x||_1=1`. `square`

Thus `Gamma_D` is an exact maximum of finitely many high-pass smoothed observation kernels. It can in principle be attacked by symmetry/representation theory or exact computation without knowing `P`.

If, additionally, `T`, `Pi_D`, and `R_G` admit a common real orthonormal basis `psi_alpha` with

\[
T\psi_\alpha=\lambda_\alpha\psi_\alpha,
\qquad
R_G\psi_\alpha=r_\alpha\psi_\alpha,
\qquad r_\alpha\in\{0,1\},
\]

and `Pi_D` retains precisely `deg(alpha)<=D`, then

\[
A_De_x(z)
=
\sum_{\deg(\alpha)>D}
\lambda_\alpha r_\alpha\psi_\alpha(x)
\mathbb E_Q[\psi_\alpha(X)\mid \Phi(X)=z].
\tag{5.7}
\]

Equation `(5.7)` exposes the object that must be small: an orbit-averaged, noised, high-degree kernel after conditioning on the allowed statistic.

\tag{6.3}

## 7. B20 — find why the obvious generalization fails

### 7.1 The exact failure of taking `Phi = identity`

A tempting move would be to declare `(6.3)` for the identity observation and thereby obtain a universal noisy indistinguishability theorem. Mao shows that this cannot be a benign null-side condition.

Suppose in a Mao-type family the degree-`D_n` advantage is zero and

\[
\|T_nP_n-Q_n\|_{TV}=1-\eta_n,
\qquad
\eta_n\to0.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Apply Theorem 4.1 with `Phi=id`. Since the low-degree term is zero,

\[
\sqrt{\chi^2(T_nP_n\|Q_n)}
\le
\Gamma_{D_n}(\mathrm{id};Q_n,T_n,S_n).
\tag{7.2}
\]

On the other hand `(4.18)` and `(7.1)` force

\[
\eta_n
\ge
\frac1{2+\chi^2(T_nP_n\|Q_n)},
\]

hence

\[
\boxed{
\Gamma_{D_n}(\mathrm{id};Q_n,T_n,S_n)
\ge
\sqrt{\eta_n^{-1}-2}
\longrightarrow\infty.
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

So the full-observation high-pass operator is **provably large** in the counterexample regime. This is an exact obstruction, not an informal statement that “rank is global.”

### 7.2 Why Chen–Li succeeds where Mao escapes

Chen–Li’s positive theorem uses model-specific decomposition: a large Fourier support is broken into low-degree pieces meeting through a small interface, and fixed noise absorbs the reassembly cost. Their treewidth hypothesis controls that interface in the supercritical planted-subgraph model.

Mao’s family is not a deterministic sparse planted-subgraph family governed by such a treewidth parameter. Its distinguisher assembles many locally pseudorandom observations into a global low-rank certificate. The exact conclusion we can prove here is `(7.3)`: whatever decomposition one tries to turn into a universal identity-observation theorem, its corresponding high-pass smoothing norm must blow up on Mao. Thus a successful framework has to do one of two things:

1. restrict the **observation/statistic class** so that its high-pass kernel is small; or
2. impose a substantive **model decomposition condition** strong enough to bound the relevant high-pass operator for that model.

This cleanly separates Hsieh/Chen–Li style positive structure from Mao/Buhai style global algebraic reassembly.

### 7.3 Quantifier trap: unrestricted postprocessing of “a low-degree polynomial” is too broad

There is another exact obstruction that should be made explicit before formulating the next conjecture. On the Boolean cube `Omega={0,1}^N`, the degree-one polynomial

\[
p(x)=\sum_{j=1}^N 2^j x_j
\tag{7.4}
\]

is injective. Therefore **every** test on the full input can be written as a measurable function of the value of a single degree-one polynomial `p`.

This does not contradict the low-degree framework: the standard framework measures the polynomial itself as the test statistic, not arbitrary infinite-precision decoding of an injective polynomial value. But it shows that a theorem phrased as

> “no arbitrary statistic of an arbitrary degree-`k` polynomial can distinguish”

is false without further restrictions such as symmetry, a fixed generating family, coefficient/bit-complexity control, or a representation-theoretic notion of admissible statistic. Hsieh et al.’s **symmetric** polynomial classes avoid this encoding pathology.

This quantifier issue is important for Kireeva’s phrase “polynomials of a certain degree”: the polynomial class and the allowed postprocessing must both be frozen explicitly in any source-level theorem.
