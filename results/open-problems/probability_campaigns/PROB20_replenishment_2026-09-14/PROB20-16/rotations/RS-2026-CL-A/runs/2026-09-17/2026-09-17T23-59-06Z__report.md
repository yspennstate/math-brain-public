### Lemma 3.1 — source LDA is exactly the low-degree coefficient mass

\[
\boxed{

\sum_{j=1}^{D}b_j.

\tag{3.5}

#### Proof

The supremum over nonzero `g` divided by `||g||_2=sqrt(Var_Q f)` is exactly the Hilbert norm of the projection. Orthogonality gives its squared norm as `sum_{j<=D}b_j`. `□`

## 4. B27 — positive-factorization width gives a general source-style theorem

The previous positive-support criteria controlled individual cylinders. The present criterion depends only on the signed/cancellative pair `(P,Q)` through `F_{P,Q}`.

Normalize `F` at `1`:

\[
\widehat F(z):=\frac{F(z)}{F(1)}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

This is a probability polynomial in Klich’s sense. Positive factorization is unchanged, up to harmless positive normalizations, by passing between `F` and `\widehat F`.

### Definition 4.1 — positive-factorization width

For a nonconstant nonnegative-coefficient polynomial `F` with `F(0)=1`, define

\[
w_+(F)
:=
\min
\left\{
\max_{1\le\ell\le m}\deg G_\ell:
F(z)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}G_\ell(z),
\ G_\ell(0)=1,
\ G_\ell\text{ nonconstant with nonnegative coefficients}
\right\}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The trivial one-factor decomposition shows `w_+(F)<=deg F`. Equivalently one may normalize all factors at `z=1` and work in Klich’s positive-factorization poset. Because every factorization can be refined to a maximal positive atomization, `w_+` is equivalently the smallest possible largest atom degree among maximal positive atomizations.

I use the descriptive name `positive-factorization width`; a bounded search did not find this exact phrase, but **the underlying factorization poset and atom degrees are Klich/Briggs prior art**, so no naming/novelty claim is made.

### Theorem 4.2 — low positive-factorization width upgrades bounded LDA to full statistical non-separation

Let `(P,Q)` satisfy the graded setup above. Let `1<=R<=D`, and suppose

\[
\boxed{w_+(F_{P,Q})\le R.}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Then for every `q in [0,1]`,

\[
\boxed{
1+\chi^2(T_{\sqrt q}P\|Q)
=F_{P,Q}(q)
\le
\exp\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}q^r b_r\right)
\le
\exp\left(q\,\bigl(\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D}(P,Q)\bigr)^2\right).
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

In particular, at **no noise at all** (`q=1`),

\[
\boxed{
\chi^2(P\|Q)
\le
\exp\left(\bigl(\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D}(P,Q)\bigr)^2\right)-1.
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

#### Proof

Choose a witnessing factorization

\[
F(z)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}G_\ell(z),
\qquad
G_\ell(z)=1+\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{\ell,r}z^r,
\qquad c_{\ell,r}\ge0.
\tag{4.6}
\]

(If `deg G_l<R`, extend its coefficient list by zeros.) For `q>=0`,

\[
G_\ell(q)
=1+\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{\ell,r}q^r
\le
\exp\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{\ell,r}q^r\right).
\tag{4.7}
\]

Multiplying,

\[
F(q)
\le
\exp\left(
\sum_{r=1}^{R}q^r C_r
\right),
\qquad
C_r:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}c_{\ell,r}.
\tag{4.8}
\]

Now inspect the coefficient of `z^r` in the product (4.6). One contribution comes from selecting the `z^r` term from exactly one factor and constant terms from all others; its total is precisely `C_r`. Every other contribution is a product of nonnegative coefficients from two or more factors. Hence

\[
\boxed{C_r\le b_r.}
\tag{4.9}
\]

Therefore

\[
\log F(q)
\le
\sum_{r=1}^{R}q^r b_r
\le
q\sum_{r=1}^{R}b_r
\le
q\sum_{r=1}^{D}b_r,
\tag{4.10}
\]

where `q^r<=q` for `q in [0,1]` and `r>=1`. Lemma 3.1 and Lemma 3.2 finish the proof. `□`

\tag{4.11}

and

\[
\boxed{w_+(F_{P_n,Q_n})\le D_n\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n.}
\tag{4.12}

Then

\[
\sup_n\chi^2(P_n\|Q_n)\le e^{A^2}-1<\infty.
\tag{4.13}
\]

Thus no measurable tests — and therefore no polynomial tests of **any** degree — can strongly distinguish `P_n` from `Q_n`.

\boxed{

## 7. B29 — add the points at infinity: the compactified boundary is compound Poisson

The previous run’s finite correlation profile suggested a generating-function boundary. Klich’s positive-factorization language makes that boundary exact.

Consider a fixed factor-degree cutoff `R` and a sequence

\[
F_m(z)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{N_m}
G_{m,\ell}(z),
\qquad
G_{m,\ell}(z)
=1+\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{m,\ell,r}z^r,
\quad c_{m,\ell,r}\ge0.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Define factor activities and primitive masses

\[
a_{m,\ell}:=\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_{m,\ell,r},
\qquad
C_{m,r}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_m}c_{m,\ell,r}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

### Lemma 7.1 — infinitesimal positive factors converge to a compound-Poisson entire boundary

Assume

\[
\max_\ell a_{m,\ell}\to0,
\qquad
C_{m,r}\to c_r<\infty\quad(1\le r\le R).
\tag{7.3}
\]

Then locally uniformly on the complex plane,

\[
\boxed{
F_m(z)\longrightarrow
F_\infty(z):=\exp\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r z^r\right).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

#### Proof

Fix `K<infinity` and `|z|<=K`. Put

\[
u_{m,\ell}(z):=G_{m,\ell}(z)-1.
\]

With `K_R:=max_{1<=r<=R}K^r`,

\[
|u_{m,\ell}(z)|\le K_R a_{m,\ell}.
\tag{7.5}
\]

For large `m`, the right-hand side is uniformly below `1/2`. On `|u|<=1/2`,

\[
|\log(1+u)-u|\le 2|u|^2.
\tag{7.6}
\]

Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|
\log F_m(z)-\sum_\ell u_{m,\ell}(z)
\right|
&\le
2K_R^2\sum_\ell a_{m,\ell}^2\\
&\le
2K_R^2
\left(\max_\ell a_{m,\ell}\right)
\sum_\ell a_{m,\ell}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.7}
\]

The final sum equals `sum_r C_{m,r}`, which stays bounded by (7.3), so the right-hand side tends to zero. Meanwhile

\[
\sum_\ell u_{m,\ell}(z)
=
\sum_{r=1}^{R}C_{m,r}z^r
\to
\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_rz^r
\tag{7.8}
\]

uniformly on `|z|<=K`. Exponentiation proves (7.4). `□`

After normalization at `z=1`,

\[
\frac{F_\infty(z)}{F_\infty(1)}
=
\exp\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}c_r(z^r-1)\right),
\tag{7.9}
\]

which is exactly the probability-generating function of a **compound-Poisson** count with independent Poisson numbers of jumps of sizes `r` and intensities `c_r`.

This answers B29 literally: when finite factors become infinitesimal, the missing “points at infinity” of the finite positive-factorization space are exponential/compound-Poisson factors. This limiting mechanism is classical probability (law of rare events / compound Poisson), not a novelty claim; its role here is to identify the correct compactified boundary of the spectral-energy criterion.
