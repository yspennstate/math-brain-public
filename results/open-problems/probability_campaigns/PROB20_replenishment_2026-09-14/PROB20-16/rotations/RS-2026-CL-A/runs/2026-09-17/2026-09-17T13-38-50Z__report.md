## 4. B14 — FIND THE GROUP ACTING: invariant leverage-tail criterion

### 4.1 Abstract graded symmetric setup

Let `(Omega,Q)` be a finite probability space with full support. Let

\[
L^2(Q)=\mathcal H_0\oplus\mathcal H_1\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal H_M
\tag{4.1}
\]

be the orthogonal exact-degree decomposition, with `H_0=span{1}` and

\[
V_{\le D}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{D}\mathcal H_j
\]

the degree-at-most-`D` polynomial space.

Let a finite group `G` act on `Omega`, preserving `Q` and every degree space `H_j`. Let

\[
\mathcal H_j^G
=
\{f\in\mathcal H_j:f(gx)=f(x)\text{ for all }g\in G\}
\]

and choose an orthonormal basis

\[
\psi_{j,1},\ldots,\psi_{j,d_j}
\]

of `H_j^G`, where `d_j=dim(H_j^G)`.

Let `T` be a `Q`-stationary self-adjoint Markov operator that commutes with the `G` action and preserves each `H_j`. Assume

\[
\|T|_{\mathcal H_j}\|_{2\to2}\le \tau_j,
\qquad 0\le\tau_j\le1.
\tag{4.2}
\]

For independent resampling noise on a product null, one has `tau_j=rho^j`, where `rho` is the one-coordinate Fourier retention factor.

For each exact degree define the invariant diagonal reproducing-kernel / leverage function

\[
\boxed{
\kappa_j^G(x)
=
\sum_{a=1}^{d_j}\psi_{j,a}(x)^2.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

This is basis-independent. Its `Q` expectation is exactly

\[
\mathbb E_Q\kappa_j^G=d_j.
\tag{4.4}
\]

For a cutoff `D` define the noise-weighted invariant leverage tail

\[
\boxed{
\mathscr L_{>D}^{G,T}(x)
=
\sum_{j>D}\tau_j^2\kappa_j^G(x).
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

### Theorem 4.1 — invariant leverage-tail framework

Let `P<<Q` be `G`-invariant. Write `TP` for the law after applying `T` to `P`. Then

\[
\boxed{
\chi^2(TP\|Q)
\le
\bigl(\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D}(P,Q)\bigr)^2
+
\mathbb E_P\mathscr L_{>D}^{G,T}(X).
}
\tag{4.6}
\]

Consequently, for any sequence `(P_n,Q_n,G_n,T_n,D_n)` satisfying

\[
D_n=(\log n)^{1+\delta_n},
\qquad \inf_n\delta_n>0,
\tag{4.7}
\]

if

\[
\sup_n\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D_n}(P_n,Q_n)\le A<\infty
\tag{4.8}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\sup_n
\mathbb E_{P_n}
\mathscr L_{>D_n}^{G_n,T_n}(X)
\le B<\infty,
}
\tag{4.9}
\]

then **no sequence of tests whatsoever** — hence in particular no polynomial statistic of any degree — can strongly distinguish `T_nP_n` from `Q_n`.

Quantitatively,

\[
\boxed{
\|T_nP_n-Q_n\|_{TV}
\le
1-
\frac{1}{2+A^2+B}
<1.
}
\tag{4.10}
\]

If instead both the advantage and the leverage-tail expectation tend to zero, then

\[
\|T_nP_n-Q_n\|_{TV}\to0.
\tag{4.11}
\]

The theorem also allows `T=I`: if `(4.9)` holds without noise then the same conclusion applies directly to `P_n` versus `Q_n`.

#### Proof

Let

\[
L=\frac{dP}{dQ}.
\]

Because `P` and `Q` are `G`-invariant, `L` is `G`-invariant `Q`-a.s. Therefore every exact-degree component of `L-1` lies in `H_j^G`. Write

\[
L-1
=
\sum_{j=1}^{M}L_j,
\qquad
L_j
=
\sum_{a=1}^{d_j}c_{j,a}\psi_{j,a}.
\tag{4.12}
\]

Since every `psi_{j,a}` for `j>=1` has `Q`-mean zero,

\[
c_{j,a}
=\langle L-1,\psi_{j,a}\rangle_Q
=\mathbb E_P\psi_{j,a}(X).
\tag{4.13}
\]

Because `T` is `Q`-stationary and self-adjoint, the density of `TP` relative to `Q` is `TL`. Hence

\[
\chi^2(TP\|Q)
=\|T(L-1)\|_2^2.
\tag{4.14}
\]

The grading is orthogonal and preserved by `T`, so from `(4.2)`

\[
\chi^2(TP\|Q)
\le
\sum_{j=1}^{M}\tau_j^2\|L_j\|_2^2.
\tag{4.15}
\]

For `j<=D`, `tau_j<=1`, so

\[
\sum_{j\le D}\tau_j^2\|L_j\|_2^2
\le
\sum_{j\le D}\|L_j\|_2^2.
\tag{4.16}
\]

The Riesz representation of the source low-degree advantage gives

\[
\sum_{j\le D}\|L_j\|_2^2
=
\|\Pi_{\le D}(L-1)\|_2^2
=
\bigl(\operatorname{Adv}^{\le D}(P,Q)\bigr)^2.
\tag{4.17}
\]

For the high-degree terms, define the vector

\[
\Psi_j(x)
=(\psi_{j,1}(x),\ldots,\psi_{j,d_j}(x)).
\]

Then `(4.13)` gives

\[
\|L_j\|_2^2
=
\sum_a c_{j,a}^2
=
\|\mathbb E_P\Psi_j(X)\|_2^2.
\tag{4.18}
\]

By Jensen's inequality for the convex function `v -> ||v||_2^2`,

\[
\|\mathbb E_P\Psi_j(X)\|_2^2
\le
\mathbb E_P\|\Psi_j(X)\|_2^2
=
\mathbb E_P\kappa_j^G(X).
\tag{4.19}
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_{j>D}\tau_j^2\|L_j\|_2^2
\le
\mathbb E_P
\sum_{j>D}\tau_j^2\kappa_j^G(X)
=
\mathbb E_P\mathscr L_{>D}^{G,T}(X).
\tag{4.20}
\]

Combining `(4.15)`–`(4.20)` proves `(4.6)`.

For the testing statement, if `R<<S` has likelihood ratio `Z`, then

\[
1-\|R-S\|_{TV}
=\mathbb E_S\min\{Z,1\}
\ge
\mathbb E_S\frac{Z}{1+Z}.
\]

Cauchy–Schwarz in Engel form yields

\[
1=(\mathbb E_S Z)^2
\le
\mathbb E_S\frac{Z}{1+Z}\,
\mathbb E_S[Z(1+Z)].
\]

Since `E_S Z=1` and `E_S Z^2=1+chi^2(R||S)`, the second factor equals `2+chi^2`. Hence

\[
1-\|R-S\|_{TV}
\ge
\frac{1}{2+\chi^2(R\|S)}.
\tag{4.21}
\]

Apply `(4.6)`, `(4.8)` and `(4.9)` to obtain `(4.10)`. If the right side of `(4.6)` tends to zero, the standard inequality `TV <= (1/2)sqrt(chi^2)` gives `(4.11)`. ∎

### Why condition (4.9) is not the desired conclusion in disguise

The quantity in `(4.9)` is a first moment under `P` of a function determined entirely by the null geometry, group action, polynomial grading and noise. It contains **no planted likelihood-ratio coefficients** and no testing supremum. It can be bounded by support geometry, orbit structure, hypercontractive estimates or combinatorial decomposition without first proving indistinguishability. The actual high-degree `L^2` energy is bounded by it through Jensen; Section 7 below shows exactly when that Jensen envelope is worst-case sharp.

A useful stronger-but-easier-to-check sufficient form is the **leverage-regularity condition**

\[
\mathbb E_P\kappa_j^G
\le C_j d_j,
\tag{4.22}
\]

because `E_Q kappa_j^G=d_j`. Under `(4.22)`,

\[
\mathbb E_P\mathscr L_{>D}^{G,T}
\le
\sum_{j>D}\tau_j^2 C_jd_j.
\tag{4.23}
\]

Thus the framework separates into two independently interpretable modules:

1. **representation growth/noise:** the weighted invariant dimensions `tau_j^2 d_j`;
2. **planted regularity:** how much more than the null the planted law can concentrate on invariant high-leverage states.

This is the point at which the criterion becomes structurally informative rather than merely an operator norm.

## 6. B6 — DEFORM TO A POINT YOU CAN SOLVE: why pure symmetry works at k=1 and fails by k=2

The source's original Hopkins formulation uses arrays indexed by `k`-subsets. The invariant dimensions in `(4.23)` expose a sharp structural difference between vector symmetry (`k=1`) and graph symmetry (`k=2`).

### 6.1 Coordinate permutation symmetry: exactly one invariant direction per exact degree

On the Boolean cube with coordinates indexed by `[n]`, exact degree-`j` Fourier characters are indexed by `j`-subsets of `[n]`. The full permutation group `S_n` is transitive on those subsets. Therefore

\[
\boxed{d_{n,j}^{(k=1)}=1\quad(0\le j\le n).}
\tag{6.1}
\]

At the representation-counting level, fixed noise therefore contributes only the geometric tail

\[
\sum_{j>D}\rho^{2j}
=
\frac{\rho^{2(D+1)}}{1-\rho^2}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

This does **not** by itself reprove Hsieh et al.'s stronger Boolean theorem because leverage regularity `(4.22)` need not be automatic. But it explains why the symmetry algebra has only one degree-`j` direction to control in the vector case.

### 6.2 Vertex-relabeling symmetry: invariant dimension already grows superexponentially in degree

Now let the Boolean coordinates be the edges of `K_n`, so `k=2` and `G=S_n` acts by vertex relabeling. Exact degree-`j` Fourier characters are indexed by `j`-edge subsets. Two characters lie in the same `S_n` orbit exactly when the corresponding simple graphs are isomorphic after isolated vertices are ignored. Hence

\[
\boxed{
 d_{n,j}^{(k=2)}
 =
 \#\{\text{unlabeled simple graph types with exactly }j\text{ edges and at most }n\text{ vertices}\}.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

This dimension is not merely larger than one; it is superexponential in `j` along a range far below `n`.

### Proposition 6.1 — elementary superexponential lower bound

For all sufficiently large integers `j`, set

\[
m=\left\lfloor\frac{j}{4\log j}\right\rfloor,
\qquad
M={m\choose2}.
\]

Whenever `n>=m` and `M>=j`, one has

\[
\boxed{
 d_{n,j}^{(k=2)}
 \ge
 \frac{\binom{M}{j}}{m!}.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
\log d_{n,j}^{(k=2)}
\ge
j\log j-2j\log\log j-O(j).
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

In particular, for every fixed `rho in (0,1)` and every sequence `j->infinity` with `n>=j/(4 log j)`, 

\[
\boxed{
\rho^{2j}d_{n,j}^{(k=2)}\to\infty.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

#### Proof

There are exactly `binom(M,j)` labelled simple graphs with vertex set `[m]` and exactly `j` edges. Any single unlabeled graph type contributes at most `m!` labelled graphs on `[m]`. Therefore the number of isomorphism types, and hence the invariant dimension for `n>=m`, is at least `(6.4)`.

For `M>=j`, the elementary product comparison

\[
\binom{M}{j}
=\prod_{r=0}^{j-1}\frac{M-r}{j-r}
\ge
\left(\frac{M}{j}\right)^j
\tag{6.7}
\]

holds because `(M-r)/(j-r)>=M/j` term by term. Also `m!<=m^m`. For the chosen `m`,

\[
\frac{M}{j}
\ge
c\frac{j}{(\log j)^2}
\]

for an absolute `c>0` and all large `j`, while

\[
m\log m=O(j).
\]

Taking logarithms in `(6.4)` and using these estimates gives `(6.5)`. Since the positive `j log j` term dominates every fixed linear penalty `2j log(1/rho)`, `(6.6)` follows. ∎

### B6 outcome

This deformation supplies a concrete reason that “just add permutation symmetry and noise” cannot be the whole framework. In the vector case, invariant representation complexity is one-dimensional at each degree. In the graph case, there are already `exp(j log j(1-o(1)))` invariant graph-shape directions at exact degree `j`. Fixed noise gives only an exponential penalty `rho^{2j}`, which cannot beat this dimension growth by itself.

Therefore a graph-side theorem must use **additional planted structure** to suppress most invariant directions. Chen–Li do exactly this through small-interface/treewidth decompositions in their planted-subgraph setting. Mao demonstrates that a code/rank construction can avoid such suppression. The leverage-tail criterion turns that qualitative observation into a single quantitative place where the extra structure must enter.

Importantly, I do **not** claim Chen–Li's theorem automatically verifies leverage regularity `(4.22)`; their coefficientwise decomposition may be strictly sharper than this sufficient envelope.

## 7. B24 — FIND THE EXTREMAL OBJECT: the worst symmetric law is a single orbit

Define the actual noise-weighted invariant high-degree energy

\[
\mathfrak H_D(P)
=
\sum_{j>D}\tau_j^2
\left\|
\mathbb E_P\Psi_j(X)
\right\|_2^2.
\tag{7.1}
\]

This is the exact high-degree quantity appearing before Jensen in the proof of Theorem 4.1.

The set of `G`-invariant probability measures is the simplex whose extreme points are the uniform laws on individual `G`-orbits. Since `(7.1)` is a sum of squared norms of affine functions of `P`, it is convex in `P`. Therefore its maximum over all `G`-invariant laws occurs at an extreme point.

If `O` is an orbit and `Q_O` is the uniform law on `O`, each invariant basis vector `psi_{j,a}` is constant on `O`. Hence for any `x in O`,

\[
\left\|\mathbb E_{Q_O}\Psi_j\right\|_2^2
=
\|\Psi_j(x)\|_2^2
=
\kappa_j^G(x).
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\sup_{P:\,G\text{-invariant}}\mathfrak H_D(P)
=
\max_{x\in\Omega}
\mathscr L_{>D}^{G,T}(x).
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Moreover, for a single-orbit law the Jensen step `(4.19)` is equality degree by degree. So the leverage envelope is **worst-case sharp**: no uniformly smaller bound depending only on the pointwise invariant feature norms can replace it for all symmetric planted laws.

This identifies the extremal obstruction cleanly. If one wants a source theorem broader than `(4.9)`, it must use information beyond mere symmetry and pointwise invariant leverage — for example decomposition, anti-concentration, code complexity, local interfaces, or some other restriction on how the planted law combines orbits.

## 8. Mao stress test under the new criterion

For Mao's graph distributions, the low-degree advantage through `D_n` is exactly zero, while after every fixed positive independent resampling rate a polynomial-time rank test strongly distinguishes. Therefore the noised total-variation distance tends to one along the construction.

If the leverage-tail expectations in `(4.9)` were uniformly bounded, Theorem 4.1 with `A=0` would give a uniform total-variation gap below one. Contradiction. Hence Mao necessarily satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E_{P_n}
\mathscr L_{>D_n}^{S_n,T_\varepsilon}(X)
\to\infty
\text{ along an unbounded subsequence}
}
\tag{8.1}
\]

for every fixed resampling parameter covered by his strong-distinguishing theorem.

Thus the new condition excludes Mao for a mathematically structural reason: his planted law must concentrate enough mass in the enormous graph-symmetry high-degree leverage tail to overcome fixed noise. This is stronger than merely saying “rank is global.”
