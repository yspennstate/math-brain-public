\mathcal H(x)=\mathcal H(0)

The statement there that `coordinatewise monotonicity gives the zero gradient` was too terse: monotonicity alone would not force a vanishing one-sided derivative. The zero gradient follows instead from the exact first-atom derivative already derived in equation (6.5).

At the decoupled face,
\[
p_0(s,y)=\mu(s)q_y,
\qquad
\partial_{x_i}p_0(s,y)=-\mu(s)R_i(s_i)a_{i,y},
\]
with
\[
\sum_y a_{i,y}=0,
\qquad
\mathbb E_\mu R_i=0.
\]
Since total derivative mass is zero,
\[
\partial_{x_i}\mathcal H(0)
=-\sum_{s,y}\partial_{x_i}p_0(s,y)\log p_0(s,y).
\]
Using
\[
\log p_0(s,y)=\log\mu(s)+\log q_y,
\]
the first factorized term contains \(\sum_y a_{i,y}=0\), and the second contains \(\mathbb E_\mu R_i=0\). Hence
\[
\boxed{\partial_{x_i}\mathcal H(0)=0\quad\text{for every }i.}
\]
Together with the exact Hessian identity (6.6), this rigorously gives the Taylor expansion (7.1) and therefore the raw-amplitude quartic law (7.2).

=\frac d{dt}\Big|_{0}P_{C+t u_i u_i^*}(j\in Y)

J_i=\mathbb Eg_i^2
\ge\frac{|(u_i)_j|^4}{C_{jj}(1-C_{jj})}.
