## 3. B1 — change category: axisymmetric vector mixing is a one-dimensional polygon-closure transport problem

The preceding report handled `n=3`. The correct general statement is cleaner and works for every `n>=3`.

Let `rho` be a probability law on `[-1,1]` with mean `m`. Define the axisymmetric law `A(rho)` on the unit sphere `S^2` by

\[
Z\sim\rho,\qquad \Theta\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi),\qquad
X=(r(Z)\cos\Theta,r(Z)\sin\Theta,Z),
\]

where

\[
r(z)=\sqrt{1-z^2},
\]

and `Z,Theta` are independent.

### Theorem 3.1 — exact axisymmetric `n`-gon criterion

`A(rho)` is vector `n`-CM if and only if there is a coupling

\[
(Z_1,\ldots,Z_n),\qquad Z_i\sim\rho,
\]

such that almost surely

\[
\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i=nm
\tag{3.1}
\]

and the horizontal radii

\[
r_i=\sqrt{1-Z_i^2}
\]

satisfy the polygon inequalities

\[
\boxed{\quad 2\max_i r_i\le \sum_{i=1}^n r_i.\quad}
\tag{3.2}
\]

#### Proof

Because `A(rho)` is bounded, any vector complete mix has center equal to its mean, namely `(0,0,m)`. Thus a vector complete mix has

\[
\sum_i Z_i=nm
\]

and its horizontal components are planar vectors of lengths `r_1,...,r_n` summing to zero. A finite set of nonnegative lengths can be the sides of a closed planar polygon if and only if its largest length does not exceed the sum of the others. This is exactly (3.2), proving necessity.

Conversely, suppose (3.1)–(3.2) hold. For each realized radius vector satisfying (3.2), choose a closed planar polygon with oriented edge angles `theta_1,...,theta_n`, so that

\[
\sum_i r_i e^{\mathrm i\theta_i}=0.
\]

A Borel choice can be made by sorting the radii with deterministic tie breaking and using the standard successive polygon construction; the feasible-angle correspondence is nonempty and closed. Now add one independent common random angle `U~Unif[0,2pi)` and replace every `theta_i` by `theta_i+U`. Conditional on the entire `Z`-tuple, each resulting angle is uniform; hence the `i`th vector has exactly law `A(rho)`. The horizontal sums remain zero and the vertical sum is `nm`. Therefore the vector sum is constant `(0,0,nm)`. `QED`

This changes the problem category: in the axisymmetric class, vector complete mixability is exactly scalar complete mixability plus one deterministic polygon-support constraint on the scalar coupling.

### Theorem 3.2 — integer-center automatic lifting

Assume `rho` is scalar `n`-CM with center `m` and

\[
\boxed{\quad k:=\frac{n(1-m)}2\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}\cap\mathbb Z.\quad}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Then `A(rho)` is vector `n`-CM.

#### Proof

Take any scalar complete mix `Z_1,...,Z_n` with sum `nm`. Put

\[
w_i=\frac{1-Z_i}{2}\in[0,1].
\]

Then

\[
\sum_i w_i=\frac{n-nm}{2}=k\in\mathbb Z,
\]

and

\[
r_i=2\sqrt{w_i(1-w_i)}.
\]

Write

\[
f(w)=\sqrt{w(1-w)}.
\]

The function `f` is concave on `[0,1]` and satisfies `f(0)=f(1)=0`. Fix `i`. Among all `(n-1)`-tuples `w_j in [0,1]`, `j!=i`, having sum `k-w_i`, the concave function

\[
\sum_{j\ne i} f(w_j)
\]

attains a minimum at an extreme point of that slice of the cube. At such an extreme point all but at most one coordinate lie in `{0,1}`. Since `k` is integral, the unique possible fractional coordinate is `1-w_i`; therefore

\[
\sum_{j\ne i}f(w_j)\ge f(1-w_i)=f(w_i).
\]

Multiplying by `2` gives

\[
r_i\le\sum_{j\ne i}r_j.
\]

This holds for every `i`, so the polygon condition (3.2) is automatic. Theorem 3.1 then supplies the vector complete mix. `QED`

#### Consequences

This rules out a broad class of would-be counterexamples without checking all directions:

- if `n` is even and `m=0`, then `k=n/2`, so **every centered axisymmetric sphere law whose axial law is scalar `n`-CM is automatically vector `n`-CM**;
- for `n=3`, means `m=1/3` and `m=-1/3` are automatic-lift centers;
- more generally the automatic centers are `m=1-2k/n`, `k=1,...,n-1`.

This is a proper-class theorem, hence `PARTIAL`; it does not move `C_proj`.

## 4. B5 — add a parameter: the `n=3` polygon constraint collapses to one exact cubic moment screen

For `n=3`, set

\[
s=3m,\qquad q=Z_1^2+Z_2^2+Z_3^2,\qquad p=Z_1Z_2Z_3.
\]

The triangle condition for the horizontal radii can be tested by Heron's discriminant

\[
H=2\sum_{i<j}r_i^2r_j^2-\sum_i r_i^4.
\]

For nonnegative side lengths, `H>=0` is equivalent to the triangle inequalities. Substituting `r_i^2=1-Z_i^2` and using `Z_1+Z_2+Z_3=s` gives the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
H=s^4+3-2(1+s^2)q-8sp.
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Therefore an axisymmetric vector `3`-mix is exactly a scalar constant-sum coupling supported on the semialgebraic set `H>=0`.

Let

\[
m_j=\mathbb E[Z^j],\qquad s=3m_1.
\]

For any constant-sum coupling, Newton's identity gives

\[
\mathbb E[p]=m_3+\frac{s^3}{6}-\frac{3s}{2}m_2.
\]

Taking expectations in (4.1) yields a marginal-only necessary condition:

\[
\boxed{
3-\frac{s^4}{3}+6(s^2-1)m_2-8sm_3\ge0.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This is not sufficient, but it is an exact low-order screen for axisymmetric vector `3`-CM.

Two useful specializations sharpen the search.

### Centered case

If `s=0`, then pointwise

\[
H=3-2(Z_1^2+Z_2^2+Z_3^2).
\tag{4.3}
\]

Hence the vector lift requires a zero-sum scalar complete mix supported on

\[
Z_1^2+Z_2^2+Z_3^2\le\frac32.
\]

If `rho` is centered, scalar `3`-CM, and supported in the belt

\[
|Z|\le\frac{\sqrt3}{2},
\]

then every zero-sum triple in the support satisfies `sum Z_i^2<=3/2`: after choosing the sign with two entries on one side and one on the other, the maximum under the zero-sum and box constraints occurs at a permutation of `(a,-a,0)` with `a<=sqrt(3)/2`. Thus every scalar complete mix automatically lifts to a vector one.

So any centered axisymmetric `n=3` counterexample must place mass outside that belt.

### The special centers `s=+/-1`

For `s=1`, put `w_i=(1-Z_i)/2`. Then `sum w_i=1` and (4.1) factors exactly as

\[
\boxed{H=64w_1w_2w_3\ge0.}
\tag{4.4}
\]

The `s=-1` case follows by reflection. This is the `n=3` instance of Theorem 3.2 and explains why the preceding run's linear-tilt endpoint at mean `1/3` could not be a counterexample.

## 5. B23 — assume the opposite: build projection-rich candidate families and see exactly where the obstruction survives

The goal of this route was to produce an actual projection-wise `3`-CM law that violates (4.2). The most tractable deformation is a zonal quadratic density on `S^2`.

Let

\[
P_2(z)=\frac{3z^2-1}{2},
\]

and define the sphere density, relative to uniform surface probability, by

\[
f_{a,b}(x)=1+a x_3+bP_2(x_3),
\qquad 0\le 3b\le a\le1.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The parameter restriction makes `f_{a,b}` nonnegative: the quadratic is convex and its vertex lies at or left of `-1`, so the minimum on `[-1,1]` is at `z=-1`, where it is `1-a+b>=0`.

For a unit line direction `u`, write `c=u\cdot e_3` and `Y=u\cdot X`. Circle averaging / the degree-two addition identity gives the projected density

\[
\boxed{
g_c(y)=\frac12\left[1+acy+bP_2(c)P_2(y)\right],\qquad -1<y<1.
}
\tag{5.2}

For line projections this gives an exact two-regime complete-mixability certificate:

1. If `|c|<=1/sqrt(3)`, then `P_2(c)<=0`, so the quadratic coefficient in `y` is nonpositive. Thus `g_c` is concave and Puccetti–Wang–Wang Theorem 4.2 makes it `3`-CM.
2. If `|c|>=1/sqrt(3)`, then `P_2(c)>=0`. For `c>0`,

   \[
   g_c'(y)=\frac12\left(ac+3bP_2(c)y\right).
   \]

   Its minimum is at `y=-1`. Since `P_2(c)<=c` on `[1/sqrt(3),1]` and `a>=3b`,

   \[
   ac-3bP_2(c)\ge0.
   \]

   Hence `g_c` is increasing. The `c<0` case is decreasing by reflection. Its mean is `ac/3`, which lies in the exact monotone-density mean interval `[-1/3,1/3]` because `a<=1`. Thus Wang–Wang's monotone-density theorem again gives `3`-CM.

Therefore **every one-dimensional projection of (5.1) is rigorously `3`-CM** throughout the wedge `0<=3b<=a<=1`.

Important source-scope qualification: this is a line-projection theorem in ambient `R^3`. Open Problem 6 is phrased using every essential proper subspace, so I do **not** promote (5.1) to a full source-valid counterexample candidate until its essential two-dimensional projections are also handled. The family is still valuable as a controlled projective-consistency laboratory and as a candidate if the source is specialized to line projections.

For its axial law,

\[
m_1=\frac a3,\qquad
m_2=\frac13+\frac{2b}{15},\qquad
m_3=\frac a5,
\]

so the necessary vector moment quantity (4.2) becomes

\[
\boxed{
M(a,b)=1+\frac{2a^2}{5}-\frac{a^4}{3}+\frac{4b}{5}(a^2-1).
}
\tag{5.3}

This counterexample route fails at the low-order screen: throughout the projection-certified wedge,

\[
M(a,b)\ge \frac25>0.
\]

Indeed `a^2-1<=0`, so the worst allowed `b` is `b=a/3`; crudely the two negative terms are bounded below by `-1/3` and `-4/15`, while the remaining `2a^2/5` is nonnegative. Thus the new moment obstruction cannot separate this family.

This is a useful failure rather than another checker: it says a successful axisymmetric counterexample must exploit the **pointwise coupling support constraint** `H>=0`, not merely its first averaged moment inequality.
