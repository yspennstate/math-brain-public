## 3. Witness recalled: period-eight square-star network

Cities are `Z^2`. Start from every unit horizontal and vertical square-grid edge. Select precisely the unit squares whose lower-left corner `(a,b)` satisfies

\[
a+3b\equiv0\pmod 8.

For every selected square delete its four boundary unit edges, add the square center, and join the center to the four corners. No two selected squares share an edge, and the selected-square density is `1/8`. Therefore

      =\frac{6+\sqrt2}{4}<2.

## 4. B20: why the obvious local shortening fails

A natural reaction to the counterexample is to try to shorten each selected-square gadget below the center-star length `2sqrt(2)`. The simplest possibility is a single hub `z` inside or outside the unit square joined by straight spokes to all four square corners `c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4` in cyclic order, with the four boundary roads removed.

### One-hub local optimality lemma

If this one-hub replacement respects the source stretch bound for all four adjacent corner pairs, then its total spoke length is at least `2sqrt(2)`; in fact equality is forced and `z` is the center of the square.

**Proof.** Put `r_i=|z-c_i|`. Since adjacent corners are Euclidean distance one, the source condition requires

\[
r_i+r_{i+1}\le\sqrt2
\]

for all four cyclic indices. Summing gives

\[
2\sum_{i=1}^4 r_i\le4\sqrt2,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\sum_i r_i\le2\sqrt2. \tag{4.1}
\]

On the other hand, pair opposite corners. Each opposite pair has Euclidean distance `sqrt(2)`, so the triangle inequality gives

\[
r_1+r_3\ge\sqrt2,
\qquad
r_2+r_4\ge\sqrt2.
\]

Thus

\[
\sum_i r_i\ge2\sqrt2. \tag{4.2}
\]

Equations (4.1) and (4.2) force equality throughout. Equality in both opposite-corner triangle inequalities forces `z` to lie on both diagonals, hence `z` is their intersection, the square center. Therefore the unique admissible one-hub optimum has total length `2sqrt(2)`, exactly the gadget already used. QED.

So moving the hub or choosing an asymmetric single-hub star cannot improve the witness length.

The globally shortest Euclidean Steiner tree on four square corners also does not solve the problem. In its standard two-Steiner-point `H` topology, the two Steiner points lie on the horizontal midline at horizontal offset `1/(2sqrt(3))` from the left/right sides. The route joining the two bottom corners has length

\[
\frac1{\sqrt3}+\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)+\frac1{\sqrt3}
=1+\frac1{\sqrt3}
>\sqrt2,
\]

because `(1+1/sqrt(3))^2=4/3+2/sqrt(3)>2`. Thus the usual shorter Steiner tree violates the adjacent-city source constraint. The local obstacle is real: shortening a selected-square gadget requires a topology more subtle than either a displaced one-hub star or the unconstrained square Steiner tree.

This lemma is restricted to the local one-hub family; it is **not** asserted as a global optimality theorem for all possible gadgets.

## 5. B30: sharpen the macro descent from radius 52 to radius 29

The preceding report used eight phase-uniform macros:

  \[

Normalize each macro vector `v` by its certified cost `c`. Their convex hull is a centrally symmetric octagon. The previous proof computed its exact edge inradius

\[
h^2=\frac{14720-8448\sqrt2}{4513}.
\]

It then threw away substantial slack and used only `h>3/4`. This run keeps more of the exact geometry.

### Sharper rational inradius bound

I claim

\[
\boxed{h>\frac{47}{60}}. \tag{5.1}
\]

After clearing positive denominators, `h^2>(47/60)^2` is exactly

\[
43022783-30412800\sqrt2>0.
\]

Both integers are positive and

\[
43022783^2-2(30412800)^2
=1083049385089>0,
\]

so (5.1) follows.

Hence for every nonzero displacement `d`, with `r=|d|` and direction `e=d/r`, one of the eight macros `(v,c)` satisfies

\[
e\cdot v\ge\frac{47}{60}c,
\qquad
 d\cdot v\ge\frac{47}{60}rc. \tag{5.2}
\]

Put `s=sqrt(2)`. We want the one-step induction inequality

\[
c+s\,|d-v|\le sr. \tag{5.3}
\]

Once `sr>c`, squaring (5.3) shows it is equivalent to

\[
4d\cdot v\ge2src+2|v|^2-c^2. \tag{5.4}
\]

By (5.2), it is enough that

\[
\left(\frac{47}{15}-2s\right)rc
\ge 2|v|^2-c^2. \tag{5.5}
\]

The coefficient is positive because `47-30sqrt(2)>0`.

For an axial macro, `|v|^2=64`, `c=A`, and

\[
2|v|^2-A^2=84-24s.
\]

At `r=29`, the margin in (5.5) is

\[
\frac{1146}{5}-\frac{2134}{15}s,
\]

or, after multiplying by `15`,

\[
3438-2134s>0,
\]

whose squared integer margin is

\[
3438^2-2\cdot2134^2=2711932>0.
\]

For a diagonal macro, `|v|^2=512`, `c=D`, and

\[
2|v|^2-D^2=602-252s.
\]

At `r=29`, the margin is

\[
\frac{1108}{5}-\frac{2339}{15}s,
\]

or

\[
3324-2339s>0,
\]

with

\[
3324^2-2\cdot2339^2=107134>0.
\]

Since the coefficient of `r` in (5.5) is positive, these checks prove (5.5) for every `r>=29`. Also

\[
29s-A=27s-6>0,
\qquad
29s-D=22s-18>0,

so the preliminary `sr>c` condition holds. Therefore (5.3) holds for every displacement of norm at least `29`. Because `c>0`, (5.3) also gives

so the same Euclidean-norm induction from the preceding report applies with threshold `29` instead of `52`.
