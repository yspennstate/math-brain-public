## 2. Preserved extremal setup

Put

\[
L:=\log t,
\qquad
M:=\max\{m:2\le m\le n/2,\ \binom nm=t\},
\]

and let the common `M`-rough core be

\[
W:=W_M(t):=\prod_{\substack{p\mid t\\p>M}}p^{v_p(t)}.
\tag{2.1}
\]

For every rough prime `p>M`, set

\[
Z_p:=p^{v_p(t)}.
\]

For every represented left-half row `(n_j,m_j)`, because `p>M\ge m_j`, the full component `Z_p` occurs in exactly one numerator factor. Hence there is a unique residue

\[
0\le r_{j,p}<m_j,
\qquad
Z_p\mid n_j-r_{j,p}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

The surviving exponent-one candidate regime from the earlier reports is the close deep edge in which, on any fixed block of rows considered below,

\[
m_j=(1+o(1))m,
\qquad
m\log m=o(L),
\qquad
\log W=(1-o(1))L.
\tag{2.3}
\]

The binomial equation in this regime gives uniformly on such a fixed block

\[
\log n_j=\frac Lm+O(\log m),
\qquad
\frac{m\log n_j}{L}=1+o(1).
\tag{2.4}
\]

Order the rows by increasing lower index and decreasing row index. For consecutive rows define

\[
D_i:=n_i-n_{i+1}>0.
\tag{2.5}
\]

The previous report's normalized gap coefficient is, up to `o(1)`,

\gamma_i:=\frac{m\log(D_i+m)}{L}
=\frac{1+\sigma_i}{2}+o(1),
\tag{2.6}

## 3. B18 — translate the whole block into a multi-marginal rough-core problem

The key observation is that the exact divisor-class argument works for every pair of represented rows, not only consecutive old-slot transitions.

Put logarithmic probability weight on the rough components:

\[
\mu(p):=\frac{\log Z_p}{\log W},
\qquad
\sum_{p>M}\mu(p)=1.
\tag{3.1}
\]

For each row `j`, regard `R_j(p):=r_{j,p}` as a random variable on this finite probability space.

### Lemma 3.1 — one-row atom cap

For fixed `j` and fixed residue `r`,

\[
\Pr(R_j=r)\le \frac{1+o(1)}m.
\tag{3.2}
\]

#### Proof

The prime-power components in the class `R_j=r` are pairwise coprime and each divides `n_j-r`. Therefore their product divides `n_j-r`, and

\[
\sum_{p:R_j(p)=r}\log Z_p\le\log(n_j-r)\le\log n_j.
\]

Divide by `log W=(1-o(1))L` and use (2.4). `QED`

Center each residue at its row midpoint:

\[
U_j:=R_j-\frac{m_j-1}{2}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

Since `R_j` is supported on `m_j=(1+o(1))m` consecutive values and every atom has mass at most `(1+o(1))/m`,

\[
\mathbb E U_j^2
\le
\frac{1+o(1)}m
\sum_{r=0}^{m_j-1}\left(r-\frac{m_j-1}{2}\right)^2
=\left(\frac1{12}+o(1)\right)m^2.
\tag{3.4}
\]

### Lemma 3.2 — every pair has an exact difference-class atom cap

For `j<k`, write

\[
D_{jk}:=n_j-n_k>0,
\qquad
c_{jk}:=\frac{m\log(D_{jk}+m)}L.
\tag{3.5}
\]

Then every atom of the translated lattice variable `U_j-U_k` has mass at most

\[
\frac{c_{jk}+o(1)}m.
\tag{3.6}
\]

#### Proof

Fix an integer difference `d`. Every component with

\[
R_j-R_k=d
\]

divides

\[
(n_j-R_j)-(n_k-R_k)=D_{jk}-d.
\]

In the deep edge, pairwise row separation gives `D_{jk}>|d|`, so this is a nonzero positive integer. The product of all components in the class therefore divides `D_{jk}-d`, and its logarithmic mass is at most

\[
\log(D_{jk}+m).
\]

Divide by `log W=(1-o(1))L`. Translating from `R_j-R_k` to `U_j-U_k` only translates the lattice and does not change atom masses. `QED`

### Lemma 3.3 — capped lattice second-moment floor

If a lattice-valued random variable `X` has

\[
\sup_x\Pr(X=x)\le\frac{c+o(1)}m
\tag{3.7}
\]

for fixed `c>0`, then

\[
\mathbb E X^2
\ge
\left(\frac1{12c^2}-o(1)\right)m^2.
\tag{3.8}
\]

#### Proof

Among laws with atom cap `q`, the second moment about zero is minimized by filling lattice points in increasing order of absolute value. Here `q=(c+o(1))/m`, so one needs `m/c+o(m)` occupied atoms, filling to radius

\[
R=\frac{m}{2c}+o(m).
\]

Thus

\[
\mathbb E X^2
\ge
q\left(2\sum_{r=1}^{R+O(1)}r^2\right)-o(m^2)
=\left(\frac1{12c^2}-o(1)\right)m^2.
\]

The same leading term holds for a half-integer translate of the lattice. `QED`

Combining Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3,

\[
\mathbb E(U_j-U_k)^2
\ge
\left(\frac1{12c_{jk}^2}-o(1)\right)m^2.
\tag{3.9}
\]

### Theorem 3.4 — all-lag rough-core energy inequality

For every fixed block size `s>=2` satisfying (2.3),

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{0\le j<k<s}\frac1{c_{jk}^2}
\le s^2+o(1).
}
\tag{3.10}
\]

#### Proof

Use the Hilbert-space variance identity pointwise in the rough-component probability space:

\[
\sum_{j<k}(U_j-U_k)^2
=s\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}(U_j-\bar U)^2
\le s\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}U_j^2,
\tag{3.11}
\]

where `bar U=s^{-1}sum_j U_j`. Take expectations. By (3.4),

\[
\sum_{j<k}\mathbb E(U_j-U_k)^2
\le \left(\frac{s^2}{12}+o(1)\right)m^2.
\tag{3.12}
\]

By (3.9), the same left-hand side is at least

\[
\left(\frac1{12}\sum_{j<k}c_{jk}^{-2}-o(1)\right)m^2.
\]

Cancel `m^2/12`. `QED`

This is the desired higher-order labeled-residue invariant: all `s choose 2` pairwise divisor-class capacities are coupled by one common family of rough prime-power labels.

## 4. Convert all lags to consecutive transition gaps

For `j<k`,

\[
D_{jk}=\sum_{i=j}^{k-1}D_i.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Because `s` is fixed and `L/m\to\infty`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_{jk}
&=\frac{m\log(D_{jk}+m)}L\\
&\le
\max_{j\le i<k}\frac{m\log(D_i+m)}L+o(1)\\
&=\max_{j\le i<k}\gamma_i+o(1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Therefore Theorem 3.4 implies

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{0\le j<k<s}
\frac1{\left(\max_{j\le i<k}\gamma_i\right)^2}
\le s^2+o(1).
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

A simple consequence is that if all consecutive gaps in an `s`-row block obey `gamma_i<=c+o(1)`, then

\[
\frac{\binom s2}{c^2}\le s^2+o(1),
\]

so

\[
\boxed{
c\ge\sqrt{\frac{s-1}{2s}}-o(1).}
\tag{4.4}
\]

For fixed `s` this is rigorous; taking larger and larger fixed `s` after the asymptotic limit makes the threshold tend to `1/sqrt(2)`. This already shows that the multi-marginal second-moment layer can force substantially more than the original pairwise `1/2`, but it does not approach one.

## 5. B16 — solve the three-row transport relaxation exactly

Rather than archive an approximate finite LP, solve the first genuinely higher-order case on the page. Take three consecutive rows, with normalized consecutive gaps `gamma_0,gamma_1`. Put

\[
a:=\max(\gamma_0,\gamma_1),
\qquad
b:=\min(\gamma_0,\gamma_1).
\]

The three pair terms in (3.10), together with (4.2), give

\[
\boxed{
\frac2{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}\le9+o(1).
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

The support size of a difference law also gives `a,b>=1/2-o(1)`.

We now minimize `(a+b)/2` subject to the limiting constraints

\[
a\ge b\ge\frac12,
\qquad
\frac2{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}\le9.
\tag{5.2}
\]

At an interior minimizer the inequality is equality. Lagrange multipliers for `a+b` under `2a^{-2}+b^{-2}=9` give

\[
a^3=2b^3,
\qquad
a=2^{1/3}b.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Substituting into the constraint,

\[
\frac{1+2^{1/3}}{b^2}=9,
\qquad
b=\frac{\sqrt{1+2^{1/3}}}{3},
\tag{5.4}
\]

and this value is indeed greater than `1/2`. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\gamma_0+\gamma_1}{2}
\ge
c_3-o(1),
\qquad
c_3:=\frac{(1+2^{1/3})^{3/2}}6
=0.5662244918\ldots.
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

The boundary `b=1/2` gives the slightly larger value `(1/2+sqrt(2/5))/2`, and the boundary `a=b` gives `1/sqrt(3)`, so (5.5) is the true limiting minimum.

Applying (5.5) to every adjacent pair of transitions in a long uniformly close chain and summing,

\[
\boxed{
\frac1K\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\gamma_i
\ge c_3-o(1).
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

Using `gamma_i=(1+sigma_i)/2+o(1)` from (2.6),

\[
\boxed{
\frac1K\sum_i\sigma_i
\ge 2c_3-1-o(1)
=0.1324489837\ldots-o(1).
}
\tag{5.7}
\]

This strictly improves the previous certified values `2/sqrt(15)=0.516397...` and `4/sqrt(15)-1=0.032795...`.

No exact-computation artifact is needed: the finite three-row relaxation has been solved analytically. A private floating scratch check was used only to suggest what to optimize and is not evidence for any claim in this report.

## 6. Why the multi-marginal second-moment route still cannot reach one

Theorem 3.4 is stronger than the previous two-step argument, but its own limiting geometry exposes a new barrier.

The inequality (3.10) comes entirely from two ingredients:

1. one-dimensional marginal second moments bounded by the nearly uniform residue marginals; and
2. pair-difference second moments bounded below by divisor-class atom caps.

At the level of covariance alone, the simplex bound underlying (3.10) is natural and sharp. In probability/optimal-transport language, uniform laws are jointly/ completely mixable: one can couple identical uniform marginals so that their sum is constant. After symmetrization, if the centered coordinates have common variance `v` and constant sum, their pairwise covariance is

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(U_j,U_k)=-\frac{v}{s-1}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Consequently

\[
\mathbb E(U_j-U_k)^2
=\frac{2s}{s-1}v.
\tag{6.2}
\]

With the uniform variance `v~m^2/12`, comparing (6.2) to the difference-class floor `m^2/(12c^2)` gives exactly

\[
c^2\ge\frac{s-1}{2s},
\tag{6.3}
\]

the threshold in (4.4). Complete-mixability literature proves that uniform distributions belong to the jointly/ completely mixable classes, so this is not an artificial covariance configuration. This does **not** prove that the full arithmetic atom-cap relaxation is attained by a Singmaster configuration; it proves only that no argument using just the covariance/second-moment layer can force the coefficient to one.

Thus the live problem has changed materially: stacking more second-moment transport constraints is not enough. One must exploit the **integer labels of each component across the whole chain**.

## 7. B27 — fix one rough component and follow its whole quotient trajectory

Fix a single rough prime-power component `Z=Z_p`. Write every row as

\[
n_i=Zq_i+r_i,
\qquad
0\le r_i<m_i\le M.
\tag{7.1}
\]

The preceding reports prove that on every consecutive deep-edge represented transition the quotient strictly decreases:

\[
q_i-q_{i+1}\ge1.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Set

\[
\delta_i:=q_i-q_{i+1}\in\mathbb N_{\ge1}.
\]

Then the row gap has the exact decomposition

\[
D_i=Z\delta_i+(r_i-r_{i+1}).
\tag{7.3}
\]

Subtract two consecutive gaps:

\[
D_i-D_{i+1}
=Z(\delta_i-\delta_{i+1})
+(r_i-2r_{i+1}+r_{i+2}).
\tag{7.4}
\]

Since every residue lies in `[0,M)`,

\[
|r_i-2r_{i+1}+r_{i+2}|<2M.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Hence the following exact curvature-modulus dichotomy holds:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{dist}(D_i-D_{i+1},Z\mathbb Z)<2M.
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

In particular, if `Z>2M` then either

\[
|D_i-D_{i+1}|<2M
\]

or

\[
|D_i-D_{i+1}|\ge Z-2M.
\tag{7.7}
\]

The same statement persists to all finite differences. Let `Delta` denote the forward finite-difference operator in the chain index. From (7.1),

\[
\Delta^k n_i=Z\Delta^k q_i+\Delta^k r_i,
\]

and the binomial coefficients in the finite-difference operator give

\[
|\Delta^k r_i|<2^kM.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{dist}(\Delta^k n_i,Z\mathbb Z)<2^kM.
}
\tag{7.8}
\]

If `Z>2^kM` and

\[
|\Delta^k n_i|<Z-2^kM,
\]

then necessarily

\[
\Delta^k q_i=0,
\qquad
|\Delta^k n_i|<2^kM.
\tag{7.9}
\]

Thus a sufficiently flat real row-index trajectory forces the quotient trajectory of **each labeled rough component** to become a low-degree integer polynomial on that block.

This is promising but not yet source-closing. In the ultraflat regime the available rough components can be only `exp(M^{o(1)})`, while high-order finite differences carry the rapidly growing error `2^kM`; pushing `k` upward recreates a denominator/coefficient-growth obstruction reminiscent of the high-order interpolation loss in Kane's method. The new leverage would have to come from imposing (7.8) **simultaneously for many coprime `Z_p`**, not from one modulus at a time.
