# 4. B6 — deform to a solved point: why the naive discriminant-birth strategy fails over finite fields

The intended B6 route was to deform a macroscopic offset vector continuously toward a bounded-offset strand, use the bounded-offset root theorem there, and argue that new modular roots could appear only when the moving polynomial crosses a discriminant or a component at projective infinity.

That principle is false over finite fields even in the simplest possible family.

Let `p` be odd and consider
\[
f_\lambda(x)=x^2-\lambda\in\mathbf F_p[x],
\qquad \lambda\in\mathbf F_p^\times.
\tag{4.1}
\]
Its discriminant is
\[
\operatorname{Disc}(f_\lambda)=4\lambda,
\tag{4.2}
\]
which is nonzero for every parameter in `F_p^*`. The projective degree is constant and there is no root appearing from infinity. Nevertheless the number of rational roots is
\[
\#\{x\in\mathbf F_p:x^2=\lambda\}
=
\begin{cases}
2,&\lambda\text{ a quadratic residue},\\
0,&\lambda\text{ a quadratic nonresidue}.
\end{cases}
\tag{4.3}
\]
Thus the `F_p`-rational fiber changes while the family remains smooth and while the discriminant never vanishes. What changes is Frobenius splitting, not the geometry of the total family.

This gives a precise route failure:

\[
\boxed{
\text{A discriminant / singularity / infinity locus alone cannot control modular root birth.}
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

For the I0129 moving sparse filter, a successful deformation would therefore need an additional arithmetic invariant — e.g. character sums, Frobenius data, or a uniform statement about splitting types — not merely an algebraic discriminant. I do not manufacture such an invariant here.

**B6 outcome:** the naive deformation route is **refuted as a proof strategy**; this is not a refutation of any source theorem. It prevents wasting later runs on a geometrically incomplete “roots only appear at discriminant zero” argument.

# 5. B19 — make it an algorithm: exact linearization of the parabolic transfer state

The previous report found the exact Möbius recurrence for one row. Here I conjugate it to the simplest possible state evolution and identify exactly what complexity remains.

Fix a row parameter `k` and write
\[
a_r:=\kappa_{k,r},
\qquad 0\le r<k.
\tag{5.1}
\]
Then
\[
q_r:=\frac{a_{r+1}}{a_r}
=-\frac{k-1-r}{r+1}
=1-\frac{k}{r+1}.
\tag{5.2}
\]
Define the projective coordinate
\[
u_r:=\frac{1}{1-q_r}.
\tag{5.3}
\]
By (5.2), this is simply
\[
\boxed{u_r=\frac{r+1}{k}.}
\tag{5.4}
\]
Hence the Möbius step is conjugate to a translation:
\[
\boxed{u_{r+1}=u_r+\frac1k,}
\tag{5.5}
\]
and therefore, for every legal shift `s`,
\[
\boxed{u_{r+s}=u_r+\frac{s}{k}.}
\tag{5.6}
\]
This is an exact identity, not an asymptotic approximation.

The amplitude evolution is also explicit. Iterating (5.2),
\[
\frac{a_{r+s}}{a_r}
=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}q_{r+j}
=(-1)^s
\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\frac{k-1-r-j}{r+1+j}.
\tag{5.7}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{
\frac{a_{r+s}}{a_r}
=(-1)^s
\frac{(k-1-r)^{\underline{s}}}{(r+1)^{\overline{s}}}
}
\tag{5.8}
\]
where the numerator is a falling factorial and the denominator a rising factorial. In factorial notation,
\[
\frac{a_{r+s}}{a_r}
=(-1)^s
\frac{(k-1-r)!}{(k-1-r-s)!}
\frac{r!}{(r+s)!}.
\tag{5.9}
\]

This has an important algorithmic interpretation. Once the base residue `r` is fixed, the state `u_r` is fixed and the entire phase trajectory is deterministic by translation. The amplitude is then determined by one explicit hypergeometric product. There is no hidden state tree and no dynamical branching to eliminate.

For the four-row signed defect the previous sparse-filter identity was
\[
F(x)
=K_m(x)-x^{-c_1}K_{m+1}(x)-x^{-c_2}K_{m+2}(x)+x^{-c_4}K_{m+4}(x),
\tag{5.10}
\]
with `K_k(x)=k(x-1)^{k-1}` and `c_4=X+c_1+c_2`, and
\[
F(x)=(x-1)^{m-1}B(x)
\tag{5.11}
\]
for an at-most-11-term Laurent filter `B`.

Equations (5.4)--(5.9) show that the smallest exact transfer state is essentially `(r,a_r)` — or equivalently `(u_r,a_r)` — and that the phase coordinate is trivial. The actual obstruction is the arithmetic of the **long multiplicative cocycle** in (5.8) inside the four-row sparse combination. Put differently:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the moving-degree difficulty is a hypergeometric fiber/divisibility problem, not state explosion.}

\tag{5.12}
\]

This also explains why the fixed-offset theorem is easy while the macroscopic offset case remains hard: bounded `s` gives a bounded-degree rational function of `r`; `s=\Theta(m)` produces a product with `\Theta(m)` linear factors even though the projective state itself has degree one.
