# 5. B3 — PROVE SOMETHING STRONGER: clean-core divisibility lifts to every fixed jet order

The key observation is that the signed-Pascal coefficient is the value at a simple root of a local reciprocal of the binomial polynomial.

For `k>=2`, define
\[
F_k(x):=\binom{x}{k}
=\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{u=0}^{k-1}(x-u).
\tag{5.1}
\]
For a root `r in {0,...,k-1}`, set
\[
\psi_{k,r}(z)
:=\frac{z}{F_k(r+z)}
=\frac{k!}{\prod_{u\ne r}(r-u+z)}.
\tag{5.2}
\]
This is regular at `z=0`, and
\[
\psi_{k,r}(0)
=\frac{k!}{\prod_{u\ne r}(r-u)}
=(-1)^{k-1-r}k\binom{k-1}{r}
=\kappa_{k,r}.
\tag{5.3}
\]
Thus the preceding interpolant `I_k` is exactly the order-one interpolation of these local reciprocal germs.

## 5.1 Confluent reciprocal interpolants

Fix an integer `s>=1`.

### Definition 5.1

Let `J_{k,s}(x)` be the unique polynomial in `Q[x]`, of degree `<sk`, such that for every `0<=r<k`,
\[
J_{k,s}(r+z)
\equiv
\psi_{k,r}(z)
\pmod{z^s}.
\tag{5.4}
\]
Equivalently, for `0<=j<s`,
\[
\frac{J_{k,s}^{(j)}(r)}{j!}
=[z^j]\psi_{k,r}(z).
\tag{5.5}
\]
Existence and uniqueness are the ordinary confluent Hermite interpolation theorem, or equivalently the Chinese remainder theorem for the pairwise-coprime ideals `(x-r)^s` in `Q[x]`.

For `s=1`, (5.3) gives
\[
J_{k,1}=I_k.
\tag{5.6}
\]

## 5.2 Denominators contain no large primes

The p-adic statement needed below is stronger than mere rational existence.

### Lemma 5.2 — p-integrality of the osculatory interpolant

If `p>k` is prime, then every coefficient of `J_{k,s}` lies in `Z_(p)`. Moreover there is an integer `C_{k,s}>0` such that
\[
C_{k,s}J_{k,s}(x)\in\mathbb Z[x],
\tag{5.7}
\]
all prime divisors of `C_{k,s}` are at most `k-1`, and for fixed `s`,
\[
\log C_{k,s}=O_s(k\log k).
\tag{5.8}
\]
One may for example take a power `((k-1)!)^{c_s}` with `c_s=O(s)`; the crude choice `c_s=3s` is sufficient here.

**Proof.** Define the cardinal polynomial
\[
L_{k,r}(x)
:=\prod_{u\ne r}\frac{x-u}{r-u}.
\tag{5.9}
\]
It equals `1` at `r` and vanishes at all other roots. The denominator
\[
\prod_{u\ne r}(r-u)=(-1)^{k-1-r}r!(k-1-r)!
\]
divides `(k-1)!`.

Put
\[
E_{r,s}(x):=L_{k,r}(x)^s.
\]
At every root `u!=r`, this has a zero of order `s`; at `r`, its local series `E_{r,s}(r+z)` has constant term `1`. Let
\[
P_{r,s}(z)
:=\left[\frac{\psi_{k,r}(z)}{E_{r,s}(r+z)}\right]_{<s}
\tag{5.10}
\]
be the Taylor truncation through degree `s-1`. Then
\[
J_{k,s}(x)
=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}P_{r,s}(x-r)E_{r,s}(x)
\tag{5.11}
\]
has the required jets and degree `<sk`, so it is the unique interpolant.

Every denominator occurring in the first `s` coefficients of (5.10) is a product of a bounded-by-`s` number of factors `r-u`, hence divides a fixed `O(s)` power of `(k-1)!`. Together with the `s` powers of the denominator of `L_{k,r}`, this proves (5.7)-(5.8), for instance with the deliberately loose exponent `3s`.

If `p>k`, every difference `r-u` and `k!` is a p-adic unit. Formulae (5.2), (5.9), and (5.10) therefore lie in `Z_(p)[[z]]` through the required order, so (5.11) has coefficients in `Z_(p)`. QED.

For the four represented rows, let `C_{m,s}` be any common denominator clearing all four `J_{k_h,s}`. The construction above allows
\[
C_{m,s}=((m+3)!)^{3s}.
\tag{5.12}
\]
Every clean prime satisfies `p>4m+8`, hence
\[
p\nmid C_{m,s}.
\tag{5.13}

# 6. B12 reformulated: a retraction identity behind the jet tower

There is a useful algebraic way to see why the construction is natural.

For each fixed `s`, let `T_{k,s+1}(x)` be the unique polynomial of degree `<(s+1)k` such that at every root `r=0,...,k-1`,
\[
T_{k,s+1}(r+z)\equiv r\pmod{z^{s+1}}.
\tag{6.1}
\]
Then `x-T_{k,s+1}(x)` vanishes at every root of `F_k`, hence is divisible by `F_k`. Dividing locally at `r` gives
\[
\frac{x-T_{k,s+1}(x)}{F_k(x)}
=\frac{z+O(z^{s+1})}{F_k(r+z)}
=\psi_{k,r}(z)+O(z^s).
\]
Both sides have degree `<sk` after division. By uniqueness of the Hermite interpolant,
\[
\boxed{
F_k(x)J_{k,s}(x)=x-T_{k,s+1}(x).
}
\tag{6.2}
\]
At an exact represented top index `n_h`, where `F_{k_h}(n_h)=t`,
\[
tJ_{k_h,s}(n_h)=n_h-T_{k_h,s+1}(n_h).
\tag{6.3}
\]
Thus the clean-core tower can equally be viewed as a family of high-order polynomial retractions onto the consecutive root set `{0,...,k-1}`. The bounded defect cancels the linear `n_h` part after summing the four rows, leaving only the retraction discrepancy.

This identity is exact, but it does not by itself create an Archimedean deficit: the degree of `T_{k,s+1}` grows linearly with `s k`, matching the leading `sL` size of `(W^circ)^s`. It does explain why a generic one-level primitive-divisor theorem is unlikely to be the decisive tool.
