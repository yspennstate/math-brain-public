### Proposition 4.1 — exact rough-support saturation

For every left-half representation `t=binom(n,m)`, let

\[
s_m:=\#\{0\le j<m:\ n-j\text{ is divisible by at least one prime }p>m\text{ with }p\mid t\}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
 s_m\ge \frac{L}{\log n}-\pi(m)
\ge
\frac{m}{1+m\log m/L}-\pi(m).
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

The same lower bound holds for

\[
r_{>m}(t):=\#\{p\mid t:p>m\}.
\tag{4.4}
\]

In particular, along any sequence in the unresolved MRSTT edge (3.1) with `m->infinity`,

\[
\boxed{s_m=(1-o(1))m,
\qquad
r_{>m}(t)\ge(1-o(1))m.}
\tag{4.5}
\]

#### Proof

By (2.1), each complete prime-power component `p^{v_p(t)}` is at most `n`. There are at most `pi(m)` primes `p<=m`, so

\[
S_m(t)\le n^{\pi(m)}.
\tag{4.6}
\]

Consequently

\[
W_m(t)\ge t\,n^{-\pi(m)},
\qquad
\log W_m(t)\ge L-\pi(m)\log n.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Now take any prime `p>m` dividing `t`. The denominator `m!` is prime to `p`, while among the `m` consecutive numerator factors

\[
n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)
\]

at most one is divisible by `p`. Therefore there is a unique `j_p in {0,...,m-1}` with `p|(n-j_p)`, and in fact

\[
v_p(n-j_p)=v_p(t).
\tag{4.8}
\]

For each position `j`, let `A_j` be the product of all complete components `p^{v_p(t)}` with `p>m` assigned to that position. Then

\[
A_j\mid n-j,
\qquad
\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}A_j=W_m(t),
\tag{4.9}
\]

and exactly `s_m` of the `A_j` exceed `1`. Hence

\[
W_m(t)=\prod_j A_j\le n^{s_m}.
\tag{4.10}
\]

Combining (4.7) and (4.10) gives the first lower bound in (4.3). The second follows from the upper bound on `log n` in (2.2). Since each occupied position requires at least one distinct prime `p>m`, `r_{>m}(t)>=s_m`, proving (4.4).

Finally, in (3.1), for fixed `epsilon<1/2`,

\frac{m\log m}{L}=o(1),
\qquad
\frac{\pi(m)}m=o(1),

so (4.5) follows. `QED`

\tag{5.2}

## 6. B27 — SWAP THE QUANTIFIERS: fix a prime-power component first and obtain a unique p-adic branch

The previous report fixed the largest prime-power modulus and obtained `n mod p^a < m`. Here the quantifiers can be swapped one level further: fix a prime-power component of the common `t` first, and solve the representation locally in `Z_p`.

### Proposition 6.1 — explicit full-prime-power branch equation

Let

\[
t=\binom nm,
\qquad n\ge2m,
\]

and let `p>m` be a prime divisor of `t`. Put

\[
a=v_p(t),
\qquad
Y=p^a,
\qquad
T=t/Y,
\]

so `p` does not divide `T`. Then there is a unique

\[
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
\]

such that `p|(n-r)`. In fact

\[
n=Yq+r,
\qquad p\nmid q.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Define, in `Z_p[q]`,

\[
F_{m,r,Y}(q)
:=
\frac{q}{m!}
\prod_{\substack{0\le j<m\\j\ne r}}
(Yq+r-j).
\tag{6.2}
\]

Then the representation is equivalent to the exact equation

\[
\boxed{F_{m,r,Y}(q)=T.}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Moreover

\[
\boxed{
q\equiv
(-1)^{m-1-r}
T\,m\binom{m-1}{r}
\pmod Y.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
n\equiv
r+Y(-1)^{m-1-r}T\,m\binom{m-1}{r}
\pmod{Y^2}.}
\tag{6.5}
\]

For each fixed tuple `(p,Y,T,m,r)`, equation (6.3) has a **unique solution in `Z_p`**. Consequently the residue class in (6.4) lifts uniquely modulo every `p^k`; there is one p-adic branch for each candidate `r`, not an uncontrolled tree of carries.

#### Proof

Because `p>m`, the denominator `m!` is prime to `p`, and among `n,n-1,...,n-m+1` at most one term is divisible by `p`. Since `p|t`, such a term exists and determines the unique `r`. Since the denominator is `p`-free and no other numerator factor contains `p`,

\[
v_p(n-r)=v_p\binom nm=a,
\]

which proves (6.1).

Now factor the distinguished numerator term `n-r=Yq` from the binomial coefficient:

\[
T=\frac1Y\binom nm
=
\frac{q}{m!}
\prod_{j\ne r}(Yq+r-j),
\]

which is (6.3).

Reducing (6.2) modulo `Y`, all terms `Yq` vanish and

\[
\prod_{j\ne r}(r-j)
=
(-1)^{m-1-r}r!(m-1-r)!.
\]

Therefore

\[
T\equiv
q\,
\frac{(-1)^{m-1-r}r!(m-1-r)!}{m!}
\pmod Y.
\]

Since `p>m`, the denominator and numerator factorials are units modulo `Y`, so inversion gives

\[
q\equiv
(-1)^{m-1-r}T\,
\frac{m!}{r!(m-1-r)!}
=
(-1)^{m-1-r}T\,m\binom{m-1}{r}
\pmod Y,
\]

proving (6.4), and (6.5) follows from `n=Yq+r`.

Finally reduce `F(q)-T` modulo `p`. Since `Y` is divisible by `p`,

\[
F(q)-T\equiv c_{m,r}q-T\pmod p,
\qquad
c_{m,r}:=
\frac{(-1)^{m-1-r}r!(m-1-r)!}{m!},
\tag{6.6}
\]

and `c_{m,r}` is a p-adic unit. Hence there is exactly one root modulo `p`, and

\[
F'(q)\equiv c_{m,r}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Hensel's lemma gives a unique lift to a root in `Z_p`, equivalently a unique compatible solution modulo every `p^k`. `QED`

### Corollary 6.2 — one shared top-prime datum controls every represented lower index

Let `p=P(t)` and `Y=p^{v_p(t)}`. Sylvester–Schur applied to the largest represented lower index `M` gives `p>M`; hence Proposition 6.1 applies simultaneously to **every** `m in \mathcal M(t)`. The p-adic unit

\[
T=t/Y
\]

is common to all representations. For each `m`, a source solution must therefore choose some `r<m` such that the unique p-adic root `q_{m,r}(T)` is actually a positive ordinary integer and satisfies the Archimedean binomial equation.

Combining with the edge expansion of Matomäki et al., the remaining simultaneous condition can be written schematically as

\[
\boxed{
Yq_{m,r}(T)+r
=
(m!t)^{1/m}+\frac{m-1}{2}
+O(mt^{-1/m}),
}
\tag{6.7}
\]

for many distinct `m`, where `q_{m,r}(T)` is the **unique p-adic branch** defined by (6.3).

**B27 outcome / precise failure.** This removes carry-tree ambiguity, but it does not by itself reduce the number of lower indices. There are `m` possible residue labels `r` for a given `m`, and p-adic uniqueness within a label is not yet a theorem saying that the p-adic root is an ordinary positive integer near the Archimedean inverse curve. Fixed-column monotonicity already gives at most one actual `n` per `m`; the new information is the common p-adic branch structure, not a count across growing `m`.

The missing theorem is therefore an **Archimedean–p-adic incompatibility/reuse bound**: show that for one fixed common `(p,Y,T)` only `O(L^rho)` lower indices, with some fixed `rho<1` (ideally `O(1)`), can have a branch `r<m` whose p-adic root also lands on the real binomial inverse curve (6.7).
