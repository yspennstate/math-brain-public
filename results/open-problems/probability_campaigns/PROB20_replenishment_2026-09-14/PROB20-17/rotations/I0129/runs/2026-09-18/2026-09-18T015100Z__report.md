## 2. B14 — FIND THE GROUP ACTING: the signed Pascal row modulo the largest prime

Define

\[
c_{m,r}:=(-1)^{m-1-r}m{m-1\choose r},\qquad 0\le r<m<p.
\tag{2.1}
\]

Modulo `p`, equation (1.3) gives

\[
qT^{-1}\equiv c_{m,r}\pmod p,
\tag{2.2}
\]

because `p` does not divide `T`.

### 2.1 Exact row structure

The entire row has the generating-polynomial description

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}c_{m,r}x^r
=(-1)^{m-1}m(1-x)^{m-1}
=m(x-1)^{m-1}.
}
\tag{2.3}
\]

Thus the quotient residue samples a coefficient of the derivative

\[
\frac{d}{dx}(x-1)^m=m(x-1)^{m-1}.
\]

For consecutive residues,

\[
\boxed{
\frac{c_{m,r+1}}{c_{m,r}}
=-\frac{m-1-r}{r+1}\pmod p,
}
\tag{2.4}
\]

and all denominators are units because `m<p`. Reflection gives

\[
\boxed{
c_{m,m-1-r}=(-1)^{m-1}c_{m,r}.}
\tag{2.5}
\]

So odd `m` gives exact reflection equality, while even `m` gives reflection by sign. These are genuine symmetries, but they only identify at most a bounded number of positions; they do not control the number of rows.

### 2.2 Near-top rows are large-image, not rigid

The decisive diagnostic comes from localizing `m` relative to `p`. Write

\[
m=p-h,\qquad 1\le h\le p-2.
\]

For `0<=r<=p-h-1`,

\[
{p-h-1\choose r}
=\frac{(p-h-1)(p-h-2)\cdots(p-h-r)}{r!}
\equiv
(-1)^r{h+r\choose r}\pmod p.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Because `p` is odd and `p-1` is even,

\[
(-1)^{p-h-1-r}=(-1)^{h+r},
\]

while `m=p-h\equiv-h (mod p)`. Substituting in (2.1) yields the exact formula

\[
\boxed{
c_{p-h,r}\equiv(-1)^{h+1}h{h+r\choose r}\pmod p.}
\tag{2.7}
\]

The strongest special case is `h=1`, i.e. `m=p-1`:

\[
\boxed{c_{p-1,r}\equiv r+1\pmod p,\qquad 0\le r\le p-2.}
\tag{2.8}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{\{c_{p-1,r}:0\le r\le p-2\}=\mathbb F_p^*.}
\tag{2.9}
\]

So at the completely legal scale `m=p-1`, the mod-`p` part of the quotient congruence (2.2) is **surjective**: every nonzero quotient residue `qT^{-1} mod p` is compatible with some residue position `r`.

More generally, for fixed `h`, the right side of (2.7) is a nonconstant polynomial in `r` of degree `h` over `F_p` (up to a nonzero scalar). Every fiber has size at most `h`; on the allowed domain of size `p-h`, its image therefore has size at least

\[
\boxed{\frac{p-h}{h}.}
\tag{2.10}
\]

This is the opposite of the hoped-for rigidity when `h` is small.

### B14 outcome

The finite-field group view is useful because it kills a tempting route cleanly. A proof cannot simply say that `q/T mod p` has few allowed values coming from a signed Pascal row: at `m=p-1` it has **all** nonzero values, and for `m=p-h` with small `h` it still has a large image. Any successful modular argument must exploit **cross-row coupling, higher prime-power digits, or additional real-size information**, not first-digit image size alone.

## 3. B17 — LOOK FOR YOUR NUMBERS SOMEWHERE ELSE: Mattarei / Lucas / prime-power automaticity

### 3.1 Mattarei periodicity is adjacent but its hypothesis is not generated by Singmaster collisions

Mattarei's 2005 modular-periodicity theorem concerns the complete signed row

\[
s_i=(-1)^i{k\choose i}\pmod p,\qquad 0\le i\le k.
\]

If this complete function of `i` is periodic with a period `h` prime to `p`, and the period is not too large (in particular `2h<=k` suffices), then `k+1` must be a power of `p`.

Our row (2.1) is merely a nonzero scalar multiple of Mattarei's row with `k=m-1`:

\[
c_{m,r}=(-1)^{m-1}m\,(-1)^r{m-1\choose r}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Consequently, if a legal Singmaster row `2<=m<p` had such a complete small period, Mattarei would force

\[
m=p^j
\]

for some `j>=0`. But `1<m<p`, so this is impossible. Thus the complete legal row cannot have the kind of nontrivial short periodicity to which Mattarei applies.

This is a **prior-art consequence**, not a new theorem claim. More importantly, high Singmaster multiplicity supplies only one selected coefficient `c_{m,r}` from each varying row. It does **not** imply periodicity of any entire row. Therefore Mattarei cannot be invoked to bound the number of represented `m` without an additional mechanism that upgrades sparse cross-row coincidences into full-row periodicity. Formula (2.8) shows why such an upgrade is nontrivial: a near-top row can realize every nonzero residue without being periodic in the prohibited sense.

### 3.2 Lucas/Granville/Rowland–Yassawi describe residues; they do not yet count quotient blocks

Lucas-type formulas and their prime-power refinements organize binomial coefficients by base-`p` digits and carries. Rowland–Yassawi give finite-automaton descriptions modulo `p^alpha` for broad diagonal/algebraic sequences, and their binomial-coefficient discussion recovers prime-power congruence structure. This is relevant to (1.3), but in the present source geometry `m<p` already puts us in a particularly simple single-block carry regime. The missing statement is not “compute `c_{m,r} mod p^a`”; it is a **uniform bound on how many varying rows can produce quotient blocks that also satisfy the real binomial equation**.

The automatic/prime-power machinery located in this run does not supply such a cross-row counting theorem. Its state complexity may itself grow with the modulus exponent, while the hard branch below allows both `p` and `a` to vary with `t`.

### B17 outcome

The closest modular literature is diagnostically useful but does not close the current obligation. Mattarei rules out one kind of full-row periodicity, while Lucas/Granville/automatic methods efficiently encode residues. Neither converts the one-sample-per-row condition of Singmaster into a uniform row count. This route therefore **fails at its quantifier transition**, not because the modular calculations are unavailable.

## 4. B30 — ONE PRIME / ONE SCALE: a fixed-power-saving theorem outside an ultraflat window

The previous all-residue theorem (1.1) can be combined with the trivial row count to give a sharper scale localization than `log Y=(log t)^{o(1)}` alone.

Let

\[
L:=\log t
\]

and, if there is at least one nontrivial left-half representation, let

\[
M:=\max\{m:2\le m\le n/2,\ {n\choose m}=t\}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

For fixed `m`, `{n\choose m}` is strictly increasing in `n`, so there is at most one left-half representation for each lower index `m`. Restoring symmetry and the trivial `m=1,n-1` positions therefore gives the crude but uniform estimate

\[
\boxed{N(t)\le 2M+2.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

On the other hand, (1.1) implies for sufficiently large `t`

\[
\boxed{N(t)\le C\left(1+\frac{L}{\log Y}\right)}
\tag{4.3}
\]

with an absolute `C` (for example one may absorb the ceilings and the displayed `+6` into `C`).

### Theorem 4.1 — local power saving from prime-power scale relative to maximal lower index

Fix `delta>0`. On the source-valid subregime

\[
\boxed{\log Y\ge M^\delta,}
\tag{4.4}
\]

one has

\[
\boxed{N(t)=O_\delta\!\left((\log t)^{1/(1+\delta)}\right).}
\tag{4.5}
\]

In fact the implicit constant can be chosen absolute once the coefficient `1` in (4.4) is fixed.

### Proof

By (4.2) and (4.3), assumption (4.4) gives

\[
N(t)\le C'\min\left\{M,\frac{L}{M^\delta}\right\}+C'.
\tag{4.6}
\]

Put

\[
X=L^{1/(1+\delta)}.
\]

If `M<=X`, the first term in the minimum is at most `X`. If `M>X`, then

\[
\frac{L}{M^\delta}
<\frac{L}{X^\delta}
=L^{1-\delta/(1+\delta)}
=L^{1/(1+\delta)}=X.
\]

Thus (4.6) gives (4.5). QED.

The same conclusion holds if one assumes only

\[
\log P(t)\ge M^\delta,
\]

because `Y=P(t)^{v_{P(t)}(t)}` implies `log Y>=log P(t)`.

### Corollary 4.2 — any exponent-one extremizing sequence is doubly flat in the natural row scale

Suppose `t_j -> infinity` is a sequence for which

\[
\frac{\log N(t_j)}{\log\log t_j}\longrightarrow1.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Let `M_j` be the corresponding maximal left-half lower index and `Y_j=P(t_j)^{v_{P(t_j)}(t_j)}`. Then

\[
\boxed{\log Y_j=M_j^{o(1)}.}
\tag{4.8}
\]

### Proof

If (4.8) failed, there would be a fixed `delta>0` and an infinite subsequence on which `log Y_j>=M_j^delta`. Theorem 4.1 would give

\[
\log N(t_j)\le\left(\frac1{1+\delta}+o(1)\right)\log\log t_j,
\]

contradicting (4.7). QED.

Since `p=P(t)>M` for every nontrivial representation in this setup, (4.8) says that an exponent-one extremizing sequence must live in the narrow arithmetic window

\[
\boxed{
M<P(t)\le Y=\exp(M^{o(1)}),
\qquad
v_{P(t)}(t)=M^{o(1)}.
}
\tag{4.9}
\]

The last assertion follows from `a log p=log Y=M^{o(1)}` and `log p>=log M`: in particular `a` is also subpower in `M`.

This is materially sharper than the preceding obligation `log Y=(log t)^{o(1)}`: the flatness is now measured against the **largest represented lower index itself**, which is the actual combinatorial degree of the edge equations.

### B30 outcome and gluing failure

Every regime with `log Y>=M^delta` for one fixed positive `delta` has a genuine fixed logarithmic power saving. The only possible exponent-one extremizing regime is the **ultraflat window** (4.9).

I looked for a known theorem forcing `log P(t)` or `log Y` to dominate a fixed positive power of `M` for every binomial representation. The classical prime-factorization results do not provide that uniform statement when `M` itself grows: the 1978 Ecklund–Eggleton–Erdős–Selfridge machinery gives strong information on the mass in primes above `m`, while the Mahler-type statements quoted there concern fixed `m`/nonuniform thresholds. Those facts are compatible with the common rough mass being spread over many individually small prime-power components. Therefore the localization does **not** glue to an unconditional `rho_SM<1` theorem.

Notice also that the modular obstruction from B14 sits exactly inside the surviving window: `m=p-1` has `p` only linear in `m`, hence `log p=m^{o(1)}`, and its signed coefficient row is surjective modulo `p`. Thus the two diagnostics agree rather than conflict: the scale where the analytic bound degenerates is also a scale where first-digit finite-field rigidity can vanish completely.
