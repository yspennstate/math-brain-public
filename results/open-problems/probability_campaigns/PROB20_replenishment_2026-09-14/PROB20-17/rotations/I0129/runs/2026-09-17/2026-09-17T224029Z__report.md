## 3. B1 — CHANGE THE CATEGORY: consecutive blocks as a high-rank S-unit/repartition problem

### 3.1 The category shift

The last run showed why pure prime-mass counting is too weak: the same `M`-rough core of the common value `t` can be reused in every represented row. The more informative viewpoint is to retain **where** each rough prime power sits inside each consecutive numerator block.

For a left-half representation

\[
{n\choose m}=t,
\]

write

\[
(n)_m:=n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)=m!t.
\]

Fix the largest represented lower index `M` and a prime `p>M` dividing `t`. Since `p>m` for every represented `m\le M`, the denominator `m!` contains no factor `p`, and among any `m` consecutive integers at most one is divisible by `p`. Thus the complete `p`-power contribution carried by the common value `t` is assigned to one position of every represented numerator block. Across all `p>M`, each row therefore induces a repartition of the same fixed rough prime-power atoms among consecutive positions.

After one strips those `>M` atoms, the residual factors are supported only on primes `\le M`. Schematically a represented row has

\[
n-j=U_jV_j,\qquad 0\le j<m,
\]

where the `U_j` are pairwise coprime products of the fixed rough prime-power atoms (up to the already controlled overlap conventions) and the `V_j` are `M`-smooth. Consecutive positions satisfy

\[
U_jV_j-U_{j+1}V_{j+1}=1.
\tag{3.1}
\]

So the exceptional branch can be viewed not merely as “a smooth integer” but as a **system of coupled unit-type equations plus a partition of a fixed rough core across a consecutive block**.

This is a diagnostic reformulation of already established arithmetic structure, not a new source theorem.

### 3.2 Why classical S-unit machinery is close but not enough

The nearest mature theory controls equations such as

\[
a_1x_1+\cdots+a_kx_k=1
\]

when the `x_i` range over a fixed finitely generated multiplicative group. Evertse, van der Poorten–Schlickewei, Schlickewei, and later Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt give finiteness and quantitative bounds via the Subspace Theorem; Schlickewei (1990) gave an explicit upper bound for S-unit equations, and later bounds can be expressed in terms of the multiplicative rank.

That theory reveals exactly what is missing here:

- the relevant small-prime set grows at least up to `M`;
- the rough-prime support of `t` itself grows in the high-multiplicity branch;
- the number of coupled consecutive equations grows with `m`;
- the coefficients change with `m`, `t`, and the rough-factor partition;
- quantitative bounds from standard S-unit/Subspace-Theorem technology are exponential or worse in the cardinality/rank parameters that are themselves growing.

Thus a black-box S-unit theorem would replace the original multiplicity question by a bound far too large to certify any fixed `rho_SM<1`. The useful lesson is not “apply the Subspace Theorem,” but rather:

> a successful import must exploit the **consecutive-block geometry and common rough-core repartition** strongly enough to avoid paying exponentially in the full growing multiplicative rank.

That feature is absent from the off-the-shelf fixed-rank theory.

**B1 outcome:** useful category change, no metric movement. It identifies the desired missing theorem as a *structured high-rank unit-equation bound*, rather than an arbitrary smooth-number estimate.

## 5. B20 — FIND WHY THE ALGORITHM FAILS

The previous Hensel reduction suggests an apparently straightforward algorithm:

1. choose the common largest prime `p=P(t)` and `Y=p^{v_p(t)}`;
2. for every candidate `m` and every residue `r<m`, lift the unique p-adic root of (1.1);
3. compare that branch to the real candidate `((m!t)^{1/m}+(m-1)/2-r)/Y`;
4. reject all but the true ordinary-integer roots.

For each **fixed finite input** this is a legitimate decision procedure in principle. It does not, however, prove a uniform multiplicity bound. The exact failure has three layers.

### 5.1 Hensel uniqueness is local, not Archimedean

A p-adic branch is unique in `Z_p`, but a p-adic neighborhood has no monotone relation to the ordinary real size of its elements. The source problem requires counting roots that lie in the diagonal copy of `Z_{>0}` and at the same time lie in an extremely narrow real window. Pure p-adic uniqueness says nothing quantitative about that mixed intersection.

### 5.2 Prime-power flatness makes residue determination asymptotically expensive

In the only branch that can support high multiplicity, previous work forces

\[
\log Y=o(\log t).
\]

To identify an ordinary integer `q` uniquely from a congruence modulo `p^k`, one needs a modulus larger than the relevant real search interval. But the natural real scale is roughly

\[
q\asymp { (m!t)^{1/m}\over Y}.
\]

When `Y=t^{o(1)}`, the number of p-adic digits required before `p^k` exceeds that scale can itself grow without bound. Computing more Hensel digits is therefore an exact finite calculation, not a uniform asymptotic argument. It moves the work into an ever-deeper congruence rather than bounding how many `m` can survive.

### 5.3 Global Diophantine bounds pay for the parameters that are growing

One can reinterpret the integer-root condition through Thue/S-unit/Subspace-Theorem language, but the polynomial degree is `m`, the multiplicative rank grows, and the relevant coefficients depend on `m`, `t`, and the rough-core partition. Existing global-height tools therefore introduce constants whose dependence on the very parameters we need to count is too large to yield a fixed power saving in `log t`.

**B20 outcome:** the branch enumerator is valuable as an exact finite checker for a future candidate, but it does not itself produce the uniform theorem. The failure is now precise: it lacks a *rank-efficient Archimedean–p-adic transversality/height estimate*.

## 8. Nearest solved literature analogue and what it has that I0129 lacks

The nearest mature analogue is the **fixed-rank S-unit equation**.

- H. P. Schlickewei, “An explicit upper bound for the number of solutions of the S-unit equation,” *J. reine angew. Math.* 406 (1990), 109–120, DOI `10.1515/crll.1990.406.109`, gives explicit quantitative solution bounds using the p-adic Subspace Theorem.
- The Evertse / van der Poorten–Schlickewei / Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt line gives finiteness and quantitative bounds for nondegenerate unit equations in fixed finitely generated multiplicative groups; later quantitative Subspace-Theorem work improves constants and structural information.

What the solved analogue has:

1. fixed dimension of the additive equation;
2. a multiplicative group with controlled rank/cardinality of `S`;
3. coefficients that are fixed while solutions vary;
4. quantitative bounds allowed to depend exponentially (or worse) on rank.

What I0129 lacks:

1. `m` itself grows;
2. the number of coupled consecutive equations grows;
3. the small-prime set and rough-prime support grow;
4. the coefficient data changes with `m` and `t`;
5. the required final estimate cannot afford generic exponential dependence on this growing rank.

A secondary analogue is the fixed-`(m,m')` integral-point treatment of binomial collisions: fixing the degrees produces a fixed algebraic curve, where Siegel/Thue-type machinery can force finiteness. Again, the missing feature in Singmaster is **uniformity as the degrees grow**.

The literature therefore supplies a technique family, but not the source-uniform parameter control the conjecture needs.
