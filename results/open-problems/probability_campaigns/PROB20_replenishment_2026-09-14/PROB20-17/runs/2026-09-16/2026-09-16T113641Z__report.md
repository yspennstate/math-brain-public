# 3. A stronger weighted Hamiltonian-path bound on six vertices

Let `x_{ij}=x_{ji} >= 0`, `x_{ii}=0`, on six labeled vertices. Put

\[
W:=\sum_{1\le i<j\le 6}x_{ij},
\qquad
d_v:=\sum_{u\ne v}x_{vu}.
\]

Let

\[
D_6(x):=\sum_{\sigma\in S_6}\prod_{r=1}^{5}x_{\sigma(r),\sigma(r+1)}
\]

be the directed Hamiltonian-path polynomial.

From (2.1),

\[
D_6(x)
\le
\sum_{v=1}^6 d_v\left(\frac{W-d_v}{4}\right)^4.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Assume `W>0` and normalize

\[
y_v:=\frac{d_v}{W}.
\]

Then

\[
0\le y_v\le1,
\qquad
\sum_{v=1}^6 y_v=2,
\]

and

\[
D_6(x)
\le
\frac{W^5}{4^4}
\sum_{v=1}^6 y_v(1-y_v)^4.
\tag{3.2}
\]

The scalar optimization can be solved exactly.

## Lemma 3.1

If `0 <= y_i <= 1` and `sum_i y_i = 2`, then

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i=1}^6 y_i(1-y_i)^4\le \frac{256}{625}.
}
\tag{3.3}
\]

Equality occurs at a permutation of

\[
(1,1/5,1/5,1/5,1/5,1/5).
\]

### Proof

Let

\[
f(y)=y(1-y)^4.
\]

Then

\[
f'(y)=(1-y)^3(1-5y),
\qquad
f''(y)=4(1-y)^2(5y-2).
\tag{3.4}
\]

A maximizer exists by compactness.

A maximizer cannot have a zero coordinate. Indeed, if `y_i=0` and `y_j>0`, then the feasible perturbation `y_i -> epsilon`, `y_j -> y_j-epsilon` has first variation

\[
f'(0)-f'(y_j)=1-f'(y_j)>0,
\]

because `f'(y)<1` for every `y>0`. Hence a zero coordinate cannot occur at a maximum.

If a maximizer has a coordinate equal to one, say `y_1=1`, then the other five sum to one. A feasible transfer from `y_1` to any `y_j<1` has first variation `f'(y_j)-f'(1)=f'(y_j)`. Maximality therefore forces `f'(y_j)<=0`, hence `y_j>=1/5` for all `j>=2`. Since those five coordinates sum to one, all equal `1/5`, and

\[
\sum_i f(y_i)
=5\cdot\frac15\left(\frac45\right)^4
=\frac{256}{625}.
\tag{3.5}
\]

It remains to consider an interior maximizer. The second-order condition along every tangent direction `e_i-e_j` gives

\[
f''(y_i)+f''(y_j)\le0.
\tag{3.6}
\]

Since `f''(y)>0` exactly for `y>2/5`, at most one coordinate can exceed `2/5`.

If none exceeds `2/5`, then all coordinates lie in the interval on which `f` is concave. Jensen gives

\[
\sum_i f(y_i)
\le
6f(1/3)
=\frac{32}{81}
<\frac{256}{625}.
\tag{3.7}
\]

If exactly one coordinate is `t>2/5`, then the remaining five lie below `2/5`. At an interior constrained extremum their derivatives are equal, and `f'` is strictly decreasing on `[0,2/5]`, so those five coordinates are equal to

\[
s=\frac{2-t}{5}.
\]

Thus

\[
\sum_i f(y_i)
=f(t)+5f\left(\frac{2-t}{5}\right).
\]

A direct expansion gives

\[
\frac{256}{625}
-
\left[
f(t)+5f\left(\frac{2-t}{5}\right)
\right]
=
-\frac{2(t-1)^2}{625}
\left(312t^3-631t^2+286t-47\right).
\tag{3.8}
\]

On `[2/5,1]`, the cubic in parentheses is strictly negative. Its derivative is

\[
936t^2-1262t+286,
\]

whose two real roots are approximately `0.28825` and `1.06004`; hence the cubic is decreasing throughout `[2/5,1]`, and at `t=2/5` its value is `-1699/125<0`. Therefore the right-hand side of (3.8) is nonnegative. This proves (3.3). `square`

Substituting Lemma 3.1 into (3.2) gives the first new Hamiltonian theorem.

## Theorem 3.2 — global weighted K6 bound

For arbitrary nonnegative symmetric edge weights on six vertices,

\[
\boxed{
D_6(x)\le \frac{W^5}{625}.
}
\tag{3.9}
\]

The previous all-`n` weighted Moran–Snir–Manber estimate specialized to `n=6` gave

\[
D_6\le 6(W/5)^5=\frac{6W^5}{3125},
\]

so (3.9) improves its coefficient by the factor `5/6`.

# 4. The cycle-conjectured coefficient in a dense capped K6 regime

For the rank-one Gram application the edge weights additionally satisfy `0<=x_{ij}<=1`. This cap improves (3.9) when the total edge mass is moderately large.

Let `c_0` denote the unique real root in `(0,1)` of

\[
27c^3-90c^2+99c-32=0.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Numerically,

\[
c_0=0.561801262574415\ldots .
\]

The cubic in (4.1) is strictly increasing on `[0,1]`, because its derivative

\[
81c^2-180c+99=9(9c-9)(c-11/9)
\]

is positive for `0<=c<1`; the cubic changes sign between zero and one, so the root is unique.

The tangent line to `f(y)=y(1-y)^4` at `y=1/3` is

\[
L(y)=\frac{32}{243}-\frac{16}{81}y.
\]

An exact factorization gives

\[
L(y)-f(y)
=
-\frac{(3y-1)^2}{243}
\left(27y^3-90y^2+99y-32\right).
\tag{4.2}
\]

Therefore

\[
f(y)\le L(y)
\quad\text{for }0\le y\le c_0.
\tag{4.3}
\]

If `x_{ij}<=1`, then `d_v<=5`. Hence

\[
y_v=\frac{d_v}{W}\le\frac5W.
\]

Thus, whenever

\[
W\ge W_0:=\frac5{c_0}=8.89994439864347\ldots,
\tag{4.4}
\]

all six normalized degrees lie in `[0,c_0]`. Summing (4.3) and using `sum y_v=2`,

\[
\sum_vf(y_v)
\le
6\frac{32}{243}-\frac{16}{81}\cdot2
=\frac{32}{81}.
\tag{4.5}
\]

Equation (3.2) now yields:

## Theorem 4.1 — dense capped weighted K6 bound

If `0<=x_{ij}<=1` and `W>=W_0`, then

\[
\boxed{
D_6(x)\le\frac{W^5}{648}.
}
\tag{4.6}
\]

The coefficient `1/648` is exactly the constant predicted by the stronger cycle-extremal candidate

\[
P_H(x)\stackrel{?}{\le}\frac{W^5}{6^4}
\]

for the undirected Hamiltonian-path polynomial, since `D_6=2P_H`. Theorem 4.1 proves that coefficient only in the dense capped regime (4.4); it is **not** a proof of the full six-vertex extremal conjecture.

# 5. Consequence for six equal-scale rank-one PSD matrices: low/medium overlap

Let

\[
A_i=a\,u_i u_i^*,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,6,
\qquad \|u_i\|=1,
\]

with a common scale `a>=0`. By homogeneity take `a=1`.

Define

\[
g_{ij}:=|\langle u_i,u_j\rangle|,
\qquad g_{ii}=1,
\]

and the mean off-diagonal overlap

\[
\bar g:=\frac1{15}\sum_{i<j}g_{ij}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

For rank-one projectors,

\[
\|A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_6}\|
=
\prod_{r=1}^{5}g_{i_r i_{r+1}}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Hence the all-distinct owner average is

\[
L_6=\frac{D_6(G)}{6!}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Here the edge mass is

\[
W=15\bar g.
\]

Theorem 3.2 gives

\[
\boxed{
L_6\le\frac{27}{16}\bar g^5.
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

The exact rank-one transfer identity and Blakley–Roy give

\[
R_6
\ge
\mu^5,
\qquad
\mu:=\frac{1+5\bar g}{6}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Consequently (5.4) closes the owner inequality whenever

\[
\bar g
\le
\gamma_0
:=
\frac1{6(27/16)^{1/5}-5}
=
0.601710384113611\ldots .
\tag{5.6}
\]

The dense capped theorem starts when

\[
\bar g\ge\frac{W_0}{15}
=
\frac1{3c_0}
=
0.593329626576232\ldots .
\tag{5.7}
\]

In that range,

\[
\boxed{
L_6\le\frac{625}{384}\bar g^5.
}
\tag{5.8}
\]

Comparing (5.8) to (5.5), the owner inequality follows whenever

\[
\bar g
\le
\gamma_1
:=
\frac1{6(625/384)^{1/5}-5}
=
0.619597287209554\ldots .
\tag{5.9}
\]

Since the dense theorem begins at `0.593329... < gamma_0`, the two low/medium-overlap certificates overlap. Therefore:

## Theorem 5.1

For six equal-scale rank-one PSD matrices in arbitrary real or complex dimension,

\[
\boxed{
\bar g\le0.619597287209554\ldots
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
L_6\le R_6.
}
\tag{5.10}
\]

This strictly improves the preceding all-`n` path certificate, whose `n=6` threshold was `0.5237425...`.

# 6. A new all-n second-order deficit upper bound

There is a complementary estimate near the all-ones kernel that works for every `n`.

Let `g_{ij}=g_{ji}` satisfy `0<=g_{ij}<=1`, `g_{ii}=1`, and set

\[
h_{ij}:=1-g_{ij},
\qquad
T:=\sum_{i<j}h_{ij},
\qquad
r_v:=\sum_{u\ne v}h_{vu}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Let `H_n(G)` be the mean weight of a uniformly random directed Hamiltonian path:

\[
H_n(G)
=
\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}g_{\sigma(k),\sigma(k+1)}.
\]

For a fixed path, write the elementary symmetric sums of its `n-1` deficit variables as `s_1,s_2,...`. The even Bonferroni truncation gives

\[
\prod_{e\in P}(1-h_e)
\le
1-s_1+s_2.
\tag{6.2}
\]

A fixed edge belongs to a uniform Hamiltonian path with probability `2/n`, so

\[
\mathbb E s_1=\frac{2T}{n}.
\tag{6.3}
\]

For two fixed edges, the probability that both occur is

\[
\frac{2}{n(n-1)}
\]

if they share a vertex, and

\[
\frac{4}{n(n-1)}
\]

if they are disjoint. Let `A` be the sum of products `h_e h_f` over adjacent edge pairs and `D` the corresponding sum over disjoint edge pairs. Then

\[
\mathbb E s_2
=
\frac{2A+4D}{n(n-1)}.
\tag{6.4}
\]

If `Q=sum_e h_e^2`, then

\[
\sum_v r_v^2=2Q+2A,
\qquad
T^2=Q+2A+2D.
\]

Therefore

\[
2A+4D=2T^2-\sum_vr_v^2.
\]

Substitution into (6.2) proves:

## Theorem 6.1 — degree-sensitive deficit bound

For every `n>=3`,

\[
\boxed{
H_n(G)
\le
1-\frac{2T}{n}
+
\frac{2T^2-\sum_v r_v^2}{n(n-1)}.
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

By Cauchy–Schwarz, `sum_v r_v = 2T` implies

\[
\sum_vr_v^2\ge\frac{4T^2}{n},
\]

and hence the scale-free corollary

\[
\boxed{
H_n(G)
\le
1-\frac{2T}{n}
+
\frac{2(n-2)}{n^2(n-1)}T^2.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

The `n=5` high-overlap estimate from the preceding checkpoint is exactly the `n=5` specialization of (6.6). The present derivation shows that the mechanism is not special to five vertices.

# 7. High-overlap n=6 closure and a degree-variance certificate

For six equal-scale rank-one PSD matrices,

\[
T=15(1-\bar g),
\qquad
\mu=1-\frac{T}{18},
\]

and Blakley–Roy gives

\[
R_6\ge\left(1-\frac{T}{18}\right)^5.
\tag{7.1}
\]

The scale-free deficit bound (6.6) is

\[
L_6
\le
1-\frac{T}{3}+\frac{2T^2}{45}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

The exact difference factors as

\[
\left(1-\frac{T}{18}\right)^5
-
\left(1-\frac{T}{3}+\frac{2T^2}{45}\right)
=
-\frac{T}{9447840}P(T),
\tag{7.3}
\]

where

\[
P(T)=5T^4-450T^3+16200T^2+128304T-524880.
\tag{7.4}
\]

On `[0,4]`,

\[
P'(T)=20T^3-1350T^2+32400T+128304>0,
\]

because the crude lower bound `P'(T) >= -1350*16+128304 > 0` already suffices. Also

\[
P(3)=-5913<0,
\qquad
P(4)=220016>0.
\]

Thus there is a unique root `T_* in (3,4)`, numerically

\[
T_*=3.02760033897812\ldots .
\tag{7.5}
\]

Equation (7.3) is nonnegative for `0<=T<=T_*`. Hence:

## Theorem 7.1

For six equal-scale rank-one PSD matrices,

\[
\boxed{
\bar g
\ge
1-\frac{T_*}{15}
=
0.798159977401459\ldots
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
L_6\le R_6.
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

The degree-sensitive form is stronger on heterogeneous instances. Define the deficit-degree variance

\[
V:=\sum_{v=1}^6\left(r_v-\frac{T}{3}\right)^2.
\]

Since

\[
\sum_vr_v^2=\frac{2T^2}{3}+V,
\]

Theorem 6.1 gives

\[
L_6
\le
1-\frac{T}{3}+\frac{2T^2}{45}-\frac{V}{30}.
\tag{7.7}
\]

Therefore any six-vector equal-scale rank-one instance is certified whenever

\[
\boxed{
V
\ge
30\left[
1-\frac{T}{3}+\frac{2T^2}{45}
-
\left(1-\frac{T}{18}\right)^5
\right].
}
\tag{7.8}
\]

The right-hand side is nonpositive in the automatic high-overlap region (7.6); in the intermediate gap it gives an explicit heterogeneity certificate.

A second degree-sensitive certificate comes directly from the deletion bound. Since the overlap-degree is `d_v=5-r_v` and `W=15-T`,

\[
\boxed{
L_6
\le
\frac1{720}
\sum_{v=1}^6
(5-r_v)
\left(\frac{10-T+r_v}{4}\right)^4.
}
\tag{7.9}
\]

Comparing the right-hand side of (7.9) with `(1-T/18)^5` gives another fully rigorous degree-only closure test inside the unresolved mean-overlap gap.

# 11. Next attack

The highest-value next step is the unresolved middle-overlap window for six equal-scale rank-one matrices. There are two concrete routes.

First, strengthen the deficit expansion beyond second order. For a five-edge path,

\[
\prod_{e\in P}(1-h_e)
=1-s_1+s_2-s_3+s_4-s_5.
\]

The present theorem discards `-s_3+s_4-s_5`. In the difficult near-regular deficit regime, that discarded contribution should be quantitatively negative; a lower bound for the expected third elementary symmetric term in terms of `T` and the deficit-degree variance could plausibly bridge much of the remaining interval.

Second, attack the six-vertex weighted extremal inequality

\[
D_6(x)\le W^5/648
\]

directly. This run shows the desired coefficient already follows from the rooted deletion relaxation once the edge cap forces all normalized degrees below `c_0`. The only remaining obstruction in that method is a sparse/moderate-mass degree configuration. A sharper rooted theorem depending simultaneously on the root degree and residual degree sequence, rather than only residual total edge mass, is the natural refinement.

For arbitrary scales, the exact rank-one target remains the weighted walk-versus-Hamiltonian-path inequality from earlier checkpoints. No arbitrary-scale `n=6` closure is claimed here.
