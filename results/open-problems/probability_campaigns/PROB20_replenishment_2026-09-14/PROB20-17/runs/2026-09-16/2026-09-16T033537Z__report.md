# 3. A pure-power closure principle

For the remainder of this section let
\[
a_i:=\|A_i\|.
\]
If some `a_i=0`, then `A_i=0`, every distinct word on the left vanishes, and the owner inequality is trivial. Hence assume `a_i>0`.

Define the normalized pair interaction
\[
\gamma_{ij}:=\frac{\|A_iA_j\|}{a_i a_j}\in[0,1]
\qquad(i\ne j).
\tag{3.1}
\]
The symmetry `gamma_ij=gamma_ji` follows from `||A_iA_j||=||A_jA_i||` for PSD matrices, because the two products are adjoints of one another.

Let
\[
m:=\lfloor n/2\rfloor.
\]
For any permutation `sigma`, submultiplicativity lets us group adjacent factors into disjoint pairs:
\[
\|A_{\sigma(1)}\cdots A_{\sigma(n)}\|
\le
\left(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i\right)
\prod_{r=1}^{m}
\gamma_{\sigma(2r-1),\sigma(2r)}.
\tag{3.2}
\]
When `n` is odd the final unpaired factor contributes only its norm, already present in `prod_i a_i`.

On the with-replacement side, retain only the `n` pure words
\[
A_1^n,\ldots,A_n^n.
\]
Since each `A_i` is PSD,
\[
\|A_i^n\|=a_i^n.
\]
Therefore
\[
\frac1{n^n}\sum_{k_1,\dots,k_n}\|A_{k_1}\cdots A_{k_n}\|
\ge
\frac1{n^n}\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^n.
\tag{3.3}
\]
Define the scale parameter
\[
\boxed{
\Theta(a_1,\ldots,a_n)
:=
\frac{\sum_i a_i^n}{n^n\prod_i a_i}.
}
\tag{3.4}
\]
Power AM–GM gives
\[
\Theta\ge n^{1-n}.
\tag{3.5}
\]

# 4. Exact maximum-matching certificate for arbitrary PSD matrices

Let `M_n` be the set of maximum-cardinality matchings of the complete graph on `[n]`, so every `M in M_n` contains exactly `m=floor(n/2)` edges. Define
\[
\mathcal M_n(\gamma)
:=
\frac1{|M_n|}
\sum_{M\in M_n}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in M}\gamma_{ij}.
\tag{4.1}
\]
For even `n=2m`, these are perfect matchings; for odd `n=2m+1`, they are near-perfect matchings with one unmatched vertex.

## Theorem 4.1 — matching certificate
For arbitrary PSD `A_1,...,A_n`, the owner inequality holds whenever
\[
\boxed{\mathcal M_n(\gamma)\le\Theta(a_1,\ldots,a_n).}
\tag{4.2}
\]

### Proof
Average (3.2) over all `n!` permutations. The adjacent pairing
\[
(\sigma(1),\sigma(2)),\ldots,(\sigma(2m-1),\sigma(2m))
\]
induces a maximum matching.

For even `n=2m`, every perfect matching occurs exactly `2^m m!` times: orient each of its `m` edges and order the `m` oriented pairs. Since
\[
(2m)!=(2^m m!)\,|M_{2m}|,
\]
the permutation average of the product in (3.2) is exactly `mathcal M_n(gamma)`.

For odd `n=2m+1`, choose the unmatched last vertex and then orient/order the remaining `m` pairs. Again each near-perfect matching occurs exactly `2^m m!` times, so the permutation average is again exactly `mathcal M_n(gamma)`.

Hence
\[
\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma}\|A_{\sigma(1)}\cdots A_{\sigma(n)}\|
\le
\left(\prod_i a_i\right)\mathcal M_n(\gamma).
\tag{4.3}
\]
If (4.2) holds, the right side of (4.3) is at most
\[
\left(\prod_i a_i\right)\Theta
=
\frac1{n^n}\sum_i a_i^n,
\]
which is at most the full with-replacement average by (3.3). QED.

This is a full arbitrary-dimensional owner theorem on an explicit region of pair-interaction space, not merely a bound on one occupancy layer.

# 5. A simple all-`n` weak-interaction theorem

Let
\[
\gamma_*:=\max_{i\ne j}\gamma_{ij}.
\]
Then every matching product in (4.1) is at most `gamma_*^m`, so Theorem 4.1 gives:

## Corollary 5.1 — scale-sensitive coherence closure
The full owner inequality holds whenever
\[
\boxed{
\gamma_*
\le
\Theta(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^{1/\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Using (3.5), a scale-free sufficient condition is
\[
\boxed{
\gamma_*
\le
n^{-(n-1)/\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\gamma_*\le n^{-2}
\quad\text{for odd }n,
\tag{5.3}
\]
and
\[
\gamma_*\le n^{-2+2/n}
\quad\text{for even }n.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Concrete first unresolved diagonal values are
\[
n=4:\qquad \gamma_*\le4^{-3/2}=\frac18,
\tag{5.5}
\]
\[
n=5:\qquad \gamma_*\le\frac1{25},
\tag{5.6}
\]
\[
n=6:\qquad \gamma_*\le6^{-5/3}.
\tag{5.7}
\]

Thus the conjecture is now proved for every `n` whenever all normalized pair products are sufficiently weak. Unequal matrix norms can enlarge the allowable region through the exact scale-sensitive threshold (5.1), because `Theta` can be much larger than its AM–GM floor.

# 6. Exact arbitrary-`n` rank-one transfer reduction

Now specialize to rank-one PSD matrices
\[
A_i=a_i u_i u_i^*,
\qquad a_i\ge0,
\qquad \|u_i\|=1.
\]
Set the absolute Gram kernel
\[
g_{ij}:=|\langle u_i,u_j\rangle|,
\qquad g_{ii}=1,
\tag{6.1}
\]
and let `G=(g_ij)`. For every word `(i_1,...,i_n)`,
\[
\boxed{
\|A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_n}\|
=
\left(\prod_{r=1}^n a_{i_r}\right)
\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}g_{i_r i_{r+1}}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Define
\[
q_i:=\sqrt{a_i},
\qquad
M:=\operatorname{diag}(q)\,G\,\operatorname{diag}(q).
\tag{6.3}
\]
Then direct expansion gives the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i_1,\dots,i_n=1}^n
\|A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_n}\|
=q^T M^{n-1}q.
}
\tag{6.4}
\]

On the distinct side, every permutation uses every scale once. If
\[
H_n(G):=
\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}g_{\sigma(r),\sigma(r+1)},
\tag{6.5}
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
\frac1{n!}\sum_\sigma
\|A_{\sigma(1)}\cdots A_{\sigma(n)}\|
=
\left(\prod_i a_i\right)H_n(G).
}
\tag{6.6}
\]
Thus the full arbitrary-`n` rank-one owner problem is exactly a weighted walk-versus-Hamiltonian-path inequality for the absolute Gram kernel.

This reduction preserves arbitrary positive scales and does not assume commuting projectors or equal overlaps.

# 7. Rank-one constant-word certificate

Let
\[
g_*:=\max_{i\ne j}g_{ij}.
\]
From (6.6),
\[
H_n(G)\le g_*^{n-1}.
\]
The pure-word lower bound (3.3) is exact enough to close the comparison.

## Corollary 7.1
For arbitrary scaled rank-one PSD matrices, the owner inequality holds whenever
\[
\boxed{
 g_*
\le
\Theta(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^{1/(n-1)}.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]
In particular, since `Theta>=n^{1-n}`,
\[
\boxed{g_*\le1/n}
\tag{7.2}
\]
is a universal scale-free sufficient condition.

This rank-one threshold is much stronger than applying the general PSD theorem through pair norms: for rank one, the exact word formula carries the overlap `g_ij` itself rather than its square-root surrogate from a generic product bound.

# 8. Full arbitrary-`n` theorem for scaled equiangular rank-one families

A stronger conclusion is possible when all distinct absolute overlaps are equal:
\[
|\langle u_i,u_j\rangle|=c
\qquad(i\ne j),
\qquad 0\le c\le1.
\tag{8.1}
\]
No restriction is placed on the positive scales `a_i`.

Then every Hamiltonian path has weight `c^{n-1}`, so the distinct average is exactly
\[
L=\left(\prod_i a_i\right)c^{n-1}.
\tag{8.2}
\]
Moreover
\[
G=(1-c)I+c\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T\succeq0,
\tag{8.3}
\]
so `M` in (6.3) is PSD.

Let
\[
S:=\sum_i a_i,
\qquad
S_2:=\sum_i a_i^2.
\]
For a PSD matrix `M`, spectral Jensen applied to the convex function `x -> x^{n-1}` gives
\[
q^T M^{n-1}q
\ge
S\left(\frac{q^TMq}{S}\right)^{n-1}.
\tag{8.4}
\]
Here
\[
q^TMq
=a^TGa
=(1-c)S_2+cS^2.
\tag{8.5}
\]
Since `S_2>=S^2/n`,
\[
q^TMq
\ge
S^2\frac{1+(n-1)c}{n}.
\tag{8.6}
\]
Combining (6.4), (8.4), and (8.6),
\[
\frac1{n^n}q^TM^{n-1}q
\ge
\frac{S^n}{n^n}
\left(\frac{1+(n-1)c}{n}\right)^{n-1}.
\tag{8.7}
\]
AM–GM gives `S^n/n^n>=prod_i a_i`, hence
\[
\boxed{
R
\ge
\left(\prod_i a_i\right)
\left(\frac{1+(n-1)c}{n}\right)^{n-1}.
}
\tag{8.8}
\]
But
\[
\frac{1+(n-1)c}{n}\ge c
\qquad(0\le c\le1),
\]
so (8.8) and (8.2) prove:

## Theorem 8.1 — scaled equiangular rank-one case
For every `n`, every dimension in which such vectors exist, every `0<=c<=1`, and arbitrary nonnegative scales `a_i`, the full owner norm-inside inequality holds for
\[
A_i=a_i u_i u_i^*
\]
whenever all distinct absolute overlaps equal `c`.

This includes the strongly interacting regime `c>1/n`, far beyond the constant-word certificate.

For equal scales, the with-replacement average is in fact exactly
\[
a^n\left(\frac{1+(n-1)c}{n}\right)^{n-1},
\]
so the comparison reduces to the scalar inequality `1+(n-1)c>=nc`.

# 11. Next attack

The new rank-one transfer identity turns the arbitrary-`n` rank-one problem into a concrete graph-kernel inequality:
\[
\left(\prod_i a_i\right)H_n(G)
\stackrel?\le
\frac1{n^n}q^TM^{n-1}q.
\]
The equiangular theorem shows that large overlap is not itself an obstruction; the issue is heterogeneity of the absolute Gram kernel. The next high-value step is therefore to identify a Gram-positivity or walk-moment condition implying
\[
H_n(G)\le
\left(\frac{a^TGa}{(\sum_i a_i)^2}\right)^{n-1},
\]
or a stronger higher-moment substitute. That would extend the arbitrary-`n` result far beyond the equiangular class.

For general PSD matrices, the maximum-matching certificate suggests replacing the crude `gamma_*` by exact weighted matching polynomials and then charging additional repeated-word occupancy classes when the matching mean slightly exceeds `Theta`. This gives a systematic interpolation between the newly solved weak-interaction region and the difficult strongly interacting `n=4` regime.
