# 3. Notation for `n=4`

Let `A_1,...,A_4` be PSD and define
\[
S:=\sum_{\sigma\in S_4}
\|A_{\sigma(1)}A_{\sigma(2)}A_{\sigma(3)}A_{\sigma(4)}\|,
\]
\[
R:=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^4\|A_iA_jA_kA_\ell\|.
\]
Partition by multiplicity pattern:
\[
R=S+Q_{211}+Q_{22}+Q_{31}+Q_4.
\tag{3.1}
\]
Set
\[
a_i:=\|A_i\|,
\qquad
p_{ij}:=\|A_iA_j\|,
\qquad
P:=\sum_{i<j}p_{ij}^2,
\]
\[
E:=\sum_{i<j}a_i^2a_j^2,
\qquad
Q:=\sum_i a_i^4=Q_4.
\]
The elementary bounds
\[
S\le4P,
\qquad
P\le E\le\frac32Q
\tag{3.2}
\]
are already durable and are also immediate from pair splitting, `p_ij<=a_i a_j`, and scalar Cauchy.

# 4. New two-matrix `3+1` layer theorem

Fix two PSD matrices `A,B` and put
\[
p:=\|AB\|.
\]
Define the two PSD quantities
\[
h_A:=\|A^{3/2}BA^{3/2}\|,
\qquad
h_B:=\|B^{3/2}AB^{3/2}\|.
\tag{4.1}
\]

## Lemma 4.1 — complementary Cauchy product
\[
\boxed{h_Ah_B\ge p^4.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

### Proof
Apply the Bhatia–Davis norm Cauchy inequality (2.1) with
\[
P=A,
\qquad
Q=B,
\qquad
X=A^{1/2}B^{1/2}.
\]
Then
\[
P^{1/2}XQ^{1/2}
=A^{1/2}(A^{1/2}B^{1/2})B^{1/2}
=AB,
\]
so the left side of (2.1) is `p^2`.

The first right-hand factor is
\[
\|PX\|=\|A^{3/2}B^{1/2}\|.
\]
Its square is
\[
\|A^{3/2}B^{1/2}\|^2
=
\|B^{1/2}A^3B^{1/2}\|.
\]
The latter PSD matrix and `A^{3/2}BA^{3/2}` are respectively `X^*X` and `XX^*` for `X=A^{3/2}B^{1/2}`, hence have the same nonzero spectrum and the same norm.  Therefore
\[
\|PX\|=\sqrt{h_A}.
\]
Likewise
\[
\|XQ\|=\|A^{1/2}B^{3/2}\|=\sqrt{h_B}.
\]
Thus (2.1) gives
\[
p^2\le\sqrt{h_Ah_B},
\]
which is (4.2).  QED.

## Lemma 4.2 — each four-word half dominates its PSD core
The four words with three copies of `A` and one copy of `B` each have norm at least `h_A`:
\[
\|A^3B\|,
\|A^2BA\|,
\|ABA^2\|,
\|BA^3\|
\ge h_A.
\tag{4.3}
\]

### Proof
For `A^3B`, write
\[
X=A^{3/2},\qquad Y=A^{3/2}B.
\]
Then `XY=A^3B` and `YX=A^{3/2}BA^{3/2}`.  The matrices `XY` and `YX` have the same nonzero eigenvalues, while `YX` is PSD.  Hence
\[
\rho(A^3B)=h_A,
\]
and therefore `||A^3B||>=h_A`.  Its adjoint `BA^3` has the same norm.

For `A^2BA`, take
\[
X=A^{1/2},\qquad Y=A^{3/2}BA.
\]
Then
\[
XY=A^2BA,
\qquad
YX=A^{3/2}BA^{3/2}.
\]
The same `XY/YX` spectral argument gives
\[
\|A^2BA\|\ge h_A.
\]
Its adjoint `ABA^2` has the same norm.  QED.

By symmetry, the four words with three copies of `B` and one copy of `A` each have norm at least `h_B`.

## Theorem 4.3 — sharp pairwise `3+1` lower bound
The eight `3+1` words formed from the unordered pair `{A,B}` satisfy
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\|A^3B\|+\|A^2BA\|+\|ABA^2\|+\|BA^3\|\\
&\quad+\|B^3A\|+\|B^2AB\|+\|BAB^2\|+\|AB^3\|
\ge8\|AB\|^2.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{4.4}
\]

Indeed, Lemma 4.2 and its symmetric counterpart give a lower bound `4h_A+4h_B`.  Scalar AM–GM and Lemma 4.1 give
\[
4(h_A+h_B)
\ge8\sqrt{h_Ah_B}
\ge8p^2.
\]

Equality is attained when `A=B` (and in scalar commuting equality configurations), so the coefficient `8` is sharp.

# 5. Exact `Q_31` occupancy theorem

Sum (4.4) over the six unordered pairs `{i,j}`.  Every `3+1` word in `Q_31` appears exactly once.  Therefore
\[
\boxed{Q_{31}\ge8P.}
\tag{5.1}
\]
Since the old perfect-matching estimate gives
\[
S\le4P,
\]
we obtain the sharp layer inequality
\[
\boxed{Q_{31}\ge2S.}
\tag{5.2}
\]

This exactly matches the equal-matrix combinatorial ratio: there are 48 `3+1` words and 24 distinct words.

This supersedes the older aggregate bound
\[
Q_{31}\ge8P^{3/2}/\sqrt E
\]
for the present operator-norm problem, because `E>=P`.

# 6. Re-optimization of the arbitrary-PSD `n=4` bound

The durable collision estimates used here are
\[
Q_{211}
\ge
2S+\frac{S^2}{2\sqrt{PE}},
\tag{6.1}
\]
\[
Q_{22}
\ge
4P+\frac{2P^2}{E},
\tag{6.2}
\]
and the new theorem
\[
Q_{31}\ge8P.
\tag{6.3}
\]
Also
\[
Q_4=Q,
\qquad
Q\ge\frac23E
\tag{6.4}
\]
by `E<=3Q/2`.

Substitute into (3.1):
\[
R
\ge
3S
+\frac{S^2}{2\sqrt{PE}}
+12P
+\frac{2P^2}{E}
+Q.
\tag{6.5}
\]
Normalize
\[
s:=\frac SP,
\qquad
e:=\frac EP,
\qquad q:=\frac QP.
\]
The constraints are
\[
0<s\le4,
\qquad e\ge1,
\qquad q\ge\frac{2e}{3}.
\tag{6.6}
\]
Dividing (6.5) by `S`,
\[
\frac RS
\ge
3+\frac{s}{2\sqrt e}
+\frac{12+2/e+q}{s}.
\tag{6.7}
\]
For fixed `s,e`, this is increasing in `q`, so set `q=2e/3`.  For fixed `e`, the right side is strictly decreasing on `0<s<=4`: indeed
\[
\partial_s
=
\frac1{2\sqrt e}
-
\frac{12+2/e+2e/3}{s^2}
\le
\frac12-\frac{12}{16}<0.
\]
Hence the relaxed minimum occurs at `s=4`.

We are left with
\[
\frac RS
\ge
F(e)
:=
6+\frac{2}{\sqrt e}+\frac{1}{2e}+\frac e6,
\qquad e\ge1.
\tag{6.8}
\]
The derivative is
\[
F'(e)
=
\frac16-e^{-3/2}-\frac12e^{-2},
\]
and
\[
F''(e)=\frac32e^{-5/2}+e^{-3}>0.
\]
Thus there is a unique minimizer.  Writing `e=x^2`, it is the unique root `x>1` of
\[
\boxed{x^4-6x-3=0.}
\tag{6.9}
\]
Numerically,
\[
x_*=1.9600874554733903\ldots,
\qquad
e_*=3.8419428331041499\ldots,
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\frac RS
\ge
7.7908289283058305\ldots .
}
\tag{6.10}
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{
\frac{R/256}{S/24}
=
\frac{3R}{32S}
\ge
0.7303902120286716\ldots .
}
\tag{6.11}
\]

This is a substantial improvement over the previous durable arbitrary-dimensional factor `0.6293369187589889...`, but is still below the conjectured value `1`.
