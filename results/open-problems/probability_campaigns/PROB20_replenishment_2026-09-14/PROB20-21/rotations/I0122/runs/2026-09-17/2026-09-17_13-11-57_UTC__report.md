## 2. Mathematical idea before code

The central idea of this run was to stop treating order 15 primarily as an enumeration problem and instead use the **order polytope** to connect pair probabilities to **expected ranks** and then import a sharp shifted Grünbaum halfspace inequality.

For an `n`-element poset, the order polytope is

\[
\mathcal O(P)=\{t\in[0,1]^P:t_u\le t_v\text{ whenever }u<_P v\}.
\]

Its standard triangulation has one equal-volume simplex for each linear extension. This gives a geometric representation of both the pair probability `p_P(x<y)` and the expected rank of every element. A halfspace theorem about a centroid can therefore become a quantitative lower bound on a linear-extension probability.

Only after obtaining the analytic reduction below did I write two small exact checkers: one verifies a rational order-15 threshold, and the other destroys a tempting but false expected-rank-gap lemma.

# B11 — dualize to order-polytope halfspaces

## 3. Exact order-polytope identities

Let `L(P)` be the set of linear extensions, and let

\[
\mu_x=\mathbb E[\operatorname{rank}_L(x)]
\]

for uniformly random `L in L(P)`.

The standard triangulation of `O(P)` has, for each linear extension `pi`, the simplex

\[
0\le t_{\pi(1)}\le t_{\pi(2)}\le\cdots\le t_{\pi(n)}\le1.
\]

Every such simplex has volume `1/n!`. Therefore a uniform point `T` in `O(P)` chooses each linear-extension simplex with equal probability. Conditional on a simplex, the coordinates are the order statistics of `n` independent uniform points, hence

\[
\mathbb E[T_x\mid \pi]=\frac{\operatorname{rank}_\pi(x)}{n+1}.
\]

Consequently the centroid `c` of `O(P)` satisfies

\[
\boxed{c_x=\frac{\mu_x}{n+1}.}
\]

Also, ignoring the measure-zero hyperplane `t_x=t_y`,

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O(P)\cap\{t_x<t_y\})}
{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O(P))}
=p_P(x<y).
}
\]

Thus pair probabilities are literally halfspace-volume fractions of the order polytope.

## 4. Centroid-direction lemma

Grünbaum's centroid theorem gives, for every convex body in dimension `n`, a volume fraction at least

\[
\gamma_n=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n>e^{-1}>\frac13
\]

for every halfspace containing the centroid.

If `mu_x <= mu_y`, then `c_x<=c_y`, so the halfspace `{t_x<=t_y}` contains the centroid. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\mu_x\le\mu_y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p_P(x<y)\ge\gamma_n>\frac13.
}
\]

Two immediate consequences matter for a hypothetical counterexample `P` with `delta(P)<1/3`:

1. No incomparable pair can have equal expected rank. If `mu_x=mu_y`, both opposite halfspaces contain the centroid, so both orientations have probability at least `gamma_n>1/3`.
2. For every incomparable pair with `mu_x<mu_y`, the strong majority orientation must agree with expected-rank order:

\[
\boxed{p_P(x<y)>\frac23.}
\]

Indeed `p_P(x<y)>1/3` from Grünbaum; since the pair is assumed unbalanced, the opposite probability must be `<1/3`.

So in any counterexample the `>2/3` orientation on every incomparable pair is acyclic and is induced by strict ordering of the expected ranks. I do **not** claim novelty for this observation: older mutual-rank-probability / cycle-free-cut literature may subsume it. It is used here only as a source-compatible reduction.

## 5. Shifted Grünbaum gives a quantitative expected-rank-gap bound

The more useful import is the shifted Grünbaum inequality of Letwin and Yaskin (arXiv:2410.04741). In the form quoted in later convex-geometry work: if a dimension-`n` convex body `K` has centroid `0`, `xi != 0`, and

\[
H_{\alpha,\xi}=\{z:\langle z,\xi\rangle\ge \alpha h_K(-\xi)\},
\qquad 0<\alpha<\frac1n,
\]

then

\[
\frac{\operatorname{vol}(K\cap H_{\alpha,\xi})}{\operatorname{vol}(K)}
\ge
\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
(1+\alpha)^{n-1}(1-n\alpha).
\]

I checked the hypotheses explicitly for an order polytope.

Take incomparable `x,y`, assume `mu_x<mu_y`, and put

\[
d=\mu_y-\mu_x,\qquad m=\frac{d}{n+1}.
\]

Center `O(P)` at its centroid and let `ell(t)=t_y-t_x`. Because `x,y` are incomparable, both relative orders occur in linear extensions and the support of `ell` on `O(P)` is exactly `[-1,1]`. Hence on the centered body

\[
h_K(\ell)=1-m.
\]

The minority event `y<x` is the halfspace `ell<0`, which after centering is

\[
-\ell\ge m.
\]

Choose `xi=-ell`. Then

\[
\alpha=\frac{m}{1-m}=\frac{d}{n+1-d}.
\]

The shifted theorem applies whenever `d<1`, because then `alpha<1/n`. Substitution and cancellation give the clean formula

\[
\boxed{
 p_P(y<x)
 \ge L_n(d)
 :=\frac{n^n(1-d)}{(n+1-d)^n},
 \qquad 0\le d<1.
}
\]

The algebra is

\[
\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
\left(\frac{n+1}{n+1-d}\right)^{n-1}
\left(\frac{(n+1)(1-d)}{n+1-d}\right)
=
\frac{n^n(1-d)}{(n+1-d)^n}.
\]

This is the strongest concrete analytic output of the run. It does **not** prove the source conjecture, but it translates a continuous statistic — expected-rank separation — into a certified lower bound for the minority orientation probability.

\]

# B2 — attack the negation first

## 6. Necessary conditions for a minimal order-15 counterexample

Because every non-chain poset through order 14 is already certified, any first finite counterexample would have exactly 15 elements. Combining the existing literature with the B11 reduction yields a concrete sieve. Any order-15 counterexample must satisfy all of the following.

### 6.1 It must be asymmetric

Peczarski's later Gold-Partition work strengthens the older automorphism result: a finite non-chain poset with a nontrivial automorphism satisfies the Gold Partition Conjecture, hence the 1/3–2/3 conjecture. Therefore a counterexample must have trivial automorphism group.

### 6.2 It cannot be 6-thin

Peczarski proved the Gold Partition Conjecture for 6-thin posets. Therefore a counterexample must contain at least one element incomparable with at least seven other elements.

### 6.3 It cannot be N-free

The 1/3–2/3 conjecture is known for N-free posets, and Peczarski later proved the stronger Gold-Partition statement for a wider class. Hence a counterexample must at least lie outside the N-free class and outside Peczarski's larger certified class.

### 6.4 It cannot contain a non-chain substitution block covered by the lexicographic-sum theorem

Dolores-Cuenca, Guzmán-Sáenz and Kim prove: if a finite poset `Q` satisfies the Gold Partition Conjecture and `P` is arbitrary, then replacing one point of `P` by `Q` via their lexicographic sum preserves the Gold Partition Conjecture.

Every non-chain `Q` of order at most 14 is certified by Gupta. Therefore an order-15 counterexample cannot be represented as such a one-point lexicographic substitution with a non-chain inserted block `Q` of size `2,...,14`. This does not exclude every possible modular decomposition — in particular I am not silently treating chain blocks as Gold-Partition instances — but it removes a broad exact class.

### 6.5 Its incomparable-pair majority relation must be ordered by expected rank

By Section 4, incomparable expected ranks are all distinct, and

\[
\mu_x<\mu_y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_P(x<y)>2/3.
\]

Thus strong-majority cycles are impossible in a counterexample.

### 6.6 Every incomparable expected-rank gap exceeds `399/1000`

This is the new shifted-Grünbaum structural restriction from Section 5.

These six conditions are jointly much more restrictive than “an arbitrary order-15 poset,” although I have not counted the surviving isomorphism classes and therefore do not claim a numerical reduction factor.

## 7. A forced adjacent incomparable pair, and why the first obvious lemma fails

There is a simple additional fact useful for the next attack. In a hypothetical counterexample all expected ranks are distinct. Sort the elements so

\[
\mu_{v_1}<\mu_{v_2}<\cdots<\mu_{v_n}.
\]

If every consecutive pair were comparable, then necessarily

\[
v_1<_P v_2<_P\cdots<_P v_n,
\]

so `P` would be a chain. Thus at least one consecutive pair is incomparable.

Moreover, if `v_i<_P v_{i+1}`, then in every linear extension

\[
\operatorname{rank}(v_{i+1})-\operatorname{rank}(v_i)\ge1,
\]

so its expected-rank gap is at least 1. If every consecutive incomparable pair also had gap `>1`, then every one of the `n-1` consecutive expected-rank gaps would be at least 1 and at least one would be strictly larger, forcing

\[
\mu_{v_n}-\mu_{v_1}>n-1,
\]

impossible because all ranks lie in `[1,n]`. Hence every hypothetical counterexample has a consecutive incomparable pair with

\[
0<d\le1.
\]

Combining with the new order-15 filter gives a very precise local target:

\[
\boxed{399/1000<d\le1}
\]

for some incomparable consecutive pair in expected-rank order.

A tempting next lemma would have been “an incomparable pair of expected-rank gap at most 1 is automatically 1/3-balanced.” That statement is **false**.

Take the disjoint union `C_2 + C_3`, with chains

`a0<a1` and `b0<b1<b2`.

There are exactly `binom(5,2)=10` linear extensions. Exact interleaving counts give

\[
(\mu_{a0},\mu_{a1},\mu_{b0},\mu_{b1},\mu_{b2})
=(2,4,3/2,3,9/2).
\]

Thus `a0` and `b1` are incomparable with expected-rank gap exactly 1, but

\[
\Pr(a0<b1)=7/10,\qquad \Pr(b1<a0)=3/10,
\]

so that pair has balance `3/10<1/3`.

This does **not** refute the source conjecture: the same poset has

\[
\delta(P)=2/5.
\]

# B16 — recover the exact finite machinery before computing

## 8. Gupta's exact order-14 certificate architecture

I recovered the public implementation rather than re-deriving a new checker from scratch. The released `src/gpc.c` converts each Hasse diagram to its transitive closure and applies Peczarski's sufficient certificates using exact integer arithmetic.

The core linear-extension recurrence on order ideals is the standard exact recurrence

\[
F(\varnothing)=1,\qquad
F(I)=\sum_{x\in\max(I)}F(I\setminus\{x\}).
\]

For each poset the implementation constructs order ideals, computes `e(P)`, and obtains exact pair counts. Its certificate pipeline is:

1. a cheap bilateral low-slave screen;
2. exact pair counts, including the half-balanced case;
3. low-slave orientation certificates when the relevant orientation occurs at least half the time;
4. exact triple certificates of Peczarski's form

\[
e(P+xy+yz)\le
\max\{e(P+yx),e(P+zy)\}
\le e(P)/2;
\]

5. report the class as `OPEN` only if none of these certificates applies.

Gupta's released order-14 aggregate is

- total unlabeled posets: `1,338,193,159,771`;
- pair certificates: `1,272,077,147,789`;
- triple certificates: `66,116,011,981`;
- open: `0`;
- plus the unique chain.

This exactly explains why `n_bal=14` is a legitimate frozen baseline.

## 9. Why naive order-15 exhaustion is the wrong first computation

At order 15 there are

\[
68,275,077,901,156
\]

unlabeled posets (OEIS A000112), more than fifty times the order-14 count. The per-poset dynamic program is still arithmetically modest: there are at most `2^15=32768` subsets/ideal states, and even the maximal linear-extension count `15! = 1,307,674,368,000` fits easily in unsigned 64-bit arithmetic. The bottleneck is the number of isomorphism classes, not integer overflow or the ideal recurrence itself.

Therefore this run did **not** launch a pretend “order-15 exhaustive check.” The better finite strategy is to put the cheap exact structural filters from B2 in front of Gupta's certificate machinery and measure the survivor set before considering deeper pair/triple DP. That can create a genuinely smaller certified order-15 search problem rather than simply scaling an already enormous census by another factor of about fifty.
