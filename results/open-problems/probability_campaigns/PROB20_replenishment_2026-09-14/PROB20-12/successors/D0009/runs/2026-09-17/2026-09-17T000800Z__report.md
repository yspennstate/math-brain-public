## 4. New idea before computation

The previous run showed that trying to sample an arbitrary mixing law `h` on the constant-sum hyperplane is essentially equivalent to solving the original problem. The present route instead asks whether Wang's **one-dimensional monotone source class** has its own canonical convex geometry that can be exposed *before* constructing a complete mix.

For a decreasing density, the answer is yes: write the margin as a mixture of uniforms `U[0,t]`. The crucial observation is that the source mean condition becomes a **single first-moment constraint on the layer length `t`**. Once that is done, the extreme points of the layer-measure class are only one- and two-point laws. This collapses the infinite-dimensional marginal class to a two-parameter family of two-step densities.

That is B24. B25 then gives an explicit barycentric law that samples those extremes directly from the marginal's layer measure. B3 asks for something stronger: identify exactly which extremes already admit an exact complete-mix sampler in arbitrary dimension and prove why the remaining generic extremes cannot be removed by another same-center mixture trick.

## 5. B24 — exact extremal reduction of the monotone source class

### 5.1 Classical uniform-layer representation with the mean retained

Let `f` be a nonincreasing probability density on `[0,1]`, choose its right-continuous version, and let

\[
\mu=\int_0^1 x f(x)\,dx,
\qquad
m:=2\mu.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Because `f` is nonincreasing and integrable,

\[
t f(t)\le \int_0^t f(x)\,dx\longrightarrow0
\qquad(t\downarrow0).
\tag{5.2}
\]

Define the Stieltjes probability measure `G` on `(0,1]` by

\[
G(dt)=-t\,df(t)\quad(0<t<1),
\qquad
G(\{1\})=f(1).
\tag{5.3}
\]

Then, at every continuity point of `f`,

\[
f(x)=\int_{[x,1]}\frac1t\,G(dt).
\tag{5.4}
\]

Equivalently, if `T~G` and conditional on `T=t` we draw `X~U[0,t]`, then `X` has density `f`.

The normalization and mean are exact. Stieltjes integration by parts gives

\[
\int G(dt)
=
\int_{(0,1)}-t\,df(t)+f(1)
=
\int_0^1 f(t)\,dt
=1,
\tag{5.5}
\]

and

\[
\int t\,G(dt)
=
\int_{(0,1)}-t^2\,df(t)+f(1)
=
2\int_0^1 t f(t)\,dt
=2\mu=m.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Thus the monotone margin `F` is the affine image, under

\[
\mathcal L(G)=\int U[0,t]\,G(dt),
\tag{5.7}
\]

of the convex set

\[
\mathcal G_m
:=
\left\{G:\ G\text{ probability on }(0,1],\ \int t\,G(dt)=m\right\}.
\tag{5.8}
\]

The map `G -> f` is injective because (5.4), or equivalently the Stieltjes derivative relation (5.3), recovers `G` from `f`. For `0<t<1` one can also read off the layer CDF directly:

\[
G((0,t])=F(t)-t f(t).
\tag{5.9}
\]

This is useful algorithmically: the layer law is determined by the one-dimensional margin itself; it is not the unknown hyperplane mixing law `h` from the previous attempt.

### 5.2 Extreme layer laws

Fix `m in (0,1]`. Besides the one-point law `delta_m`, the natural two-point fixed-mean laws are

\[
K_{a,b}
:=
\alpha\,\delta_a+\beta\,\delta_b,
\qquad
0<a<m<b\le1,
\tag{5.10}
\]

where

\[
\alpha=\frac{b-m}{b-a},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{m-a}{b-a}.
\tag{5.11}
\]

Then

\[
\alpha+\beta=1,
\qquad
\alpha a+\beta b=m.
\tag{5.12}
\]

Their layer images are the two-step decreasing laws

\[
F_{a,b}^{(m)}
=
\alpha U[0,a]+\beta U[0,b].
\tag{5.13}
\]

**Claim 5.1.** The extreme points of `G_m` are exactly `delta_m` and the two-point laws `K_{a,b}` above (with the harmless limiting cases at the boundary understood when present).

**Proof.** A one-point fixed-mean probability measure must be `delta_m`. A two-point measure with one point below and one above `m` has its weights uniquely forced by the two equations mass=`1` and mean=`m`, giving (5.11); hence it admits no nontrivial fixed-mean perturbation supported on the same two points.

Conversely, if a fixed-mean law has either mass at `m` plus other mass, or more than one effective location on one side of `m`, or support on at least three effective locations, then Section 6 below writes it explicitly as a nontrivial mixture of `delta_m` and two-point fixed-mean laws. Therefore it is not extreme unless that barycentric law collapses to a single component. QED.

Because the layer map is affine and injective, the corresponding `U[0,m]` and two-step laws (5.13) are exactly the extreme objects of this normalized decreasing-density class at fixed mean.

### 5.3 Every extreme stays inside the same source sufficient class

Suppose the original decreasing law is in Wang's `n`-CM source class. In normalized coordinates this means

\[
\mu\ge\frac1n,
\qquad m=2\mu,
\qquad nm\ge2.
\tag{5.14}
\]

For any pair component (5.13), its support is `[0,b]`, its mean is still `m/2=mu`, and `b<=1`. Hence

\[
\mu=\frac m2\ge\frac1n\ge\frac bn.
\tag{5.15}
\]

So **every two-step extreme produced by the reduction is itself source-valid for the same `n` and the same center**. The uniform component `U[0,m]` is source-valid as well.

This matters: no source hypothesis is lost in the reduction.

## 6. B25 — explicit barycentric decomposition and sampler for the extreme selector

The usual statement “a fixed-mean probability measure is in the convex hull of two-point measures” is not enough for Open Problem 9. We need the actual mixing law. Here it is.

Let `G in G_m`. Write

\[
c:=G(\{m\}),
\qquad
G_L:=G|_{(0,m)},
\qquad
G_R:=G|_{(m,1]},
\tag{6.1}
\]

and let

\[
\ell:=G_L((0,m)),
\qquad
r:=G_R((m,1]).
\tag{6.2}
\]

The fixed-mean identity gives the same deficit and surplus:

\[
D
:=
\int_{a<m}(m-a)\,G(da)
=
\int_{b>m}(b-m)\,G(db).
\tag{6.3}
\]

If `D=0`, then `G=delta_m` and there is nothing to do. Assume `D>0`; then `ell,r>0`.

Define a measure on pairs `a<m<b` by

\[
H(da,db)
:=
\frac{b-a}{D}\,G_L(da)G_R(db).
\tag{6.4}
\]

### Theorem 6.1 — exact fixed-mean layer decomposition

We have

\[
\boxed{
G
=
c\,\delta_m
+
\int K_{a,b}\,H(da,db).
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

Moreover

\[
H((0,m)\times(m,1])=1-c.
\tag{6.6}
\]

**Proof.** First,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int H(da,db)
&=
\frac1D
\iint[(m-a)+(b-m)]\,G_L(da)G_R(db)\\
&=
\frac1D[Dr+\ell D]
=
\ell+r
=
1-c.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.7}
\]

For a fixed low point `a`, multiplying (6.4) by the low weight `alpha=(b-m)/(b-a)` cancels the pair length:

\[
\alpha H(da,db)
=
\frac{b-m}{D}G_L(da)G_R(db).
\tag{6.8}
\]

Integrating over `b` gives exactly `G_L(da)` because the surplus integral equals `D`. Similarly,

\[
\beta H(da,db)
=
\frac{m-a}{D}G_L(da)G_R(db),
\tag{6.9}
\]

and integrating over `a` gives exactly `G_R(db)`. Adding the unchanged central atom proves (6.5). QED.

Applying the affine layer map gives the **margin-level decomposition**

\[
\boxed{
F
=
c\,U[0,m]
+
\int
\left(
\frac{b-m}{b-a}U[0,a]
+
\frac{m-a}{b-a}U[0,b]
\right)
H(da,db).
}
\tag{6.10}
\]

Every component in (6.10) has the same mean `m/2=mu` and, by Section 5.3, lies in the same Wang source class.

### 6.2 Sampling `H` without a two-dimensional rejection problem

Formula (6.4) is itself directly decomposable because

\[
b-a=(m-a)+(b-m).
\tag{6.11}
\]

Consequently

\[
H
=
\underbrace{\frac{m-a}{D}G_L(da)G_R(db)}_{\text{total mass }r}
+
\underbrace{\frac{b-m}{D}G_L(da)G_R(db)}_{\text{total mass }\ell}.
\tag{6.12}
\]

Thus an exact pair selector can be written as the following one-dimensional primitive scheme:

1. with probability `c`, select the central component `delta_m`;
2. with probability `r`, draw
   - `A` from the deficit-biased left law `(m-a)G_L(da)/D`,
   - `B` independently from the ordinary normalized right law `G_R(db)/r`;
3. with probability `ell`, draw
   - `A` from the ordinary normalized left law `G_L(da)/ell`,
   - `B` independently from the surplus-biased right law `(b-m)G_R(db)/D`.

The two noncentral branches have exactly the law `H` in (6.4). In an exact-real / inverse-CDF oracle model, all of these are one-dimensional Stieltjes laws determined explicitly by `f` through (5.3) or (5.9).

This is a genuine reduction of the earlier unknown hyperplane mixing measure: from `F` alone we can now sample the **extreme marginal component** `(A,B)` without already knowing a complete mix. What remains is the complete-mix kernel for a generic two-step extreme.

## 7. B3 — stronger reduction theorem and exact integer-occupancy strata

### Theorem 7.1 — source-general simulation reduces to two-step extremes

Fix `n>=3`. Suppose there exists one exact procedure `S_n(a,b,m)` which, for every

\[
0<a<m<b\le1,
\qquad
m\ge \frac{2b}{n},
\tag{7.1}
\]

returns `n` random variables with common marginal

\[
F_{a,b}^{(m)}
=
\frac{b-m}{b-a}U[0,a]
+
\frac{m-a}{b-a}U[0,b]
\tag{7.2}
\]

and exact sum `nm/2`.

Then, using the explicit selector in Section 6 plus the trivial exact sampler for `U[0,m]`, one obtains an exact sampler for **every decreasing density on `[0,1]` satisfying `mu>=1/n`**. Reflection handles every increasing source-valid density, and affine rescaling handles arbitrary bounded support `[A,B]`.

**Proof.** Sample the component from (6.10). Conditional on the selected component, invoke either the uniform sampler or `S_n(A,B,m)`. Every conditional component has the target center `m/2`, hence every conditional joint sum is exactly `nm/2`; mixing therefore preserves the same deterministic sum. Equation (6.10) gives each coordinate the original marginal `F`. QED.

This is stronger and more useful than another isolated source-valid family: **for the frozen source class, the entire remaining mathematical problem is the two-parameter extreme kernel (7.2).**

### 7.2 Exact sampler on integer-occupancy extreme strata

Write

\[
\beta:=\frac{m-a}{b-a}.
\tag{7.3}
\]

Suppose

\[
\beta=\frac{k}{n}
\qquad
\text{for some }k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}.
\tag{7.4}
\]

Take `n-k` uniform distributions on `[0,a]` and `k` uniform distributions on `[0,b]`. Their total support length is

\[
(n-k)a+kb
=n[(1-\beta)a+\beta b]
=nm,
\tag{7.5}
\]

and the largest length is `b`. Source validity (7.1) gives

\[
nm\ge2b=2\max\{a,b\}.
\tag{7.6}
\]

Therefore the exact uniform-interval joint-mixability criterion of Wang--Wang (2016, Theorem 3.1) applies. Importantly, its proof is constructive: for three uniforms it decomposes the tuple into six explicitly weighted uniform subtriples reducible to the line case, and for `n>3` it gives a recursive reduction by merging two intervals.

After drawing that nonidentical uniform joint mix, apply an independent uniformly random permutation of the `n` coordinate labels. Each output coordinate then sees a long interval exactly `k/n=beta` of the time and a short interval `1-beta` of the time. Hence every output coordinate has precisely (7.2), while the sum remains the same deterministic value.

Thus every extreme satisfying (7.4) has a constructive exact sampler.

This includes, for `n=3`, the genuinely nontrivial strata `beta=1/3` and `beta=2/3`. The recovered exact checker archives the rational boundary example

\[
a=\frac12,\qquad b=1,\qquad m=\frac23,\qquad \beta=\frac13,
\tag{7.7}
\]

with the explicit line coupling

\[
Y_1=\frac14+\frac V2,
\qquad
Y_2=\frac14+\frac V2,
\qquad
Y_3=\frac12-V,
\qquad
V\sim U[-1/2,1/2],
\tag{7.8}
\]

for which `Y1+Y2+Y3=1`, `Y1,Y2~U[0,1/2]`, and `Y3~U[0,1]`; random permutation gives the required identical two-step marginal.

### 7.3 Exact no-go for repeating the same-center mixture trick

The integer strata do **not** solve the generic extreme.

Because `K_{a,b}` is an extreme point of the fixed-mean layer-measure set `G_m`, and because the layer map is affine and injective, a generic two-step law (7.2) cannot be represented nontrivially as a same-center mixture of *other decreasing laws* in the same layer class.

In particular, if

\[
n\beta\notin\mathbb Z,
\tag{7.9}
\]

then no convex mixing of the integer-occupancy decreasing extremes at the same center can reproduce (7.2). If it did, injectivity of the layer map would express the extreme two-point law `K_{a,b}` as a nontrivial fixed-mean mixture, contradicting extremality.

There is also a direct latent-count version of the obstruction. Any scheme which first chooses only the two original layer lengths `{a,b}` for each of the `n` coordinates and then, conditional on those lengths, jointly mixes the corresponding anchored uniforms with the **same target constant sum** must use a deterministic number `K` of long intervals. The conditional sum of marginal means is

\[
\frac12[(n-K)a+Kb].
\tag{7.10}
\]

For this to equal the required

\[
\frac{nm}{2}
=
\frac n2[(1-\beta)a+\beta b]
\tag{7.11}
\]

one must have

\[
K=n\beta.
\tag{7.12}
\]

Hence the obvious latent-layer algorithm is impossible when (7.9) holds. Randomizing `K` does not repair it if each conditional branch is required to have the same deterministic final sum: each branch separately must satisfy (7.12).

This is not a counterexample to complete mixability. It is a precise failure theorem for the most natural layer-based simulation architecture. A successful generic sampler must therefore do at least one of the following:

- split the original uniform layers into nontrivial subintervals whose centers compensate across coordinates;
- use non-monotone intermediate component laws;
- use an infinite compatible refinement rather than a finite fixed layer-count decomposition;
- or construct the constant-sum transport directly on the quantile/simplex geometry.

That is the main obstruction exposed by this run.

## 9. Relation to prior decomposition results

The 2012 Puccetti--Wang--Wang decomposition theorem is close enough that the distinction must be explicit.

Their theorem says, in particular, that once a law is known to be `n`-CM, it can be represented (or approximated/represented in the discrete case) by same-center `n`-discrete uniform components, and the complete mix can then be obtained by random permutation of each component's `n` support points. The proof begins from an existing complete mix or from discrete CM data.

The present layer reduction is different in scope:

1. it is constructed **directly from the one-dimensional monotone density `f`**, without assuming access to an existing complete mix;
2. its selector law is given explicitly by (6.4)/(6.12);
3. it reduces the source-general monotone simulation problem to the two-step extreme family (7.2);
4. it identifies exactly why the simplest layer-count realization succeeds on `n beta in Z` and fails generically.

This distinction is necessary to avoid circularly declaring Open Problem 9 solved by an existence decomposition whose mixing law already presupposes the desired coupling.

No claim of publication novelty is made before a deeper prior-art search focused specifically on fixed-mean uniform-layer Choquet decompositions and constructive mixability algorithms.

## 10. Failed routes and what a successful next step must contain

### Failure A — same-center recursive mixture inside the decreasing class

The extreme-point theorem blocks it. Generic `F_{a,b}^{(m)}` is already extreme in the fixed-mean layer class, so another common-center decomposition into simpler decreasing laws cannot exist.

### Failure B — use only the original two layer lengths and randomize their count

Equation (7.12) forces `K=n beta` branch-by-branch. For generic beta this is noninteger. Randomizing between `floor(n beta)` and `ceil(n beta)` changes the conditional center and therefore violates the deterministic final sum unless the conditional distributions themselves are distorted.

### Failure C — simply execute the Wang--Wang monotone-density existence proof

The later joint-mixability paper explicitly uses approximate-mixability classes `M_n(K,C)` for the step-density reduction and then passes through limiting/closure arguments to exact mixability. That proves existence but does not directly provide one exact output draw from an arbitrary input density. Treating the limit as an exact sampler without compatible refinement kernels would repeat the previous D0009-A001 gap.

### What the next construction must add

The smallest remaining source-general kernel is now explicit:

\[
\boxed{
F_{a,b}^{(m)}
=
\frac{b-m}{b-a}U[0,a]
+
\frac{m-a}{b-a}U[0,b],
\quad
0<a<m<b\le1,
\quad
m\ge2b/n.
}
\tag{10.1}
\]
