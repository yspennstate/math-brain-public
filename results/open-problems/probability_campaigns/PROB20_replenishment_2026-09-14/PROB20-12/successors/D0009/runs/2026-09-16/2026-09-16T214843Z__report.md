## 4. Mathematical idea before code

The preceding run's refinement criterion attacks the weak-limit proof from the discrete side. This run deliberately changes direction.

For `n=3` at the lower-boundary center `mu=1/3`, the constant-sum hyperplane inside `[0,1]^3` is the standard simplex

\[
\Delta=\{(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^3:x+y+z=1\}.
\]

Instead of starting from a marginal density `f` and trying to reconstruct an unknown mixing measure, start with a **symmetric probability density directly on the simplex**. Symmetry makes all three coordinate marginals identical automatically, while support on `Delta` makes the sum exactly one automatically. The only remaining task is to choose a nonnegative symmetric simplex density whose one-coordinate marginal is a source-valid decreasing density.

This is B18 (change language) and B30 (localize one direction/scale): reduce the first unresolved source case to an explicit marginal operator on one fixed two-dimensional simplex. B6 then deforms a solved point — the uniform simplex law — in a symmetry-preserving direction that keeps the joint density positive.

The baseline uniform simplex density is `2` with respect to `dx dy` on

\[
\{x\ge0,y\ge0,x+y\le1\},\qquad z=1-x-y.
\]

Its one-coordinate marginal is

\[
f_0(x)=2(1-x),
\]

the Beta(1,2) density. This is a non-uniform decreasing law exactly on the `n=3` lower mean boundary, and sampling a uniform point on the simplex is already an exact complete-mix sampler.

The new question is whether this construction has a genuine source-valid neighborhood rather than being an isolated curiosity.

## 5. B18/B6/B30 exact simplex-perturbation theorem

Let

\[
e_2(x,y,z)=xy+yz+zx,
\qquad z=1-x-y,
\]

and for a real parameter `epsilon` define

\[
w_\epsilon(x,y,z)
=2+\epsilon\left(e_2(x,y,z)-\frac14\right)
\tag{5.1}
\]

on `Delta`.

### Theorem 1 — a continuum of exact source-valid 3-mix samplers

For every

\[
\boxed{-\frac{40}{3}\le \epsilon\le \frac83,}
\tag{5.2}
\]

`w_epsilon` is a probability density on `Delta`, symmetric in `(x,y,z)`, and strictly positive. Its three coordinate marginals are identical with density

\[
\boxed{
 f_\epsilon(x)
 =(1-x)\left[
 2-\frac{\epsilon}{12}(10x^2-8x+1)
 \right],\qquad 0\le x\le1.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Each `f_epsilon` is a nonnegative nonincreasing probability density on `[0,1]` with mean exactly `1/3`. Therefore every `f_epsilon` belongs to the exact Wang–Wang monotone-density source class for `n=3`, and `(X,Y,Z)` with joint density `w_epsilon` is already an exact complete mix:

\[
X\sim Y\sim Z\sim f_\epsilon,
\qquad
X+Y+Z=1\quad\text{a.s.}
\]

Moreover there is a uniform exact rejection sampler over the entire parameter interval whose acceptance probability is at least (in fact exactly, with the chosen envelope) `3/8`.

### Proof

#### Step 1: normalization of the joint density

The simplex has area `1/2`. Direct integration gives

\[
\int_\Delta e_2\,dx\,dy=\frac18.
\]

Hence

\[
\int_\Delta\left(e_2-\frac14\right)dx\,dy
=\frac18-\frac14\frac12=0,
\]

and therefore

\[
\int_\Delta w_\epsilon\,dx\,dy=1
\]

for every real `epsilon`.

#### Step 2: positivity on the full simplex

For nonnegative `x,y,z` with `x+y+z=1`,

\[
e_2=\frac{1-(x^2+y^2+z^2)}2,
\]

so

\[
0\le e_2\le\frac13.
\]

If `epsilon>=0`, the minimum of (5.1) occurs at `e_2=0`, and throughout (5.2)

\[
w_\epsilon\ge 2-\frac{1}{4}\frac83=\frac43>0.
\]

If `epsilon<=0`, the minimum occurs at `e_2=1/3`, and throughout (5.2)

\[
w_\epsilon\ge 2+\frac1{12}\left(-\frac{40}{3}\right)=\frac89>0.
\]

Thus `w_epsilon` is a genuine strictly positive density on the whole simplex for the complete parameter interval.

#### Step 3: exact marginal

Fix the first coordinate `x`. Since `y` runs from `0` to `1-x` and `z=1-x-y`, integrate (5.1):

\[
\begin{aligned}
f_\epsilon(x)
&=\int_0^{1-x}w_\epsilon(x,y,1-x-y)\,dy\\
&=2(1-x)
+\frac{\epsilon}{12}(x-1)(10x^2-8x+1)\\
&=(1-x)\left[2-\frac{\epsilon}{12}(10x^2-8x+1)\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

Because the joint density is invariant under all coordinate permutations, the same formula is the marginal density of all three coordinates.

The joint law is supported on `x+y+z=1`; symmetry therefore also gives immediately

\[
E[X]=E[Y]=E[Z]=\frac13.
\]

The exact checker independently verifies `int f_epsilon=1` and `int x f_epsilon=1/3` symbolically.

#### Step 4: exact monotonicity interval

Differentiate (5.3):

\[
f_\epsilon'(x)
=\frac{\epsilon(10x^2-12x+3)-8}{4}.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Write

\[
p(x)=10x^2-12x+3.
\]

On `[0,1]`, `p` is convex,

\[
p(0)=3,\qquad p(1)=1,
\]

and its unique minimum is at `x=3/5`, where

\[
p(3/5)=-\frac35.
\]

For `epsilon>=0`, the maximum of `epsilon p(x)-8` occurs at the maximum of `p`, namely `p(0)=3`. Hence `f_epsilon'<=0` for all `x` exactly when (within this sign branch)

\[
3\epsilon-8\le0,
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\epsilon\le\frac83.
\]

For `epsilon<=0`, multiplication reverses which value of `p` is dangerous: the maximum of `epsilon p(x)-8` occurs at the minimum `p(3/5)=-3/5`. Thus

\[
-\frac35\epsilon-8\le0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\epsilon\ge-\frac{40}{3}.
\]

Together these are precisely (5.2). Since `f_epsilon(1)=0`, nonincreasingness implies `f_epsilon(x)>=0` on `[0,1]`. The density is positive on `[0,1)` for the parameter endpoints as well, so its essential support is `[0,1]`.

Therefore every member of the family is a decreasing density on `[0,1]` with mean `1/3`, exactly satisfying the lower equality case of the source sufficient condition for `n=3`.

#### Step 5: exact sampler

A uniform point on `Delta` has density `2` with respect to `dx dy`. It can be generated without numerical approximation as follows in the usual ideal-real sampling model:

1. draw independent `U,V ~ Unif[0,1]`;
2. let `A=min(U,V)` and `B=max(U,V)`;
3. set
   \[
   (X,Y,Z)=(A,B-A,1-B).
   \]

The gap vector has the uniform Dirichlet `(1,1,1)` law, hence proposal density `2` on `Delta`.

Over (5.2), the largest possible target density is attained in the negative-`epsilon` endpoint at `e_2=0`:

\[
\sup w_\epsilon=\frac{16}{3}.
\]

Thus the target/proposal ratio is at most

\[
C=\frac{16/3}{2}=\frac83.
\]

Draw an independent `R~Unif[0,1]` and accept the proposed simplex point iff

\[
R\le \frac{w_\epsilon(X,Y,Z)}{2C}
=\frac{3}{16}w_\epsilon(X,Y,Z).
\tag{5.5}
\]

Standard rejection sampling then returns density exactly `w_epsilon`; its mean acceptance probability is exactly `1/C=3/8`. On acceptance the coordinates sum identically to one and, by Steps 1–4, each coordinate has exactly marginal density (5.3).

This completes the proof. QED.

### Scope of Theorem 1

This is a **restricted-family exact sampler**, not a solution of Open Problem 9 and not movement of `n_exact`. Its value is structural: it shows that exact source-valid `n=3` sampling is not confined to isolated orbit laws or to one singular construction. There is a continuous positive-dimensional family of smooth-polynomial perturbations of the uniform-simplex mechanism with a direct rejection sampler.

No novelty claim is made for the general perturb-a-simplex idea. The bounded literature search above did not locate this exact `e_2-1/4` parameter family, but a negative search is not a novelty certificate.

By affine transformation, the same construction gives exact lower-boundary samplers on any bounded interval `[a,b]`; reflection gives the corresponding increasing-density upper-boundary family.

## 6. B18 exact failure certificate for the layer-cake/radius architecture

The neighboring D0008 run had already observed qualitatively that representing a decreasing density as a mixture of initial-interval uniforms can merely move the unknown coupling to the radius law. Here is a source-valid example showing that this architecture can fail **provably**, not just inconveniently.

Take

\[
f(x)=\frac32\sqrt{1-x},\qquad 0\le x\le1.
\tag{6.1}
\]

This is the Beta `(1,3/2)` density. It is decreasing, normalized, and

\[
\mu=\int_0^1 x f(x)\,dx=\frac25>\frac13,
\]

so it lies in the exact source sufficient class for `n=3`.

Every decreasing density has its canonical initial-interval layer representation

\[
f(x)=\int_{[x,1]}\frac1t\,dH(t).
\]

For (6.1), differentiating gives an absolutely continuous mixing law

\[
dH(t)=\frac34\,t(1-t)^{-1/2}\,dt,
\tag{6.2}
\]

which is a Beta `(2,1/2)` law. Its mean is

\[
E_H[T]=\frac45.
\tag{6.3}
\]

Now consider the natural architecture:

1. couple radii `T_1,T_2,T_3`, each marginal `H`;
2. conditional on those radii, couple `X_i ~ Unif[0,T_i]` so that the final sum is the desired constant `3mu=6/5`.

Conditional expectation forces

\[
\frac{T_1+T_2+T_3}{2}=\frac65
\quad\text{a.s.},
\]

so this architecture requires

\[
T_1+T_2+T_3=\frac{12}{5}\quad\text{a.s.}
\tag{6.4}
\]

In other words `H` itself would have to be 3-completely mixable with center `4/5`.

But `H` has essential support `[0,1]`. The standard necessary mean condition for a 3-CM law on `[0,1]` is

\[
\frac13\le E[T]\le\frac23.
\]

Equation (6.3) violates the upper bound. Therefore `H` is not 3-CM, and (6.4) is impossible.

Hence:

\[
\boxed{\text{the canonical uniform-layer/radius conditional-mixing architecture cannot be source-general.}}
\]

This is **not** a counterexample to complete mixability of `F`; `F` is source-valid and is 3-CM. It is a counterexample only to that proposed construction architecture. Any successful sampler must couple across the layer decomposition more subtly than assigning one latent interval radius independently to each output coordinate and then joint-mixing conditional uniforms.

This exact failure explains why simply adding more precision to the earlier layer-cake route cannot solve D0009.

## 7. Why a tempting recursive use of the 2011 tail construction also fails

A second route looked close enough to merit recording precisely. In Wang–Wang's convex-minimization section, a copula `Q_n^P(c)` is built by splitting probability ranks into tails and a central interval. The tail branch is explicit; the central conditional law remains a monotone-density complete-mix problem. It is tempting to choose a positive `c`, recurse on the central law, and hope for geometric termination.

That does **not** solve exact complete mixing. On a tail branch with low rank `t`, the construction places the other `n-1` ranks at `1-(n-1)t`, so after applying the quantile `G` the branch sum is

\[
H(t)=G(t)+(n-1)G(1-(n-1)t).
\]

For general `G`, `H(t)` varies with `t`. Thus a positive-`c` tail branch does not have the required constant sum even though the construction preserves marginals and is useful for convex minimization. At complete mixability the paper's optimal cutoff is `c_n=0`, exactly where the explicit tail mass disappears and the unknown central complete mix becomes the whole problem.

Therefore recursive positive-tail peeling is another false shortcut: it terminates probabilistically but returns the wrong sum law. A successful recursion would have to replace the equal-high-rank tail line by a genuinely new residual allocation that enforces the quantile sum pointwise.

## 10. Failed routes and remaining obligation

## 11. Next attack

The strongest next question is whether the boundary class can be generated from explicit simplex pieces rather than one perturbation direction.

A decreasing density on `[0,1]` has the classical layer representation as a mixture of uniforms `U[0,t]`. At boundary mean `1/3`, the associated radius mixing law has mean `2/3`. Equivalently, the convex set of decreasing boundary densities is affinely isomorphic to probability measures on `[0,1]` with one fixed mean. Extreme measures of that one-moment set have support on at most two points, so the corresponding extreme marginal densities are two-step mixtures of initial-interval uniforms.

This suggests a genuinely different route: instead of trying to complete-mix the radius law, solve the **two-step extreme marginal** directly by a simplex measure. If every such extreme has an explicit constant-sum sampler with a measurable dependence on its two support points, a Choquet mixture over extreme samplers would preserve the common center `1/3` and solve the entire decreasing boundary class. That would be a major jump toward `n_exact=3`.
