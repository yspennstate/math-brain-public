## 3. Idea before code

The counterexample-first plan was to force a discrete parity/count obstruction using an absolutely continuous law whose mass is concentrated very close to the two endpoints of a symmetric bounded interval. The key realization is that exact zero-sum coupling in an **odd** number of coordinates has a deterministic endpoint-deficiency cost that does not involve any probabilistic union bound.

For `x in [-1,1]`, define its endpoint deficiency

\[
\delta(x)=1-|x|.
\]

If an odd number of points in `[-1,1]` sums exactly to zero, then their total deficiency is at least one. This turns the geometric parity obstruction into a single first-moment necessary condition, and lets one choose one fixed absolutely continuous law with that deficiency expectation as small as desired.

This is the main B2 advance of the run.

## 4. B2 — freeze one fixed candidate and certify large bad dimensions

### 4.1 Frozen candidate

The candidate is now frozen for this D0007 track:

\[
\boxed{
 f_*(x)=\frac{20001}{2}x^{20000}\mathbf 1_{[-1,1]}(x).
}
\]

Let `F_*` be the corresponding law. It is absolutely continuous, supported on the bounded interval `[-1,1]`, symmetric about zero, and has mean zero.

Normalization is exact:

\[
\frac{20001}{2}\int_{-1}^1 x^{20000}\,dx
=\frac{20001}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{20001}=1.
\]

This candidate is retained across future D0007 runs. Swapping to a different law will not count as movement in the frozen metric.

### 4.2 Deterministic odd-parity deficiency lemma

**Lemma.** Let `m` be odd and let `x_1,...,x_m in [-1,1]` satisfy

\[
\sum_{i=1}^m x_i=0.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i=1}^m (1-|x_i|)\ge 1.
}
\]

**Proof.** If any `x_i=0`, its single deficiency is `1`, so the result is immediate.

Assume therefore no `x_i` is zero. Let `p` be the number of positive entries and `r` the number of negative entries. Since `p+r=m` is odd, `p != r`. Interchanging signs if necessary, assume `p>r`, hence `p>=r+1`.

Let

\[
P=\sum_{x_i>0}x_i,
\qquad
N=\sum_{x_i<0}|x_i|.
\]

The zero-sum condition gives `P=N`. Because each negative entry has magnitude at most one,

\[
N\le r.
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_{i=1}^m |x_i|=P+N=2N\le2r\le m-1.
\]

Subtracting from `m` gives

\[
\sum_{i=1}^m(1-|x_i|)
=m-\sum_{i=1}^m|x_i|
\ge1.
\]

This proves the lemma. Equality is possible, for example with paired `+1,-1` entries and one zero. `square`

### 4.3 Consequence for any odd complete mix

Let `F` be any integrable symmetric law on `[-1,1]`. If `F` is odd-`m`-CM, then its center is its mean, hence zero. Thus there are `X_1,...,X_m ~ F` with

\[
X_1+\cdots+X_m=0\quad\text{a.s.}
\]

Apply the deterministic lemma pointwise and take expectations:

\[
\sum_{i=1}^m E[1-|X_i|]\ge1.
\]

All marginals are `F`, so necessarily

\[
\boxed{
 m\,E[1-|X|]\ge1.
}
\tag{1}
\]

This is a general necessary condition for odd complete mixability of symmetric laws supported on `[-1,1]`.

### 4.4 Apply (1) to the frozen candidate

Set `E=20001`. Under `F_*`, `|X|` has density

\[
E y^{E-1},\qquad 0<y<1.
\]

Hence

\[
E|X|=E\int_0^1y^E\,dy=\frac{E}{E+1}
=\frac{20001}{20002},
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{
E[1-|X|]=\frac1{20002}.
}
\]

If `m` is odd and `m<=20001`, then

\[
mE[1-|X|]=\frac{m}{20002}<1,
\]

contradicting (1). Thus

\[
\boxed{
F_*\text{ is not }m\text{-CM for every odd }m=3,5,\ldots,20001.
}
\tag{2}
\]

The metric therefore moves from its required first-run value `UNMEASURED` to

\[
\boxed{N_{\rm bad}(F_*)=20001.}
\]

This is genuine frozen-metric movement for one fixed source-valid law. It is **not** a refutation of Open Problem 7 because the certified bad set is still finite.

At the next odd dimension, `m=20003`, this particular necessary condition no longer obstructs:

\[
20003\,E[1-|X|]=\frac{20003}{20002}>1.
\]

No claim is made yet about whether `F_*` is `20003`-CM.

### 4.5 Even dimensions are completely mixable

Because `F_*` is symmetric, if `X~F_*` then `-X~F_*`. The pair `(X,-X)` is a 2-complete mix. Concatenating independent such pairs shows that `F_*` is `2k`-CM for every `k>=1`.

So the candidate's only possible failures are in odd dimensions.

### 4.6 Semigroup property and why dimension 20003 is decisive for this candidate

For any integrable law `F`, if `F` is `m`-CM and `k`-CM, both complete mixes have center `E[X]`. Put the two couplings on a product probability space and concatenate their coordinates. The concatenated `(m+k)`-tuple has every marginal `F` and constant sum `(m+k)E[X]`. Hence

\[
\boxed{
F\in M_m(\mu),\ F\in M_k(\mu)
\Longrightarrow F\in M_{m+k}(\mu).
}
\tag{3}
\]

Since `F_*` is 2-CM, if it is `m_0`-CM for even one odd `m_0`, then it is `(m_0+2j)`-CM for every `j>=0`.

Therefore the candidate-specific problem has a threshold structure on odd dimensions. We have proved every odd dimension through 20001 is bad. The **first unresolved odd dimension is exactly 20003**. If 20003 is CM, then every larger odd dimension is CM and this candidate is eventually CM. If 20003 is not CM, the metric moves immediately to at least 20003.

## 5. B20 — audit the claimed Yin–Zhu resolution and isolate the exact wall

A bounded literature search found a directly relevant claimed resolution that was not preserved in the original intake attempt:

Chuancun Yin and Dan Zhu, *Joint mixability of elliptical distributions and related families*, arXiv `1706.05499v4`, 11 May 2023.

Its abstract states that an open problem proposed by Wang (2015) is solved by a bimodal-symmetric distribution. In Section 2, Example 2.4 defines densities

\[
f_m(x)=C_m\frac{x^{2m}}{\sqrt{1-x^2}},\qquad -1<x<1,
\]

and then a fixed positive mixture `f=sum_{m>=1} alpha_m f_m`, followed immediately by the assertion that this distribution is not `(2n+1)`-CM for any `n>=1` and hence is a counterexample to Wang's Open Problem 7.

The visible text supplies no argument between that assertion and the start of Section 3. The immediately preceding mechanisms are Examples 2.1 and 2.2. Both mechanisms provably cannot establish infinitely many failures for one fixed absolutely continuous bounded-support law.

### 5.1 Example 2.1 cannot persist for a fixed absolutely continuous law

For a fixed law `F` supported on `[-a,a]`, Example 2.1 uses a threshold

\[
t_q=\frac{q}{q+1}a\uparrow a
\]

and requires, in the identical symmetric case,

\[
F(t_q)\le\frac{q+1}{2q+1}.
\tag{4}
\]

For an absolutely continuous law on `[-a,a]`, there is no atom at `a`, so

\[
F(t_q)\to F(a^-)=1.
\]

But

\[
\frac{q+1}{2q+1}\to\frac12.
\]

Hence (4) is false for all sufficiently large `q`. Thus Example 2.1 can certify only finitely many odd bad dimensions for any one fixed absolutely continuous bounded-support law.

### 5.2 Example 2.2 cannot persist either

Example 2.2 requires, for some `a_q>0`, a shell carrying mass at least about one half:

\[
F(a_q)-F\!\left(\frac{q}{q+1}a_q\right)
\ge\frac{q}{2q+1}.
\tag{5}
\]

Suppose the fixed absolutely continuous law has density `f in L^1([-A,A])`. For any `0<a_q<=A`, the shell interval has length

\[
a_q-\frac{q}{q+1}a_q
=\frac{a_q}{q+1}
\le\frac{A}{q+1}\to0.
\]

Absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral says that the `F`-mass of any interval whose length tends uniformly to zero also tends uniformly to zero. Thus the left side of (5) tends to zero uniformly in `a_q`, while the right side tends to `1/2`. Condition (5) is impossible for all large `q`.

### 5.3 Correct conclusion about the claimed resolution

The displayed Example 2.1 / Example 2.2 machinery does **not** justify the fixed-mixture all-odd-dimensions assertion in Example 2.4. This is an asymptotic quantifier gap in the visible proof route.

I do **not** infer that the Example 2.4 distribution is actually eventually CM, nor that the authors' final assertion is false. A different argument could exist but is not present in the visible transition from Example 2.4 to Section 3. Therefore the correct status for this task is not `KNOWN` and not `REFUTED`; it remains a source-level issue requiring either a valid later proof or a new argument.

This also sharpens the old water-filling obstruction: any one-shell endpoint argument for a fixed absolutely continuous law is necessarily finite-dimensional. A genuine negative solution needs a global/multiscale invariant that survives absolute continuity as `n -> infinity`.

## 7. B29 — hierarchical-gap route and why the naive version collapses

I also tried the requested hierarchical-support route: construct a fixed absolutely continuous law on nested two-child interval systems with infinitely many scales, and at scale `j` force a discrete digit/count obstruction for a growing dimension `n_j`.

The simplest unique-decoding implementation needs the level-`j` gap to dominate the maximum possible contribution of all finer scales in an `n_j`-term sum. In a nested interval model this asks schematically for

\[
\text{gap}_j \gg n_j\,\text{(residual width below level }j).
\]

If `n_j -> infinity` along infinitely many levels, enforcing this by strong separation forces the child contraction ratios to become very small at infinitely many scales. In the naive construction the product of retained length fractions then vanishes, and the limit support has Lebesgue measure zero. The resulting law is singular, hence invalid for Wang's absolutely continuous target.

This does not rule out every hierarchical construction. It rules out the straightforward exact-digit/zero-carry architecture. The obstruction agrees with Section 5: absolute continuity prevents a fixed law from concentrating order-one mass in arbitrarily thin endpoint shells, so a successful negative construction must preserve thickness while using a more global constraint than unique digit decoding.
