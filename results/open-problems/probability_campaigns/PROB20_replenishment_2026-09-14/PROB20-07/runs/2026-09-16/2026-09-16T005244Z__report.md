# 3. Geometry forced by the coefficient floor

Let

\[
\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb R^3:x_i\ge0,\ x_1+x_2+x_3=1\}.
\]

If

\[
P_{ij,k}\ge a>0,
\]

then for every `x in Delta`,

\[
V_k(x)
=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij,k}x_ix_j
\ge a\sum_{i,j}x_ix_j
=a.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{V(\Delta)\subseteq \Delta_a:=\{x\in\Delta:x_i\ge a\}.}
\tag{3.1}
\]

In particular, for `a=1/12`, every fixed point lies in the compact set `Delta_{1/12}` strictly inside the relative interior of the simplex. There are no boundary fixed points.

Brouwer's theorem already gives existence of at least one fixed point. The issue is uniqueness.

# 4. Degree of `I-V` is one

Work in the affine plane

\[
H:=\{x_1+x_2+x_3=1\}\cong\mathbb R^2
\]

and let `Omega=relint(Delta)`.

Choose the barycentre

\[
c=(1/3,1/3,1/3).
\]

For `s in [0,1]`, define the homotopy

\[
V_s(x):=(1-s)V(x)+sc,
\qquad
F_s(x):=x-V_s(x).
\tag{4.1}
\]

Because both `V(x)` and `c` are in the relative interior for every `x in Delta`, every `V_s(x)` is in the relative interior. Hence if `x in partial Delta`, then

\[
x\ne V_s(x),
\]

so

\[
0\notin F_s(\partial\Delta)
\qquad\forall s\in[0,1].
\tag{4.2}
\]

The Brouwer degree is therefore well defined and homotopy invariant:

\[
\deg(F_0,\Omega,0)=\deg(F_1,\Omega,0).
\tag{4.3}
\]

But

\[
F_1(x)=x-c
\]

has exactly one zero, at `c`, and its derivative on the affine plane is the identity. Thus

\[
\boxed{\deg(I-V,\Omega,0)=1.}
\tag{4.4}
\]

The only degree facts used below are the standard finite-dimensional properties visible in this argument: homotopy invariance when no zero crosses the boundary, and for an isolated nondegenerate zero `p`, local degree

\[
\operatorname{ind}_p(I-V)=\operatorname{sgn}\det(I-DV(p)|_{T\Delta}).
\tag{4.5}
\]

# 5. Every non-exceptional fixed point has local index `+1`

Let `p` be a fixed point of a QSO satisfying

\[
P_{ij,k}\ge\frac1{12}.
\]

Let

\[
M:=DV(p)|_{T_p\Delta}
\]

be the real `2x2` tangent derivative.

From the archived positive-real spectral rigidity theorem, either:

1. the QSO is the single exceptional endpoint tensor described in Section 6 below; or
2. **no real eigenvalue of `M` is `>=1`.**

Assume for the moment that we are in case 2.

First, `1` is not an eigenvalue, so

\[
\det(I-M)\ne0.
\]

Hence every fixed point is nondegenerate and isolated.

Second, its local index is positive. There are only two spectral possibilities for a real `2x2` matrix.

### Real spectrum

If the tangent eigenvalues are real, say `lambda_1,lambda_2`, the rigidity theorem gives

\[
\lambda_1<1,\qquad\lambda_2<1.
\]

No lower bound is needed here. Therefore

\[
\det(I-M)
=(1-\lambda_1)(1-\lambda_2)>0.
\tag{5.1}
\]

### Nonreal spectrum

If the tangent eigenvalues are a conjugate pair `lambda,bar(lambda)`, then

\[
\det(I-M)
=(1-\lambda)(1-\bar\lambda)
=|1-\lambda|^2>0.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Thus every fixed point of a non-exceptional endpoint-floor QSO has

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{ind}_p(I-V)=+1.}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Because all fixed points lie in the compact interior set `Delta_{1/12}` and each is isolated, there can be only finitely many: an infinite family would have an accumulation point in `Delta_{1/12}`, which by continuity would be a fixed point and hence would fail to be isolated.

Let the fixed points be `p_1,...,p_N`. Additivity of degree gives

\[
1
=\deg(I-V,\Omega,0)
=\sum_{j=1}^N\operatorname{ind}_{p_j}(I-V)
=N.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Therefore every non-exceptional QSO with coefficient floor at least `1/12` has exactly one fixed point.

Notice an important feature of this proof: **the unresolved off-centre complex-modulus question is irrelevant to uniqueness.** A complex conjugate tangent pair contributes positive fixed-point index whether its modulus is below or above one. Only a real eigenvalue crossing `+1` can flip the local degree in dimension two, and that is exactly the event already rigidly classified in the `170307Z` checkpoint.

# 6. The single exceptional weak-endpoint tensor

The positive-real rigidity theorem says the only QSO at floor `>=1/12` for which a fixed point can have a real tangent eigenvalue `mu>=1` is, up to simultaneous relabeling, the tensor

\[
P_{11}=P_{12}=\left(\frac56,\frac1{12},\frac1{12}\right),
\]

\[
P_{13}=P_{22}=\left(\frac1{12},\frac56,\frac1{12}\right),
\]

\[
P_{23}=P_{33}=\left(\frac1{12},\frac1{12},\frac56\right).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Its barycentre is fixed and has tangent multiplier `+1`. The degree argument above cannot count this degenerate zero directly, so this tensor must be checked separately.

Write

\[
a=\frac1{12},\qquad b=\frac34.
\]

On the simplex the map is

\[
\begin{aligned}
V_1(x)&=a+b(x_1^2+2x_1x_2),\\
V_2(x)&=a+b(2x_1x_3+x_2^2),\\
V_3(x)&=a+b(2x_2x_3+x_3^2).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Set

\[
x=x_1,\qquad y=x_2,\qquad x_3=1-x-y.
\]

The first two fixed-point equations generate, by exact polynomial elimination, the lexicographic Groebner relations

\[
y+\frac{27}{2}x^3-12x^2+\frac72x-\frac23=0,
\tag{6.3}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{(3x-1)^3(9x+1)=0.}
\tag{6.4}
\]

The only nonnegative possibility is

\[
x=\frac13.
\]

Equation (6.3) then gives

\[
y=\frac13,
\]

and hence

\[
x_3=\frac13.
\]

(The other algebraic solution has `x=-1/9`, `y=11/9`, `x_3=-1/9` and lies outside the simplex.)

Therefore the exceptional tensor also has exactly one simplex fixed point:

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{Fix}(V)=\{(1/3,1/3,1/3)\}.}
\tag{6.5}
\]

This completes the weak-endpoint uniqueness theorem.

# 9. New structural direction: degree for iterates

The same topological setup gives a useful organizing identity for future attacks.

For every integer `n>=1`, the iterate `V^n` also maps the simplex into its relative interior. Therefore

\[
\boxed{\deg(I-V^n,\Omega,0)=1.}
\tag{9.1}
\]

At the unique fixed point `p_*`, under the strict floor `>1/12`, the archived real-spectrum results imply that every real tangent eigenvalue `lambda` of `DV(p_*)` lies in `(-1,1)`. Hence for every `n`,

- if the tangent spectrum is real, `(1-lambda_1^n)(1-lambda_2^n)>0`;
- if it is nonreal, `det(I-DV(p_*)^n)=|1-lambda^n|^2>0` unless an exact root-of-unity resonance occurs.

Thus, away from a possible complex root-of-unity resonance, the unique fixed point contributes local index `+1` to every iterate. Equation (9.1) then says that all nontrivial periodic points of periods dividing `n` must have total index zero.

This does **not** itself exclude higher cycles because different periodic orbits may carry canceling indices. It does, however, identify a concrete higher-period route: prove a positive-index theorem for periodic points of `V^n` under the `1/12` floor. Such a theorem, combined with (9.1), would eliminate all periodic orbits at once.

A second route remains the adapted-metric / Lyapunov program. Earlier checkpoints show that raw one-step and raw two-step Fisher contraction are too weak at `1/12`, so any global proof must exploit more of the three-type geometry, a fixed-point-centered metric, or a genuine invariant-region argument.
