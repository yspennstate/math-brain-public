# 3. Setup: fixed point, score coordinates, and the residual child

Fix

\[
0<a<\frac13,
\qquad b:=1-3a>0,
\]

and suppose every heredity coefficient satisfies

\[
P_{ij,k}\ge a.
\]

Write the rowwise floor decomposition

\[
P_{ij,\bullet}=a(1,1,1)+bQ_{ij,\bullet},
\]

where every residual row `Q_{ij,bullet}` is a probability vector.

Let `x` be a fixed point and let `h` be a tangent eigenvector:

\[
V(x)=x,
\qquad
DV(x)h=\mu h,
\qquad
\sum_i h_i=0.
\]

Use score coordinates

\[
h_i=x_i f_i,
\qquad
\sum_i x_i f_i=0.
\]

Let `I,J` be iid with law `x`, let `K` be the residual child generated by `Q`, and put

\[
S:=f(I)+f(J).
\]

At a fixed point the residual child marginal is

\[
q_k=\frac{x_k-a}{b}.
\]

The derivative eigenvalue equation gives the exact conditional-expectation identity

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E[S\mid K=k]
=
\frac{\mu x_k}{x_k-a}f_k.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

This is the same mechanism underlying the convex-order theorem in the previous checkpoint; for the present theorem only Jensen's inequality for two explicitly chosen convex functions is needed.

# 5. Normal form for the score

Because one score coordinate is zero and the score has `x`-mean zero, the other two scores have opposite signs. Multiplying the eigenvector by `-1` if necessary and relabeling the two nonzero-score states, write

\[
x=(p,q,r),
\qquad
f=(-1,0,w),
\qquad
0<w\le1,
\]

where the normalization `w<=1` is achieved by choosing the negative-score state to have no larger probability than the positive-score state.

The mean-zero condition gives

\[
-p+rw=0,
\qquad
\boxed{w=\frac pr},
\qquad
p\le r.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Write

\[
\mu=-\rho,
\qquad
\rho\ge1.
\]

By (3.1), the conditional expectation `R:=E[S|K]` takes the values

\[
R_1=\rho\frac{p}{p-a}>0,
\qquad
R_2=0,
\qquad
R_3=-\rho\frac{p}{r-a}<0,
\tag{5.2}
\]

with residual-child probabilities

\[
\pi_1=\frac{p-a}{b},
\qquad
\pi_2=\frac{q-a}{b},
\qquad
\pi_3=\frac{r-a}{b}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

If a nonzero-score coordinate were equal to `a`, the corresponding residual marginal would be zero; positivity of all parent masses would then force the entire residual output column to vanish, contradicting a nonzero eigenvalue on that coordinate. Hence `p>a` and `r>a`. Under the strict coefficient hypothesis relevant to the original target, all fixed-point coordinates are in fact strictly larger than `a`.

Because `R=E[S|K]`, Jensen implies for every real threshold `t`

\[
\mathbb E(R-t)_+\le\mathbb E(S-t)_+.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Only `t=0` and `t=w` are needed.

# 6. First call inequality: `t=0`

For `f=(-1,0,w)` with `0<w<=1`, the only positive values of

\[
S=f(I)+f(J)
\]

are

- `w` from parent pairs `(2,3)` and `(3,2)`, total probability `2qr`;
- `2w` from `(3,3)`, probability `r^2`.

The mixed pair `(1,3)` has value `w-1<=0`.

Therefore, using `rw=p`,

\[
\mathbb E S_+
=2qrw+2r^2w
=2rw(q+r)
=\boxed{2p(1-p)}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

The positive part of `R` comes only from state 1, so

\[
\mathbb E R_+
=\frac{p-a}{b}\,\rho\frac{p}{p-a}
=\boxed{\frac{\rho p}{b}}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Applying (5.4) at `t=0` and cancelling `p>0` gives

\[
\frac\rho b\le2(1-p).
\]

Since `rho>=1`,

\[
\boxed{
p\le c(a):=1-\frac1{2b}
=\frac{1-6a}{2(1-3a)}.
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

If `a>=1/6`, then `b<=1/2` and the right side is nonpositive, impossible because `p>0`. Hence any possible case already satisfies

\[
a<\frac16.
\tag{6.4}
\]

# 7. Second call inequality: `t=w`

At threshold `t=w`, the source variable `S` contributes only the `(3,3)` atom, since the `(2,3)` atom equals the threshold exactly. Thus

\[
\mathbb E(S-w)_+
=r^2(2w-w)
=r^2w
=\boxed{pr}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

For the target variable `R`, only `R_1` can exceed `w`. Indeed

\[
\rho\frac p{p-a}>\frac pr=w,
\]

because `rho>=1` and `r>=p>p-a`.

Hence

\[
\mathbb E(R-w)_+
=\frac{p-a}{b}
\left(\rho\frac p{p-a}-\frac pr\right)
=
\frac{p(\rho r-p+a)}{rb}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

The call inequality (5.4) yields

\[
\frac{p(\rho r-p+a)}{rb}\le pr.
\]

After cancellation,

\[
\rho r-p+a\le br^2.
\]

Since `rho>=1`,

\[
\boxed{
p\ge L_a(r):=r+a-br^2.}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Because `p<=r`, (7.3) also forces

\[
a\le br^2,
\qquad
\boxed{r\ge r_0(a):=\sqrt{\frac ab}.}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Finally, fixed-point coordinates are at least `a`, and the two nonzero-score coordinates are strictly above `a`; in particular

\[
r<1-2a.
\tag{7.5}
\]

Thus every putative eigenpair lies in

\[
r\in[r_0(a),1-2a).
\tag{7.6}
\]

# 8. The `1/12` contradiction

Assume first

\[
\frac1{12}<a<\frac16.
\]

The function

\[
L_a(r)=r+a-(1-3a)r^2
\]

is strictly concave in `r`, so its minimum on the closed interval

\[
[r_0(a),1-2a]
\]

occurs at an endpoint.

At the left endpoint, because `br_0^2=a`,

\[
L_a(r_0)=r_0.
\]

Moreover

\[
r_0^2-c(a)^2
=
-\frac{(4a-1)(12a-1)}{4(3a-1)^2}>0
\]

throughout `(1/12,1/6)`, and both quantities are positive there. Hence

\[
L_a(r_0)=r_0>c(a).
\tag{8.1}
\]

At the right endpoint,

\[
L_a(1-2a)-c(a)
=
\frac{N(a)}{2(3a-1)},
\]

where

\[
N(a)=72a^4-120a^3+68a^2-18a+1.
\tag{8.2}
\]

On `[1/12,1/6]`,

\[
N'(a)=2H(a),
\qquad
H(a)=144a^3-180a^2+68a-9.
\]

Also

\[
H'(a)=4(108a^2-90a+17)>0
\]

on this interval, while

\[
H(1/6)=-2.
\]

Thus `H<0`, hence `N'<0`, throughout `[1/12,1/6]`. Since

\[
N(1/12)=-\frac3{32}<0,
\]

we have `N(a)<0`; because `3a-1<0`, it follows that

\[
L_a(1-2a)>c(a).
\tag{8.3}
\]

By concavity, (8.1) and (8.3) imply

\[
L_a(r)>c(a)
\qquad
\text{for every }r\in[r_0(a),1-2a].
\tag{8.4}
\]

But the two call inequalities gave simultaneously

\[
p\le c(a)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
p\ge L_a(r),
\]

contradicting (8.4).

Therefore

\[
\boxed{a>1/12\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{no real eigenvalue }\mu\le-1\text{ in a two-coordinate tangent direction}.}
\]

# 9. Endpoint equality `a=1/12`

At

\[
a=\frac1{12},
\qquad
b=\frac34,
\]

we have

\[
c(a)=\frac13,
\qquad
r_0(a)=\frac13.
\]

The first call inequality gives

\[
p\le1-\frac{\rho}{2b}
=1-\frac{2\rho}{3}
\le\frac13,
\tag{9.1}
\]

with equality possible only when `rho=1`.

For the second inequality,

\[
L_{1/12}(r)-\frac13
=
\frac{(3r-1)(1-r)}4.
\tag{9.2}
\]

On the feasible interval `r in [1/3,5/6)`, the right side is nonnegative and vanishes only at `r=1/3`. Thus

\[
p\ge L_{1/12}(r)\ge\frac13.
\tag{9.3}
\]

Combining (9.1)–(9.3) forces

\[
\rho=1,
\qquad
p=r=\frac13,
\qquad
q=\frac13,
\qquad
w=1.
\]

Therefore, up to label permutation and eigenvector scaling,

\[
\boxed{
x=u=(1/3,1/3,1/3),\quad \mu=-1,\quad f=(-1,0,1).}
\]

The earlier barycentric equality theorem then forces precisely the endpoint tensor displayed in Theorem 4.1.

This establishes **endpoint rigidity for every fixed point whose unstable negative tangent direction is supported on two coordinates**, not only for a barycentre assumed in advance.

# 10. Interpretation and relation to the known lower family

The explicit lower-bound family has a transposition 2-cycle approaching the barycentre as its minimum coefficient tends to `1/12` from below. Its infinitesimal flip direction is exactly a two-coordinate direction with score proportional to `(1,0,-1)` after relabeling.

Together with the previously archived finite-amplitude transposition-cycle theorem, both the **finite transposition cycle** and its **local two-coordinate period-doubling mechanism** terminate sharply at `1/12`.

The remaining local difficulty is therefore genuinely the all-three-coordinate score case.
