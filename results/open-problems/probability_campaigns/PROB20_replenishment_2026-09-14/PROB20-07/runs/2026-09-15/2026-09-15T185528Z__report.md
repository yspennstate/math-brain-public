# PROB20-07 — Sharp three-type quadratic-stochastic regularity threshold

**SOLUTION STATUS: PARTIAL**

The exact unrestricted threshold remains open. The cumulative rigorous interval is still

\[
\boxed{\frac1{12}\le \alpha_3\le\frac19}.
\]

This iteration proves a new exact finite-amplitude theorem that strictly strengthens the previously archived coordinate-transposition result:

> **Any nontrivial period-two orbit whose two states share even one coordinate is impossible once all heredity coefficients are at least `1/12`.**

Equivalently, under coefficient floor `>=1/12`, every hypothetical nontrivial 2-cycle must change **all three** coordinates. Since coordinate differences sum to zero, any remaining 2-cycle must have one coordinate moving in one direction and the other two moving in the opposite direction, with no zero difference.

The theorem is proved directly from two stop-loss inequalities in the exact relative-majorization formulation of prescribed 2-cycles. It does **not** assume permutation equivariance, and it applies to arbitrary amplitudes and arbitrary midpoints. Thus it subsumes the earlier transposition-cycle barrier as a special case.

This still does not exclude a fully three-coordinate 2-cycle, higher periodic or aperiodic recurrent sets, or prove unique-fixed-point global convergence.

# 3. Exact channel formulation needed for the proof

Fix a coefficient floor `a<1/3` and write

\[
b=1-3a.
\]

If every heredity coefficient satisfies `P_{ij,k}>=a`, write

\[
P_{ij,\bullet}=a(1,1,1)+bQ_{ij,\bullet},
\]

where each `Q_{ij,bullet}` is a probability vector and `Q_{ij}=Q_{ji}`.

For a state `z=(z_1,z_2,z_3)`, let

\[
U(z)=
(z_1^2,\,2z_1z_2,\,2z_1z_3,\,z_2^2,\,2z_2z_3,\,z_3^2)
\]

be the law of the unordered iid parent pair. If `x,y` form a 2-cycle,

\[
V(x)=y,\qquad V(y)=x,
\]

then the same residual stochastic channel from unordered parent pairs to child types satisfies

\[
U(x)Q=\frac{y-a\mathbf1}{b}=:A,
\qquad
U(y)Q=\frac{x-a\mathbf1}{b}=:B.
\tag{3.1}
\]

For every `lambda>=0`, channel monotonicity of positive parts gives the necessary stop-loss inequality

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{k=1}^3(B_k-\lambda A_k)_+
\le
\sum_{r=1}^6(U_r(y)-\lambda U_r(x))_+.
}
\tag{3.2}
\]

Indeed, for each output child `k`,

\[
B_k-\lambda A_k
=
\sum_rQ_{r,k}(U_r(y)-\lambda U_r(x)),
\]

and `(sum q_r z_r)_+ <= sum q_r(z_r)_+` for nonnegative `q_r`; summing over `k` and using row stochasticity proves (3.2).

The proof below needs only two choices of `lambda`.

# 4. Main theorem: no nontrivial 2-cycle with one unchanged coordinate

## Theorem

Let `V` be a three-type QSO with symmetric heredity coefficients satisfying

\[
P_{ij,k}\ge a\qquad\forall i,j,k
\]

for some

\[
a\ge\frac1{12}.
\]

Then there do not exist distinct states `x,y` with

\[
V(x)=y,\qquad V(y)=x
\]

such that `x_k=y_k` for at least one coordinate `k`.

In particular every QSO satisfying the strict target hypothesis `P_{ij,k}>1/12` has no such 2-cycle.

## Proof

Relabel coordinates so that the unchanged coordinate is the third. Since the two changed coordinate differences sum to zero, write the midpoint-amplitude parameterization

\[
x=(p+d,\ q-d,\ r),
\qquad
y=(p-d,\ q+d,\ r),
\tag{4.1}
\]

where

\[
p+q+r=1,
\qquad d>0.
\]

Every cycle point is an output of `V`, so every coordinate is at least the coefficient floor; in particular all displayed coordinates are nonnegative and `r>=a`.

By exchanging the first two coordinate labels and simultaneously reversing the phase of the 2-cycle if necessary, we may assume

\[
\boxed{p\ge q.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Put again

\[
b=1-3a.
\]

### Step 1: the `lambda=1` stop-loss inequality

At `lambda=1`, the target side of (3.2) is

\[
\sum_k(B_k-A_k)_+
=
\frac1b\sum_k(x_k-y_k)_+
=
\frac{2d}{b}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

On the source side, under `p>=q`, the positive changes in the unordered-pair probabilities are exactly the `12`, `22`, and `23` entries:

\[
U_{12}(y)-U_{12}(x)=4d(p-q),
\]

\[
U_{22}(y)-U_{22}(x)=4qd,
\]

\[
U_{23}(y)-U_{23}(x)=4rd.
\]

Therefore

\[
\sum_r(U_r(y)-U_r(x))_+
=4d[(p-q)+q+r]
=4d(1-q).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Combining (3.2), (4.3), and (4.4), and cancelling `2d>0`,

\[
\frac1b\le2(1-q).
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
q\le c(a):=1-\frac1{2b}
=\frac{1-6a}{2(1-3a)}.
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

If `a>=1/6`, then `b<=1/2` and `c(a)<=0`, contradicting `q>0`. Therefore any possible case must already have

\[
\frac1{12}\le a<\frac16.
\tag{4.6}
\]

### Step 2: a second exact stop-loss inequality

Set

\[
u:=\frac{p-d}{p+d}=\frac{y_1}{x_1}\in(0,1).
\tag{4.7}
\]

We evaluate (3.2) at `lambda=u`.

For the source unordered-parent distributions, direct factorization gives

\[
U_{11}(y)-uU_{11}(x)=-2d(p-d)<0,
\]

\[
U_{13}(y)-uU_{13}(x)=0,
\]

while the `12`, `22`, `23`, and `33` terms are nonnegative (strictly positive except for degenerate excluded boundary coincidences). Hence

\[
\sum_r(U_r(y)-uU_r(x))_+
=
\frac{2d(1+p^2-d^2)}{p+d}.
\tag{4.8}
\]

On the target side, the three unscaled differences have signs

\[
(x_1-a)-u(y_1-a)>0,
\]

\[
(x_2-a)-u(y_2-a)
=
\frac{-2d(a+p-q)}{p+d}<0,
\]

and

\[
(r-a)-u(r-a)=(1-u)(r-a)\ge0.
\]

Thus only states `1` and `3` contribute to the positive part. Exact simplification yields

\[
\sum_k(B_k-uA_k)_+
=
\frac{2d(1+p-q-2a)}{b(p+d)}.
\tag{4.9}
\]

Subtracting (4.9) from (4.8), the stop-loss slack is exactly

\[
\boxed{
\frac{2d}{b(p+d)}
\left[
 b(p^2-d^2)-(p-q+a)
\right].
}
\tag{4.10}
\]

Therefore the channel necessity (3.2) forces

\[
\boxed{
b(p^2-d^2)\ge p-q+a.}
\tag{4.11}
\]

We now show that (4.11) is incompatible with (4.2), (4.5), and `a>=1/12`.

# 5. The scalar contradiction

Because `p>=q` and (4.5) holds,

\[
q\le\min\{p,c(a)\}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The left-minus-right side of (4.11) is

\[
F:=b(p^2-d^2)-p+q-a.
\tag{5.2}
\]

We prove `F<0`.

## Case A: `p<=c(a)`

Then `q<=p`, so

\[
F
\le
bp^2-a-bd^2
\le
bc(a)^2-a-bd^2.
\tag{5.3}
\]

The endpoint expression factors exactly as

\[
\boxed{
bc(a)^2-a
=-\frac{(4a-1)(12a-1)}{4(3a-1)}.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

For `1/12<=a<1/6`, the right-hand side is nonpositive. Since `d>0` and `b>0`, (5.3) is in fact strict:

\[
F<0.
\]

This contradicts (4.11).

## Case B: `p>=c(a)`

Now `q<=c(a)`, so

\[
F
\le
E_a(p)-bd^2,
\qquad
E_a(p):=bp^2-p+c(a)-a.
\tag{5.5}
\]

The function `E_a(p)` is convex in `p`. On the interval `[c(a),1]`, its endpoint values are

\[
E_a(c(a))=bc(a)^2-a\le0
\tag{5.6}
\]

by (5.4), and

\[
E_a(1)=c(a)-4a
=-\frac{(2a-1)(12a-1)}{2(3a-1)}\le0.
\tag{5.7}
\]

A convex function lies below the chord joining its endpoint values, so

\[
E_a(p)\le0
\qquad(c(a)\le p\le1).
\]

Again `d>0` makes (5.5) strict:

\[
F<0,
\]

contradicting (4.11).

Both cases are impossible. This proves the theorem.

# 6. Consequences and relation to the previous transposition theorem

The earlier recovered transposition result assumed the two cycle states were related by swapping two coordinates. Such a cycle necessarily has one unchanged coordinate, so the theorem above strictly contains that result.

The new statement needs no equivariance and no relation between the two changed midpoint coordinates. In particular, it excludes every finite-amplitude two-cycle of the form

\[
(p+d,q-d,r)\longleftrightarrow(p-d,q+d,r)
\]

for arbitrary `p,q,r,d` satisfying the simplex constraints.

Thus, under the weak endpoint floor `P>=1/12`, any hypothetical nontrivial 2-cycle must satisfy

\[
\boxed{x_i\ne y_i\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,3.}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Since the coordinate differences sum to zero, after orienting the cycle one coordinate must decrease and the other two must increase (or vice versa), with all three changes nonzero.

This is the precise next finite-amplitude chamber.

The proof also exposes why `1/12` appears sharply. The first stop-loss condition produces the same scalar

\[
c(a)=1-\frac1{2(1-3a)}
\]

that arose in the earlier fixed-point negative-spectrum argument. The second stop-loss condition produces the factor

\[
12a-1
\]

in both endpoint expressions (5.4) and (5.7). At `a=1/12` both endpoint bounds become tight only when the cycle amplitude `d` vanishes; the strict `-bd^2` term rules out every nonzero-amplitude orbit.
