# 3. Fixed-point pairs as a common-channel comparison

Fix a floor

\[
0<a<\frac13,
\qquad
b:=1-3a,
\]

and write

\[
P_{ij,\bullet}=a(1,1,1)+bQ_{ij,\bullet},
\]

where each residual heredity row `Q_{ij,bullet}` is stochastic.

For a state `z=(z_1,z_2,z_3)`, let

\[
U(z)=
(z_1^2,2z_1z_2,2z_1z_3,z_2^2,2z_2z_3,z_3^2)
\]

be the unordered iid-parent distribution.

If `x` and `y` are **both fixed points** of the same QSO, then the same residual channel satisfies

\[
U(x)Q=\frac{x-a\mathbf1}{b},
\qquad
U(y)Q=\frac{y-a\mathbf1}{b}.
\]

Therefore positive-part data processing gives, for every `lambda>=0`,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{k=1}^3
\left(
\frac{y_k-a}{b}-\lambda\frac{x_k-a}{b}
\right)_+
\le
\sum_{r=1}^6
\bigl(U_r(y)-\lambda U_r(x)\bigr)_+.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

This is the fixed-point analogue of the exact two-cycle comparison already archived. It is a necessary condition for two prescribed points to be simultaneous fixed points of one endpoint-floor QSO.

# 4. New theorem I: no two distinct fixed points can share a coordinate

## Theorem 4.1

Let `V` be a three-type QSO satisfying

\[
P_{ij,k}\ge\frac1{12}
\qquad\forall i,j,k.
\]

If `x` and `y` are fixed points and

\[
x_k=y_k
\]

for at least one coordinate `k`, then

\[
\boxed{x=y.}
\]

In fact the proof works for every floor `a>=1/12` (with the trivial uniform case handled separately at `a=1/3`).

### Proof

Suppose `x!=y` and one coordinate is unchanged. Relabel so the third coordinate is unchanged. Since the coordinates sum to one, the first two changes are opposite. Write

\[
x=(p+d,q-d,r),
\qquad
y=(p-d,q+d,r),
\tag{4.1}
\]

with

\[
d>0,
\qquad p+q+r=1.
\]

By swapping labels `1,2` and simultaneously exchanging the names of `x,y` if necessary, we may assume

\[
\boxed{p\ge q.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Every fixed point is an output of `V`, so every coordinate is at least `a`; in particular `q-d>=a>0`.

### 4.1 The stop-loss inequality at `lambda=1`

For the target distributions `(x-a1)/b` and `(y-a1)/b`, the only positive coordinate change is coordinate 2, with mass

\[
\Sigma_{\rm tgt}(1)=\frac{2d}{b}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

For the unordered parent distributions, under `p>=q` the positive changes are exactly the `12,22,23` entries:

\[
U_{12}(y)-U_{12}(x)=4d(p-q),
\]

\[
U_{22}(y)-U_{22}(x)=4qd,
\]

\[
U_{23}(y)-U_{23}(x)=4rd.
\]

Hence

\[
\Sigma_{\rm src}(1)
=4d(p-q+q+r)
=4d(1-q).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Applying (3.1),

\[
\frac{2d}{b}\le4d(1-q).
\]

After cancelling `2d>0`,

\[
\boxed{
q\le c(a):=1-\frac1{2b}
=\frac{1-6a}{2(1-3a)}.
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

If `a>=1/6`, then `c(a)<=0`, contradicting `q>0`. Thus only

\[
\frac1{12}\le a<\frac16
\tag{4.6}
\]

needs further work.

### 4.2 The stop-loss inequality at the increasing single-coordinate ratio

Set

\[
\lambda=v:=\frac{q+d}{q-d}>1.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Among the six source likelihood ratios, only the `22` parent atom lies strictly above `v`; `23` is exactly at `v`. Thus

\[
\Sigma_{\rm src}(v)
=(q+d)^2-v(q-d)^2
=2d(q+d).
\tag{4.8}
\]

On the target side, subtracting the floor amplifies an increasing coordinate ratio:

\[
\frac{q+d-a}{q-d-a}>\frac{q+d}{q-d}=v.
\]

The other two target likelihood ratios are below `v`, so coordinate 2 is the only positive target contribution. Exact simplification gives

\[
\Sigma_{\rm tgt}(v)
=
\frac{(q+d-a)-v(q-d-a)}{b}
=
\frac{2ad}{b(q-d)}.
\tag{4.9}
\]

Data processing now yields

\[
\frac{2ad}{b(q-d)}\le2d(q+d),
\]

hence

\[
\boxed{
q^2-d^2\ge\frac ab.
}
\tag{4.10}
\]

But the square of the upper bound in (4.5) satisfies the exact identity

\[
\boxed{
c(a)^2-\frac ab
=
\frac{(4a-1)(12a-1)}{4(3a-1)^2}.
}
\tag{4.11}
\]

For

\[
\frac1{12}\le a<\frac16,
\]

we have `4a-1<0` and `12a-1>=0`, so

\[
c(a)^2\le\frac ab.
\tag{4.12}
\]

Combining `q<=c(a)` with `d>0`,

\[
q^2-d^2<q^2\le c(a)^2\le\frac ab,
\]

contradicting (4.10).

This proves Theorem 4.1. At the endpoint `a=1/12`, the two scalar bounds meet exactly at `q=1/3`; the nonzero amplitude term `-d^2` makes the contradiction strict.

# 5. Sharpness of Theorem 4.1 from the archived lower family

The first checkpoint gives, for every `0<s<1/3`, a QSO `V_s` with minimum coefficient

\[
t(s)=\frac{1-9s^2}{12-27s^2}<\frac1{12},
\qquad
t(s)\uparrow\frac1{12}\quad(s\downarrow0),
\]

and the genuine 2-cycle

\[
x^+=\left(\frac13+s,\frac13-s,\frac13\right),
\qquad
x^-=\left(\frac13-s,\frac13+s,\frac13\right),
\]

\[
V_s(x^+)=x^-,
\qquad
V_s(x^-)=x^+.
\]

Let `tau` be the output permutation swapping child types `1` and `2`, and define

\[
W_s:=\tau\circ V_s.
\]

This simply permutes the child coordinates in every heredity row, so it preserves stochasticity, parent symmetry, positivity, and the minimum coefficient `t(s)`.

Since `tau x^-=x^+` and `tau x^+=x^-`,

\[
W_s(x^+)=x^+,
\qquad
W_s(x^-)=x^-.
\]

Thus `W_s` has **two distinct fixed points sharing the third coordinate**. For every `alpha<1/12`, choose `s` small enough that `t(s)>alpha`; then all coefficients of `W_s` are strictly `>alpha`.

Therefore the `1/12` endpoint in Theorem 4.1 is sharp for the same-coordinate fixed-point property.

# 6. New theorem II: exclude the all-coordinate chamber `r_1 r_3 <= 1`

Now suppose `x,y` are two distinct fixed points at the weak endpoint `a=1/12`. By Theorem 4.1 they must differ in all three coordinates.

After exchanging `x,y` if necessary, exactly one coordinate decreases and two increase. Relabel so

\[
x_1>y_1,
\qquad
y_2>x_2,
\qquad
y_3>x_3,
\]

and order the increasing likelihood ratios as

\[
r_1:=\frac{y_1}{x_1}<1<r_2:=\frac{y_2}{x_2}\le r_3:=\frac{y_3}{x_3}.
\tag{6.1}
\]

Write

\[
X:=x_1>Y:=y_1,
\qquad
u:=X-Y>0,
\qquad
S:=X+Y,
\qquad
P:=XY,
\qquad
C:=1-S.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Let

\[
t:=\frac{y_2-x_2}{\nu}\in(0,1),
\]

and define exact chamber coordinates `A,B` by

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
x_2&=t(A+Y),& y_2&=t(A+X),\\
x_3&=(1-t)(B+Y),& y_3&=(1-t)(B+X).
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Mass conservation gives

\[
\boxed{tA+(1-t)B=C.}
\tag{6.4}
\]

Also

\[
r_2=\frac{A+X}{A+Y},
\qquad
r_3=\frac{B+X}{B+Y}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Because `r_2<=r_3`, the map `K -> (K+X)/(K+Y)` is decreasing, so

\[
A\ge B.
\tag{6.6}
\]

The mixed-product changes factor exactly as

\[
y_1y_2-x_1x_2=-\nu tA,
\qquad
 y_1y_3-x_1x_3=-\nu(1-t)B.
\tag{6.7}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
r_1r_2-1
=-\frac{A\nu}{X(A+Y)},
\qquad
r_1r_3-1
=-\frac{B\nu}{X(B+Y)}.
\tag{6.8}
\]

Assume toward contradiction

\[
\boxed{r_1r_3\le1.}
\tag{6.9}
\]

Then `B>=0`, hence by (6.6)

\[
A\ge B\ge0.
\tag{6.10}
\]

So both mixed parent products are nonincreasing.

## 6.1 `lambda=1` forces `S<=2/3`

At `lambda=1`, the positive source changes are exactly the `22,23,33` block. Its total is

\[
(1-Y)^2-(1-X)^2
=\nu(2-S).
\tag{6.11}
\]

The target positive mass is the total increase in coordinates `2,3`, divided by `b=3/4`:

\[
\Sigma_{\rm tgt}(1)=\frac{\nu}{b}=\frac{4\nu}{3}.
\tag{6.12}
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac43\le2-S,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{S\le\frac23.}
\tag{6.13}
\]

In particular

\[
P=XY\le\frac{S^2}{4}\le\frac19.
\tag{6.14}
\]

## 6.2 `lambda=r_1 r_2` gives an incompatible scalar inequality

Set

\[
\lambda=r_1r_2.
\]

The source atoms strictly above this likelihood ratio are `13,22,23,33` (with `13` allowed to be exactly zero in a degenerate equality case). Exact summation using (6.3)–(6.4) gives

\[
\boxed{
\Sigma_{\rm src}
=
\frac{\nu\{A(1+P)+PS\}}
{X(A+Y)}.
}
\tag{6.15}
\]

For the shifted target likelihood ratios

\[
s_k:=\frac{y_k-a}{x_k-a},
\]

coordinate 1 is inactive because subtracting a positive floor makes a decreasing ratio smaller:

\[
s_1<r_1<r_1r_2.
\]

Coordinates 2 and 3 are active because subtracting the floor makes increasing ratios larger:

\[
s_2>r_2>r_1r_2,
\qquad
s_3>r_3\ge r_2>r_1r_2.
\]

Their total unscaled positive contribution simplifies exactly to

\[
\frac{\nu\{A(1-2a)+P\}}
{X(A+Y)}.
\]

After division by `b`, data processing yields

\[
\frac{A(1-2a)+P}{b}
\le
A(1+P)+PS.
\tag{6.16}
\]

At

\[
a=\frac1{12},
\qquad b=\frac34,
\]

this is equivalent to

\[
\boxed{
A(9P-1)+P(9S-12)\ge0.
}
\tag{6.17}
\]

But (6.10), (6.13), and (6.14) give

\[
A\ge0,
\qquad 9P-1\le0,
\qquad 9S-12\le-6<0.
\]

Since `P>0`, the second term in (6.17) is strictly negative and the first is nonpositive. Thus the left side of (6.17) is strictly negative, contradiction.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{r_1r_3\le1\text{ is impossible for two distinct endpoint fixed points}.}
\tag{6.18}
\]

Any surviving pair must satisfy

\[
\boxed{r_1r_3>1.}
\tag{6.19}
\]

# 7. Localization of the remaining fixed-point uniqueness problem

The all-coordinate problem is now split into only two strict mixed-product chambers.

### Chamber F1: exactly one mixed product increases

\[
r_1r_2\le1<r_1r_3.
\]

In the exact coordinates this is

\[
A\ge0>B.
\]

At `lambda=1`, equation (6.7) gives the necessary inequality

\[
\boxed{
\frac43
\le
2-S-2(1-t)B.
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Thus the positive `13` mixed-parent increment must supply exactly the deficit left by the increasing `22,23,33` block.

### Chamber F2: both mixed products increase

\[
1<r_1r_2\le r_1r_3.
\]

Then

\[
A<0,
\qquad B\le A<0.
\]

At `lambda=1`, every source parent atom except `11` increases. Hence the source positive mass equals the decrease of the `11` atom:

\[
X^2-Y^2=\nu S.
\]

The target positive mass is `4nu/3`, so

\[
\boxed{S=X+Y\ge\frac43.}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Every fixed point coordinate is at least `1/12`, hence every single coordinate is at most `5/6`. Therefore from `X+Y>=4/3`,

\[
\boxed{X\ge\frac12,
\qquad Y\ge\frac12.}
\tag{7.3}
\]

So the two-mixed-increase chamber is confined to a very narrow region in which the decreasing type has at least one half of the mass at both fixed points.

This is a useful geometric split for the next iteration: F1 is a moderate-mass, one-mixed-sign chamber; F2 is an extreme high-mass chamber.
