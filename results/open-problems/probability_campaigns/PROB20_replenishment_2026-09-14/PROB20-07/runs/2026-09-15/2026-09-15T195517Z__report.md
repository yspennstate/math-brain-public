# 3. Endpoint channel formulation

Because the new theorem assumes `P_{ij,k}>=1/12`, it is enough to use the endpoint decomposition

\[
a:=\frac1{12},\qquad b:=1-3a=\frac34,
\]

\[
P_{ij,\bullet}=a(1,1,1)+bQ_{ij,\bullet},
\]

where every residual row `Q_{ij,bullet}` is stochastic and `Q_{ij}=Q_{ji}`.

For a state `z`, let

\[
U(z)=(z_1^2,2z_1z_2,2z_1z_3,z_2^2,2z_2z_3,z_3^2)
\]

be the unordered iid-parent law. If `x,y` form a 2-cycle, then one common stochastic residual channel satisfies

\[
U(x)Q=A:=\frac{y-a\mathbf 1}{b},
\qquad
U(y)Q=B:=\frac{x-a\mathbf 1}{b}.
\]

Hence for every `lambda>=0`,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_k(B_k-\lambda A_k)_+
\le
\sum_r(U_r(y)-\lambda U_r(x))_+.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

This is the stop-loss form of the binary relative-majorization necessity proved in the earlier checkpoint.

# 4. Likelihood-ratio chamber notation

By the preceding run, every remaining nontrivial 2-cycle changes all three coordinates. Since coordinate differences sum to zero, reverse the phase if necessary so that exactly one coordinate decreases. Relabel it as coordinate 1:

\[
x_1=X>Y=y_1.
\]

The other two coordinates increase. Relabel them so their likelihood ratios satisfy

\[
r_1:=\frac YX<1<r_2:=\frac{y_2}{x_2}\le r_3:=\frac{y_3}{x_3}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Put

\[
u:=X-Y>0,
\qquad
S:=X+Y.
\]

For the mixed unordered parent pairs define

\[
M_i:=y_1y_i-x_1x_i\qquad(i=2,3).
\tag{4.2}
\]

Then

\[
\operatorname{sgn}M_i=\operatorname{sgn}(r_1r_i-1),
\]

and a direct sum gives

\[
M_2+M_3
=Y(1-Y)-X(1-X)
=u(S-1).
\tag{4.3}
\]

There are only three source likelihood-ratio chambers:

- **A:** `r_1 r_3<=1`, so both mixed products are nonincreasing (`M_2<=0`, `M_3<=0`).
- **B:** `r_1 r_2<=1<r_1 r_3`, so exactly one mixed product increases (`M_2<=0<M_3`).
- **C:** `1<r_1 r_2`, hence both mixed products increase (`0<M_2<=M_3` in sign, not necessarily magnitude).

The rest of this report eliminates chamber C completely and the part of chamber B with `S>2/3`.

# 5. Two universal exact stop-loss formulas

## 5.1 The `lambda=1` formula

At `lambda=1`, the target positive part is exactly the total decrease of coordinate 1 after removal of the uniform floor:

\[
\sum_k(B_k-A_k)_+=\frac{u}{b}=\frac{4u}{3}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The increasing-coordinate block `22,23,33` contributes

\[
(1-Y)^2-(1-X)^2=u(2-S).
\]

The two mixed pairs contribute their positive increments `2(M_i)_+`. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\frac1b
\le
2-S+2\frac{(M_2)_++(M_3)_+}{u}.
}
\tag{5.2}
\]

At the endpoint this is

\[
\boxed{
\frac43
\le
2-S+2\frac{(M_2)_++(M_3)_+}{u}.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

## 5.2 The `lambda=r_1=Y/X` formula

Because `r_2,r_3>1`, every source pair except `11` has likelihood ratio strictly larger than `r_1`. Thus

\[
\sum_r(U_r(y)-r_1U_r(x))_+
=
\frac{u}{X}(1+XY).
\tag{5.4}
\]

The target coordinate-1 contribution is

\[
\frac{u(S-a)}{bX}.
\]

For an increasing coordinate `i=2,3`, exact algebra gives

\[
(x_i-a)-r_1(y_i-a)
=
\frac{-M_i-au}{X}.
\]

Therefore the full target call is

\[
\frac{u}{bX}
\left[
S-a+
\sum_{i=2}^3\left(-\frac{M_i}{u}-a\right)_+
\right].
\]

Combining with (5.4) yields the second universal necessary inequality

\[
\boxed{
S-a+
\sum_{i=2}^3\left(-\frac{M_i}{u}-a\right)_+
\le
b(1+XY).
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

At `a=1/12`, `b=3/4`, this becomes

\[
\boxed{
S-\frac1{12}+
\sum_{i=2}^3\left(-\frac{M_i}{u}-\frac1{12}\right)_+
\le
\frac34(1+XY).
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

These two exact scalar inequalities are the engine of the new chamber exclusions.

# 6. New theorem I: the two-mixed-increase chamber C is impossible

Assume chamber C:

\[
1<r_1r_2\le r_1r_3.
\]

Then `M_2,M_3>0`. Hence every source unordered-pair probability except `11` increases from `x` to `y`. At `lambda=1`, the source positive part is therefore simply the loss of the `11` atom:

\[
X^2-Y^2=uS.
\]

Equation (5.1) gives

\[
\frac{u}{b}\le uS,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{S\ge\frac1b=\frac43.}
\tag{6.1}
\]

At `lambda=r_1`, both positive-part correction terms in (5.5) vanish because `M_2,M_3>0`. Thus

\[
S-a\le b(1+XY).
\tag{6.2}
\]

Since `XY<=S^2/4`, a necessary consequence is

\[
S-a\le b\left(1+\frac{S^2}{4}\right).
\tag{6.3}
\]

But every coordinate of a cycle point is at least `a`, so

\[
X,Y\le1-2a,
\qquad
S\le2(1-2a).
\tag{6.4}
\]

For completeness keep `a` symbolic and define

\[
F_a(S):=S-a-(1-3a)\left(1+\frac{S^2}{4}\right).
\]

It is concave in `S`. On the candidate interval

\[
\frac1{1-3a}\le S\le2(1-2a),
\]

its endpoint values factor as

\[
F_a\!\left(\frac1{1-3a}\right)
=
\frac{24a^2-20a+1}{4(3a-1)},
\tag{6.5}
\]

\[
F_a(2(1-2a))
=
a(2a-1)(6a-5).
\tag{6.6}
\]

For every `1/12<=a<1/3`, (6.5) is positive: its numerator has roots

\[
\frac{5\pm\sqrt{19}}{12},
\]

and is negative throughout `[1/12,1/3)`, while the denominator is negative. Equation (6.6) is also positive throughout `[1/12,1/3)`. If the candidate interval is empty there is nothing to prove; if nonempty, concavity means the minimum of `F_a` on that interval occurs at an endpoint. Hence

\[
F_a(S)>0,
\]

contradicting (6.3).

Therefore

\[
\boxed{\text{chamber C is impossible for every coefficient floor }a\ge1/12.}
\tag{6.7}
\]

In endpoint language, every remaining fully-three-coordinate two-cycle must satisfy

\[
\boxed{r_1r_2\le1.}
\tag{6.8}
\]

This is a genuine finite-amplitude exclusion, not a local stability statement.

# 7. New theorem II: chamber B forces `X+Y<=2/3`

Assume chamber B:

\[
r_1r_2\le1<r_1r_3,
\]

so

\[
M_2\le0<M_3.
\]

Define

\[
m:=-\frac{M_2}{u}\ge0.
\tag{7.1}
\]

From (4.3),

\[
\frac{M_3}{u}=S-1+m.
\tag{7.2}
\]

The `lambda=1` inequality (5.3) now becomes

\[
\frac43
\le
2-S+2(S-1+m)
=S+2m.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{m\ge\frac{4/3-S}{2}.}
\tag{7.3}
\]

The `lambda=r_1` inequality (5.6) reduces to

\[
\boxed{
S-\frac1{12}+\left(m-\frac1{12}\right)_+
\le
\frac34(1+XY).
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

We prove that `S>2/3` is impossible.

## 7.1 Range `2/3<S<7/6`

In this range, (7.3) gives

\[
m>\frac1{12}.
\]

Therefore (7.4), (7.3), and `XY<=S^2/4` imply

\[
\frac S2+\frac12
\le
\frac34\left(1+\frac{S^2}{4}\right).
\tag{7.5}
\]

But the left-minus-right side factors exactly as

\[
\frac S2+\frac12-rac34\left(1+\frac{S^2}{4}\right)
=
-\frac{(3S-2)(S-2)}{16}.
\tag{7.6}
\]

For `2/3<S<7/6<2`, both factors have opposite signs, so (7.6) is strictly positive. This contradicts (7.5).

## 7.2 Range `S>=7/6`

Dropping the nonnegative correction in (7.4) gives the weaker necessary inequality

\[
S-\frac1{12}
\le
\frac34\left(1+\frac{S^2}{4}\right).
\tag{7.7}
\]

The left-minus-right side is

\[
-\frac{9S^2-48S+40}{48},
\]

whose roots are

\[
\frac{8\pm2\sqrt6}{3}.
\]

The smaller root is approximately `1.03367`, strictly below `7/6`, while the larger root is above the simplex-relevant range. Hence the left-minus-right side is positive for every

\[
\frac76\le S\le\frac53
\]

(the upper bound `5/3` follows from `X,Y<=5/6`). This contradicts (7.7).

Thus

\[
\boxed{\text{chamber B implies }X+Y\le\frac23.}
\tag{7.8}
\]
