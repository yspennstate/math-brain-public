# 3. Endpoint residual representation and convex-order necessity

It is enough to prove rigidity at the weak endpoint floor

\[
a:=\frac1{12},\qquad b:=1-3a=\frac34.
\]

Indeed every QSO with strict floor `>1/12` also satisfies the weak endpoint floor.

Write

\[
P_{ij,\bullet}=a(1,1,1)+bQ_{ij,\bullet},
\]

where every residual row `Q_{ij,bullet}` is a probability vector and `Q_{ij}=Q_{ji}`.

Let `x` be a fixed point and let `h` be a nonzero tangent eigenvector:

\[
V(x)=x,\qquad DV(x)h=\mu h,\qquad \sum_i h_i=0.
\]

Use score coordinates

\[
h_i=x_if_i,\qquad \sum_i x_if_i=0.
\]

Every fixed-point coordinate satisfies `x_i>=a`, since every coordinate of `V(z)` is at least `a`. If `x_k=a`, then equality in `V_k(x)>=a` and the positivity of every parent weight `x_ix_j` force `P_{ij,k}=a` for every parent pair. Hence the derivative in coordinate `k` vanishes. Therefore when `mu!=0`, every nonzero score `f_k` has `x_k>a`, so the denominators below are legitimate.

Let `I,J` be iid with law `x`, let `K` be the residual child generated by `Q`, and define

\[
S:=f(I)+f(J).
\]

At the fixed point, the residual child marginal is

\[
q_k=\frac{x_k-a}{b}.
\]

The derivative eigenvalue equation gives the exact conditional-mean identity

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E[S\mid K=k]
=
\frac{\mu x_k}{x_k-a}f_k.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Put

\[
R:=\mathbb E[S\mid K].
\]

Then conditional Jensen gives

\[
\boxed{R\preceq_{cx}S},
\tag{3.2}
\]

so for every real threshold `t`,

\[
\mathbb E(R-t)_+\le\mathbb E(S-t)_+,
\tag{3.3}
\]

and the same holds for reflected calls `(-z-t)_+`.

We now assume

\[
\mu=\rho\ge1
\]

and prove endpoint rigidity.

# 4. Canonical score form

A nonzero mean-zero three-state score has both signs. After multiplying by `-1` if needed, relabeling states, and scaling by a positive constant, we may write

\[
\boxed{f=(-1,v,w),\qquad 0\le v\le w.}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Write

\[
x=(p,q,r).
\]

The mean-zero condition is

\[
\boxed{p=qv+rw.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

By (3.1), the three target conditional means have the **same signs** as the score:

\[
R_1=-\rho\frac p{p-a}<0,
\]

\[
R_2=\rho\frac{qv}{q-a}\ge0,
\qquad
R_3=\rho\frac{rw}{r-a}\ge0.
\tag{4.3}
\]

We split according to whether the positive side of the score is no larger than the unique negative magnitude, `w<=1`, or exceeds it, `w>1`.

# 5. Case I: `0<=v<=w<=1`

The mixed parent sums are nonpositive:

\[
-1+v\le0,\qquad -1+w\le0.
\]

Therefore the positive part of `S` comes only from positive-positive parent pairs and has exact expectation

\[
\mathbb E S_+
=2(q+r)(qv+rw)
=\boxed{2p(1-p)}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The positive part of `R` comes from states 2 and 3. Using the residual probabilities `(x_i-a)/b`,

\[
\mathbb E R_+
=\frac\rho b(qv+rw)
=\boxed{\frac{\rho p}{b}}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Convex order at threshold zero yields

\[
\frac\rho b\le2(1-p).
\]

Since `rho>=1` and `b=3/4`,

\[
\boxed{p\le\frac13.}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Now use the reflected call

\[
\phi(z)=(-z-1)_+.
\]

For the source `S`, the only value below `-1` is the `(1,1)` parent pair, where `S=-2`. Thus

\[
\boxed{\mathbb E(-S-1)_+=p^2.}
\tag{5.4}
\]

For the target, the negative atom contributes

\[
\mathbb E(-R-1)_+
=\frac{p-a}{b}
\left(\rho\frac p{p-a}-1\right)
=\frac{(\rho-1)p+a}{b}
\ge\frac ab
=\boxed{\frac19}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Hence convex order implies

\[
p^2\ge\frac19,
\qquad
\boxed{p\ge\frac13.}
\tag{5.6}
\]

Together with (5.3),

\[
\boxed{p=\frac13.}
\tag{5.7}
\]

Equality in the zero-call inequality forces

\[
\boxed{\rho=1.}
\tag{5.8}
\]

We now use two lower-tail calls to determine the whole score and state.

Since `p=1/3`, we have

\[
q+r=\frac23,
\qquad
qv+rw=\frac13.
\tag{5.9}
\]

The target negative atom is

\[
R_1=-\frac43
\]

with residual probability `1/3`.

Take the reflected call with threshold

\[
c=1-w\in[0,1].
\]

For the source, the `(1,1)` pair contributes `(1+w)/9`, the `(1,2)` and `(2,1)` pairs contribute `(2q/3)(w-v)`, and the `(1,3)` pair is exactly at the kink. Thus

\[
\mathbb E(-S-c)_+
=\frac{1+w}{9}+\frac{2q}{3}(w-v).
\]

For the target,

\[
\mathbb E(-R-c)_+
=\frac13\left(\frac43-(1-w)\right)
=\frac19+\frac w3.
\]

Using (5.9),

\[
q(w-v)
=w(q+r)-(qv+rw)
=\frac{2w-1}{3}.
\]

Therefore the source-minus-target call difference is exactly

\[
\boxed{\frac{2(w-1)}9.}
\tag{5.10}
\]

Convex order requires it to be nonnegative, while `w<=1`, so

\[
\boxed{w=1.}
\tag{5.11}
\]

Now use the reflected call with threshold `c=1-v`. Since `w=1`, the source-minus-target difference simplifies to

\[
\boxed{-\frac{2v}{9}.}
\tag{5.12}
\]

Convex order forces

\[
\boxed{v=0.}
\tag{5.13}
\]

Finally (5.9) gives

\[
q=r=\frac13.
\]

Thus Case I forces

\[
\boxed{
x=(1/3,1/3,1/3),\qquad \mu=1,\qquad f=(-1,0,1)}
\tag{5.14}
\]

up to simultaneous relabeling and scale.

# 6. Case II: `w>1` is impossible

Positive scaling does not change the eigenvalue or convex-order feasibility, so divide the score by `w` and write

\[
\boxed{f=(-c,u,1),\qquad 0<c<1,\qquad 0\le u\le1,}
\tag{6.1}
\]

where `c=1/w` and `u=v/w`.

The mean-zero equation is

\[
\boxed{pc=qu+r=:m.}
\tag{6.2}
\]

Define the mixed-pair excess

\[
\boxed{D:=q(u-c)_+ + r(1-c).}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Because `c<1` and `r>0`,

\[
D>0.
\]

## 6.1 Zero call: a lower bound on `D`

Positive-positive parent pairs contribute

\[
2(q+r)(qu+r)=2(1-p)m,
\]

and mixed negative-positive pairs contribute `2pD`. Hence

\[
\mathbb E S_+
=2(1-p)m+2pD
=2p\{c(1-p)+D\}.
\tag{6.4}
\]

The positive target mean is

\[
\mathbb E R_+
=\frac{\rho m}{b}
=\frac{\rho pc}{b}.
\tag{6.5}
\]

Using `rho>=1` and `b=3/4`, convex order at zero gives

\[
\boxed{D\ge c\left(p-\frac13\right).}
\tag{6.6}
\]

## 6.2 Lower tail at scale `c`: `p>=1/3`

Use the reflected call

\[
(-z-c)_+.
\]

For the source, the only value below `-c` is the `(1,1)` pair, where the score sum is `-2c`, so

\[
\mathbb E(-S-c)_+=p^2c.
\tag{6.7}
\]

For the target, the negative atom contributes

\[
\frac{p-a}{b}
\left(\rho\frac{pc}{p-a}-c\right)
=
\frac{c\{(\rho-1)p+a\}}b
\ge\frac c9.
\tag{6.8}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{p\ge\frac13.}
\tag{6.9}
\]

## 6.3 Upper tail at one: `pc>=1/3`

At threshold `1`, mixed pairs involving the negative score cannot contribute, because their largest sum is `1-c<1`. For two nonnegative scores `s,t in [0,1]`,

\[
(s+t-1)_+\le st.
\]

Therefore

\[
\mathbb E(S-1)_+
\le(qu+r)^2
=m^2
=(pc)^2.
\tag{6.10}
\]

The target atom corresponding to score `1` contributes at least

\[
\frac ab=\frac19,
\]

so convex order gives

\[
\boxed{pc\ge\frac13.}
\tag{6.11}
\]

## 6.4 The mixed excess gives the reverse inequality

For `0<=u<=1` and `0<c<1`,

\[
(u-c)_+\le u(1-c).
\]

Hence

\[
D
\le(1-c)(qu+r)
=(1-c)pc.
\tag{6.12}
\]

Combining (6.6) and (6.12),

\[
c\left(p-\frac13\right)
\le(1-c)pc.
\]

After dividing by `c>0`,

\[
pc\le\frac13.
\tag{6.13}
\]

Together with (6.11),

\[
\boxed{pc=\frac13.}
\tag{6.14}
\]

Consequently every inequality used above is an equality. In particular

\[
\boxed{\rho=1,}
\tag{6.15}
\]

and equality in `(u-c)_+<=u(1-c)` with `q>0` forces

\[
\boxed{u\in\{0,1\}.}
\tag{6.16}
\]

We now eliminate both possibilities exactly.

### Subcase `u=0`

Then `m=r=1/3`, so

\[
r=\frac13,
\qquad
p=\frac1{3c}>\frac13,
\qquad
q=\frac23-p<\frac13.
\]

Use the ordinary call at threshold

\[
t=1-c.
\]

The source contribution is

\[
\frac{2qrc}{1}+r^2(1+c)
=
\frac{2qc}{3}+\frac{1+c}{9}.
\]

The target contribution from state 3 is

\[
\frac13\left(\frac43-(1-c)\right)
=
\frac19+\frac c3.
\]

Their difference is exactly

\[
\boxed{\frac{2c}{3}\left(q-\frac13\right)<0,}
\]

contradicting convex order.

### Subcase `u=1`

Then

\[
m=q+r=1-p=\frac13,
\]

so

\[
p=\frac23,
\qquad
c=\frac12.
\]

At threshold `t=1/2`, every mixed negative-positive parent sum is exactly at the kink, while every positive-positive pair has sum `2`. Hence

\[
\mathbb E(S-1/2)_+
=\frac19\cdot\frac32
=\boxed{\frac16}.
\]

For the target, summing both positive states gives exactly

\[
\mathbb E(R-1/2)_+
=
\frac{(q+r)-\frac12\{(q-a)+(r-a)\}}b
=\boxed{\frac13}.
\]

Again convex order is violated.

Thus Case II is impossible.

# 7. Endpoint equality tensor is unique

We have proved that any endpoint eigenpair with `mu>=1` must, up to labels and scaling, satisfy

\[
x=(1/3,1/3,1/3),
\qquad
\mu=1,
\qquad
f=(-1,0,1).
\]

At the barycentre the residual child law is also uniform. The source sum `S=f(I)+f(J)` has distribution

\[
-2,-1,0,1,2
\]

with probabilities

\[
1/9,\ 2/9,\ 3/9,\ 2/9,\ 1/9.
\]

Equation (3.1) gives target conditional means

\[
R=(-4/3,0,4/3)
\]

with probabilities `(1/3,1/3,1/3)`.

The lower-tail call `(-z-1)_+` is exactly saturated: both source and target expectation equal `1/9`. Equality in conditional Jensen therefore forces the conditional source distribution for target state 1 to lie entirely in the affine region `S<=-1`. The total source mass of `S<=-1` is exactly `1/3`, equal to the target-state-1 probability. Hence target state 1 receives **all and only** parent pairs with scores `-2` and `-1`, namely unordered pairs `11` and `12`.

Similarly the upper-tail call `(z-1)_+` is saturated at `1/9`, so target state 3 receives all and only source pairs with `S>=1`, namely `23` and `33`. The remaining zero-sum pairs `13` and `22` must go to target state 2.

Thus the residual rows are uniquely

\[
Q_{11}=Q_{12}=e_1,
\]

\[
Q_{13}=Q_{22}=e_2,
\]

\[
Q_{23}=Q_{33}=e_3.
\]

Restoring the uniform floor gives the unique positive endpoint tensor

\[
\boxed{
P_{11}=P_{12}=(5/6,1/12,1/12),
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
P_{13}=P_{22}=(1/12,5/6,1/12),
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
P_{23}=P_{33}=(1/12,1/12,5/6).
}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Direct differentiation at the barycentre gives the tangent eigenvalue `mu=1` in the score direction `(-1,0,1)`.

Every tensor in (7.1) has coefficients **equal** to `1/12`; hence the strict-floor corollary follows:

\[
\boxed{
P_{ij,k}>\frac1{12}\ \forall i,j,k
\Longrightarrow
\text{no real tangent eigenvalue }\mu\ge1
\text{ at any fixed point.}
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Together with the previous checkpoint's negative-spectrum theorem,

\[
\boxed{
P_{ij,k}>\frac1{12}
\Longrightarrow
|\mu|<1
\text{ for every real tangent eigenvalue at every fixed point.}
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

## CONDITIONAL / STRUCTURAL

For a fixed point `x`, define the parent-to-child Markov kernel

\[
T_{jk}=\sum_i x_iP_{ij,k}.
\]

Then `xT=x`, and tangent eigenvalues of `DV(x)` equal twice the two nontrivial eigenvalues of `T`. The present and previous iterations rule out real nontrivial eigenvalues of `T` outside `(-1/2,1/2)` under strict floor `>1/12`.

The only remaining local instability can therefore be a **nonreal conjugate pair**. If the nontrivial eigenvalues are `lambda,bar lambda`, then

\[
|\mu|^2=4|\lambda|^2=4\det T.
\]

Writing `P=a*1+bQ` and

\[
R_{jk}=\sum_i x_iQ_{ij,k},
\]

one has the exact affine-area identity

\[
\boxed{\det T=b^2\det R.}
\]

At the endpoint `b=3/4`, excluding complex tangent modulus `>=1` is therefore equivalent to proving

\[
\det R<\frac49
\]

under the fixed-point and symmetric-two-parent extension constraints. Exploratory computation described below suggests the substantially stronger sharp bound `det R<=1/3`, but this is not yet proved.

# 9. Exploratory complex-spectrum diagnostic — evidence only

At endpoint floor `a=1/12`, the 729 deterministic symmetric residual kernels (`3^6` choices on the six unordered parent pairs) were enumerated. For each residual kernel, interior fixed points of

\[
V(x)=a(1,1,1)+(3/4)W(x)

were numerically solved from a bounded grid of starts. Among the recovered fixed points, the largest observed determinant of the full one-parent kernel `T` was

\[
\det T=\frac3{16}=0.1875,

attained at the barycentre by a cyclic residual example whose nontrivial eigenvalues are

\[
\lambda=\pm i\frac{\sqrt3}{4}.
\]

Thus its tangent multipliers have modulus

\[
\frac{\sqrt3}{2}<1.
\]

A separate continuous SLSQP search over residual probability rows and the fixed-point constraint repeatedly converged to

\[
\max \det R=\frac13,
\qquad
\max \det T=\frac3{16}.
\]

This strongly suggests the next exact lemma

\[
\boxed{\det R\le\frac13}
\]

for every endpoint fixed point admitting a symmetric two-parent residual extension. The computation is diagnostic only and does not establish the universal inequality.
