## 4. Statement of the new structural theorem

For a Paley four-clique `C={a,b,c,d}` define

\[
t_4(C)
:=\#\{x\in\mathbf F_p\setminus C:\ x\text{ is adjacent to all four vertices of }C\}.
\]

### Theorem 4.1 — four-clique extension parity

Let `p ≡ 1 (mod 4)` be prime and let `C` be a four-clique in the prime-field Paley graph `G_p`. Then

\[
\boxed{
 t_4(C)\equiv
 \begin{cases}
 1\pmod 2,&p\equiv1\pmod8,\\[2mm]
 0\pmod 2,&p\equiv5\pmod8.
 \end{cases}}
\tag{4.1}
\]

In particular,

\[
\boxed{p\equiv1\pmod8\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{every Paley four-clique extends to a five-clique}.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

This is a restricted structural theorem. It does not supply a polylogarithmic upper bound on `omega(G_p)`.

The remainder of Sections 5--8 proves Theorem 4.1 completely.

## 5. B25 — count the same object twice: the five-elliptic-factor cover

Affine Paley automorphisms allow any four-clique to be normalized to

\[
C=\{0,1,c,d\}.
\]

Choose nonzero square roots in `F_p`

\[
U^2=c,\qquad V^2=1-c,\qquad
S^2=d,\qquad T^2=1-d,\qquad W^2=d-c,
\tag{5.1}
\]

and choose `i` with `i^2=-1`, which exists because `p≡1 (mod 4)`.

The four triple subsets of `C` and the full four-point branch set give five genus-one curves. After elementary Möbius normalizations, all five can be represented in Legendre form

\[
E_j:\ y^2=x(x-1)(x-u_j^2),
\]

with pairs `(u_j,v_j)` satisfying `u_j^2+v_j^2=1`:

\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 j&u_j&v_j\\ \hline
0&U&V\\
1&S&T\\
2&S/U&iW/U\\
3&T/V&W/V\\
4&iTU/W&SV/W.
\end{array}
\tag{5.2}
\]

For `j=4`, the Möbius map

\[
x\longmapsto \frac{(1-d)x}{x-d}
\]

sends `0,1,d` to `0,1,infinity` and sends `c` to

\[
\lambda_4=-\frac{c(1-d)}{d-c}=(iTU/W)^2,
\qquad
1-\lambda_4=(SV/W)^2,
\]

so the quartic factor has the stated Legendre model. Let `N_j=#E_j(F_p)`.

The multiquadratic curve

\[
X_C:\qquad y_u^2=x-u\quad(u\in C)
\tag{5.3}
\]

is a `(Z/2)^4` cover of the projective line branched at the four clique points and infinity. Riemann--Hurwitz gives genus five. This is the classical Humbert-type five-branch-point geometry: its five positive-genus quadratic quotients are precisely the five elliptic curves in (5.2).

Counting rational points directly by fibres gives

\[
\#X_C(\mathbf F_p)=16t_4(C)+40.
\tag{5.4}
\]

Indeed, each common neighbor contributes 16 affine points, each of the four finite branch points contributes 8 points because all remaining clique differences are squares, and the square leading coefficients give 8 rational points above infinity.

On the other hand, expanding the exact affine fibre count

\[
\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p}\prod_{u\in C}(1+\chi(x-u))
\]

and grouping the four cubic and one quartic character sums gives

\[
\#X_C(\mathbf F_p)=\sum_{j=0}^{4}N_j-4p-4.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Equating (5.4) and (5.5) recovers the preceding run's exact identity in the cleaner form

\[
\boxed{
 t_4(C)=\sum_{j=0}^{4}\frac{N_j}{16}-\frac{p+11}{4}.
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

Every `E_j` has full rational 4-torsion because both `u_j^2` and `v_j^2=1-u_j^2` are squares. Hence every `N_j` is divisible by 16. The unresolved issue from the previous run was exactly the parity of the first term on the right of (5.6).

## 6. B6 — rationally deform each Legendre factor to its level-4 parameter

This section resolves the 2-adic parity of one factor in a form that can be coupled across all five factors.

For a nonzero field element `z`, write

\[
\beta(z)=
\begin{cases}
0,&z\text{ is a square},\\
1,&z\text{ is a nonsquare},
\end{cases}
\]

so `chi(z)=(-1)^{beta(z)}`. All bit identities below are in `F_2`.

Let

\[
E:\ y^2=x(x-1)(x-u^2),
\qquad u^2+v^2=1,
\tag{6.1}
\]

with `u,v` nonzero. Put

\[
\epsilon:=\beta(2).
\]

Because `p≡1 (mod4)` and `i^2=-1`, quadratic reciprocity gives

\[
\beta(i)=\beta(2)=\epsilon.
\tag{6.2}
\]

Define

\[
r:=\beta(u),\qquad s:=\beta(v),\qquad d:=\beta(1+u).
\tag{6.3}
\]

Since `E[4]` is rational, `16 | #E(F_p)`. Set

\[
n(E):=\frac{\#E(\mathbf F_p)}{16}\pmod2.
\]

### Lemma 6.1 — normalized order parity of a Legendre factor

Under (6.1)--(6.3),

\[
\boxed{n(E)=(1+\epsilon)d+rs.}
\tag{6.4}
\]

### Proof

We use only explicit halving in a curve with full rational 2-torsion.

For a cubic

\[
y^2=(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)(x-\gamma),
\]

choose `R^2=alpha-beta` and `S^2=alpha-gamma`. A half of the two-torsion point `(alpha,0)` has x-coordinate `alpha+RS`. Its three root differences are

\[
RS,\qquad R(R+S),\qquad S(R+S).
\]

By the standard full-2-torsion descent criterion, this half is itself divisible by 2 exactly when all three displayed quantities are squares. Equivalently,

\[
\chi(R)=\chi(S)=\chi(R+S).
\tag{6.5}
\]

Thus a nonzero two-torsion direction admits a rational point of order 8 precisely when (6.5) holds for that root.

For the root `0`, take `R=i` and `S=iu`. Condition (6.5) is

\[
r=0,\qquad d=0.
\]

Let its indicator be `h_0=(1+r)(1+d)`.

For the root `1`, take `R=1` and `S=v`. Its order-8 condition is

\[
\beta(v)=0,\qquad \beta(1+v)=0.
\]

Introduce the rational level-4 parameter

\[
z=\frac{v}{1+u}.
\tag{6.6}
\]

Then

\[
u=\frac{1-z^2}{1+z^2},\qquad
v=\frac{2z}{1+z^2},\qquad
1+u=\frac{2}{1+z^2},\qquad
1+v=\frac{(1+z)^2}{1+z^2}.
\tag{6.7}
\]

Hence

\[
\beta(1+v)=\epsilon+d,
\]

and the indicator for the root `1` is

\[
h_1=(1+s)(1+\epsilon+d).
\]

For the root `u^2`, choose square roots `R=u` and `S=iv`. Condition (6.5) says

\[
\beta(u)=\beta(iv)=\beta(u+iv).
\]

Using (6.7),

\[
u+iv=\frac{(1+iz)^2}{1+z^2},
\]

so

\[
\beta(u+iv)=\epsilon+d,\qquad
\beta(iv)=\epsilon+s.
\]

Therefore the third order-8 indicator is

\[
h_{u^2}=(1+s+d)(1+r+\epsilon+d).
\]

The rational order-8 directions form a subgroup of `E[2]`. The number of nonzero such directions is therefore `0`, `1`, or `3`. The quotient `#E(F_p)/16` is odd exactly when there is **no** rational order-8 point. Consequently

\[
n(E)=1+h_0+h_1+h_{u^2}.
\]

Substituting the three indicators and expanding in `F_2`, using `d^2=d`, gives

\[
n(E)=d^2+\epsilon d+rs=(1+\epsilon)d+rs,
\]

which proves (6.4). QED.

This is the successful B6 move: the Legendre factor is moved to the rational `X(4)`-type parameter `z`, where the mod-32 point-count question becomes finite square-class algebra rather than a separate Hasse-bound problem.

## 7. Couple the five factors: the parity blocker vanishes identically

For the five pairs in (5.2), define

\[
r_j=\beta(u_j),\qquad s_j=\beta(v_j),\qquad d_j=\beta(1+u_j).
\]

Also abbreviate the five base square-root bits by

\[
\alpha=\beta(U),\quad
\nu=\beta(V),\quad
\sigma=\beta(S),\quad
\tau=\beta(T),\quad
\omega=\beta(W).
\]

From (5.2), direct substitution gives

\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
j&r_j&s_j\\ \hline
0&\alpha&\nu\\
1&\sigma&\tau\\
2&\sigma+\alpha&\epsilon+\omega+\alpha\\
3&\tau+\nu&\omega+\nu\\
4&\epsilon+\tau+\alpha+\omega&\sigma+\nu+\omega.
\end{array}
\tag{7.1}
\]

Expanding the five products and cancelling in `F_2` yields

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{j=0}^{4}r_js_j
=(1+\epsilon)(\alpha+\nu+\omega).
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

It remains to couple the five `d_j` bits. This is the only point where the five Legendre factors must be treated together rather than separately.

### Lemma 7.1 — five-factor square-class product

With the choices (5.1)--(5.2),

\[
\boxed{
\prod_{j=0}^{4}\chi(1+u_j)=\chi(2UVW).
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{j=0}^{4}d_j
=\epsilon+\alpha+\nu+\omega
\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_2.
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

### Proof

Set

\[
x=\frac{V}{1+U},\qquad y=\frac{T}{1+S},
\]

and

\[
R=\frac{W(1+x^2)(1+y^2)}{2}.
\]

Then

\[
U=\frac{1-x^2}{1+x^2},\quad
V=\frac{2x}{1+x^2},\quad
S=\frac{1-y^2}{1+y^2},\quad
T=\frac{2y}{1+y^2},
\]

and the identity `W^2=d-c` becomes

\[
R^2=(x^2-y^2)(1-x^2y^2).
\tag{7.5}
\]

Choose `kappa` with

\[
\kappa^2=-2i.
\]

Such `kappa` exists because

\[
\chi(-2i)=\chi(2)\chi(i)=1
\]

by (6.2). A direct algebraic simplification using (7.5) gives the exact square identity

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{4}(1+u_j)}{2UVW}
=
\left[
\frac{x^2+1}{x(x^2-1)(y^2+1)}
\frac{R-i(x-y)(1+xy)}{\kappa(x-y)}
\right]^2.
}
\tag{7.6}
\]

For completeness, the only non-obvious step in (7.6) is

\[
\frac{(1+xy)\bigl(R+i y(1-x^2)\bigr)}{x-y}
=
\frac{\bigl(R-i(x-y)(1+xy)\bigr)^2}{-2i(x-y)^2},
\tag{7.7}
\]

which follows immediately after substituting (7.5). All denominators are nonzero because the four clique points are distinct and `u_j^2` is never `0` or `1`.

Taking quadratic characters in (7.6) proves (7.3), and converting signs to bits gives (7.4). QED.

### Finish of the five-curve parity calculation

Apply Lemma 6.1 to each of the five factors:

\[
\sum_{j=0}^{4}\frac{N_j}{16}
\equiv
(1+\epsilon)\sum_jd_j+\sum_jr_js_j
\pmod2.
\]

Now use (7.2) and (7.4):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{j=0}^{4}\frac{N_j}{16}
&=(1+\epsilon)(\epsilon+\alpha+\nu+\omega)
 +(1+\epsilon)(\alpha+\nu+\omega)\\
&=\epsilon(1+\epsilon)\\
&=0
\qquad\text{in }\mathbf F_2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.8}
\]

Thus the previous run's experimental blocker is now a theorem:

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{j=0}^{4}\frac{N_j}{16}\equiv0\pmod2.
}
\tag{7.9}
\]

Finally, reduce (5.6) modulo two. If `p=8m+1`, then `(p+11)/4=2m+3` is odd; if `p=8m+5`, then `(p+11)/4=2m+4` is even. Equation (4.1) follows. QED.

## 8. B25 geometric restatement

The parity theorem has a concise genus-five formulation. From (5.4),

\[
\#X_C(\mathbf F_p)=16t_4(C)+40.
\]

Therefore Theorem 4.1 is equivalent to

\[
\boxed{
\#X_C(\mathbf F_p)\equiv
\begin{cases}
24\pmod{32},&p\equiv1\pmod8,\\
8\pmod{32},&p\equiv5\pmod8.
\end{cases}}
\tag{8.1}
\]

The five elliptic quotients explain why a four-clique creates a coupled 2-adic object: separately, each quotient only says `16 | N_j`; together, the clique square-root relations force the extra parity (7.9). This is exactly the sort of cross-factor information that separate Weil/Hasse bounds lose.

A second useful interpretation comes from Frobenius. Since every `E_j[4]` is rational, its 2-adic Frobenius satisfies

\[
F_j\equiv I\pmod4.
\]

Write modulo 8

\[
F_j=I+4A_j.
\]

Then

\[
\#E_j(\mathbf F_p)=\det(I-F_j)\equiv16\det(A_j)\pmod{32}.
\]

Hence (7.9) is exactly

\[
\boxed{\sum_{j=0}^{4}\det(A_j)=0\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_2.}
\tag{8.2}
\]

This makes the coupling target explicit at the level of mod-8 Frobenius defects.

The theorem therefore moves the **structural** frontier but not the frozen finite metric or the source verdict.
