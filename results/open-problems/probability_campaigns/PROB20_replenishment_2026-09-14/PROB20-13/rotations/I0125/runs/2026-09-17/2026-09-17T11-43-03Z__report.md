## 2. Exact source target and current status

Primary source: Henry Cohn and Isaac Rajagopal, *Variations on Five-Dimensional Sphere Packings*, Discrete & Computational Geometry (2026), DOI `10.1007/s00454-026-00841-x`, published/version of record 4 July 2026; arXiv `2412.00937` (current paper version used by the source is the March 2026 revision).

Section 2 states explicitly that the five-dimensional kissing number appears to be 40 but the best proved upper bound is 44. In spherical-code normalization,

`tau_5 = max{|C| : C subset S^4, <x,y> <= 1/2 for x != y}`.

The frozen certified interval is therefore

`40 <= tau_5 <= 44`, so `W_5=4`.

The lower endpoint is attained by the `D_5` root system, and Cohn-Rajagopal organize the currently known 40-point examples as four non-isometric configurations:

- `D_5`, the 40 roots obtained from permutations of `(+-1,+-1,0,0,0)` in squared-norm-2 coordinates;
- Leech's `L_5` (1967), obtained by replacing one 8-point layer around a `D_4` / 24-cell central layer;
- Szolloesi's `Q_5` (2023), whose central coordinate-sum-zero layer is the 20-point `A_4` root system and whose adjacent layer is reflected across that hyperplane;
- Cohn-Rajagopal's `R_5`, obtained by an analogous modification of `L_5` with a modified `A_4` central section.

Cohn-Rajagopal give symmetry-group orders `3840, 384, 240, 48` respectively and prove only that there are at least these four non-isometric 40-point configurations, not that 40 is the unrestricted kissing number.

## 3. New mathematical idea before code

The source singles out the `A_4` central section of `D_5` as the structure that leads to `Q_5`. Instead of trying to append a point to a known 40-code (already blocked by exact polar maximality), I asked whether *any* five-dimensional kissing code that contains this exact 20-point cross section can exceed 40.

The answer is no, and equality can be classified exactly.

### Theorem (A4-section rigidity)

Let

`R={ (e_i-e_j)/sqrt(2) : i != j } subset S^4`

be the 20-point `A_4` root system in the hyperplane `H={sum x_i=0}`. If a five-dimensional kissing code `C` contains an isometric copy of `R`, then

`|C| <= 40`.

If equality holds, then `C` is isometric to `D_5` or `Q_5`.

A complete proof is archived in

`probability_campaigns/PROB20_replenishment_2026-09-14/PROB20-13/rotations/I0125/artifacts/2026-09-17__a4_section_rigidity.md`

creation commit `757607b05f08e7486c85fcfb92297eb193a98819`; the artifact was read back at that exact commit.

The proof is summarized next because this is a theorem-sized claim and not a sketch.

### Step 1: every extra point has large height

Put `w=(1,1,1,1,1)/sqrt(5)` and decompose an extra point as `x=h w+y` with `y in H`. Compatibility with both signs of every `A_4` root gives

`|y_i-y_j| <= 1/sqrt(2)` for all `i,j`.                                            (1)

For five real numbers of mean zero and range `delta`, the exact odd-sample range-variance inequality is

`sum_i y_i^2 <= (6/5) delta^2`.                                                    (2)

A self-contained proof is elementary: after affine normalization to `[0,1]^5`, the variance functional is convex and reaches its maximum at a cube vertex; with `k` ones it equals `k(5-k)/5 <= 6/5`.

Combining (1)-(2) gives `||y||^2<=3/5`, hence

`h^2 >= 2/5`.                                                                      (3)

Thus there are no additional equatorial points.

### Step 2: each hemisphere projects to the Petersen threshold

For two extra points on the same side write

`x=a w+sqrt(1-a^2)u`, `x'=b w+sqrt(1-b^2)v`,

with `a,b>=sqrt(2/5)` and `u,v in S^3 subset H`. Their kissing inequality implies

`u.v <= F(a,b)=(1/2-ab)/(sqrt(1-a^2)sqrt(1-b^2))`.

For this range,

`sign(partial F/partial a)=sign(a/2-b)<0`,

and symmetrically in `b`, so

`F(a,b) <= F(sqrt(2/5),sqrt(2/5)) = 1/6`.                                         (4)

Hence the projections from either open hemisphere form a four-dimensional spherical code at maximal inner product `1/6`.

Bachoc and Vallentin, *Optimality and uniqueness of the (4,10,1/6) spherical code*, JCTA 116 (2009), prove that the 10-point Petersen code is the unique optimum at this threshold. The artifact also gives a direct non-extension argument: in the standard centered-2-subset realization of the Petersen code, every unit `z in H` has `max_{i<j}(z_i+z_j)>=1/sqrt(5)`, so some Petersen vector has dot product at least `1/sqrt(6)>1/6` with `z`. Therefore there cannot be an eleventh projected point.

So there are at most ten extras in each hemisphere, and

`|C| <= 20+10+10=40`.

### Step 3: equality forces D5 or Q5

If `|C|=40`, both projected hemisphere codes have exactly ten points and are Petersen codes. Every Petersen vertex has a partner at inner product exactly `1/6`; equality in (4) then forces every extra height to satisfy `|h|=sqrt(2/5)`. Equality in the range-variance inequality forces each projected direction to be one of the 20 directions `P union (-P)`, where `P` is the standard ten-point Petersen code indexed by 2-subsets of `{1,...,5}`.

A mixed-sign pair `u_A,-u_B` is forbidden exactly when the 2-subsets `A,B` are disjoint. The forbidden graph between `P` and `-P` is the connected 3-regular bipartite double cover of the Petersen graph. In any connected regular bipartite graph with equal part sizes `n`, an independent set of size `n` must be an entire side (a one-line regular edge-counting argument). Hence each ten-point layer is exactly `P` or exactly `-P`.

The four top/bottom sign choices reduce under reflection/global isometry to precisely the two known `A_4`-section configurations: opposite layer choices give `D_5`; equal choices give the layer-reflected `Q_5`.

This completes the restricted proof.
