# 2. Wheel setup

For an integer
\[
q\ge4,
\]
let
\[
W_{q+1}=K_1\vee C_q
\]
be the wheel with hub `h` and rim vertices `0,1,...,q-1` cyclically.

There are `q` spokes and `q` rim edges, hence
\[
m=2q.
\tag{2.1}
\]
The degrees and stationary masses are
\[
d_h=q,\qquad d_i=3\ (i\in C_q),
\tag{2.2}
\]
\[
\pi_h=\frac14,
\qquad
\pi_i=\frac{3}{4q}.
\tag{2.3}
\]
Every physical edge therefore has the same Aldous–Fill capacity
\[
c_e=\pi_xP_{xy}=\frac1{4q}.
\tag{2.4}
\]
The graph has diameter
\[
D=2.
\tag{2.5}
\]

The dihedral automorphism group of the rim fixes the hub and is transitive separately on the `q` spokes and the `q` rim edges. Thus there are exactly two edge orbits.

# 3. Dual symmetrization to two variables

Take any feasible dual edge-length assignment `ell`. Average it over the dihedral automorphism group.

The capacity normalization is linear and invariant. For fixed vertices `u,v`, the weighted shortest-path distance `d_ell(u,v)` is the minimum of linear functions of `ell`, hence is concave in `ell`. Since the stationary measure is automorphism-invariant, Jensen averaging cannot decrease the dual objective.

Therefore an optimal dual can be taken with

- one common spoke length `x>=0`;
- one common rim-edge length `y>=0`.

Using (2.4), the dual normalization is
\[
q\frac{x}{4q}+q\frac{y}{4q}=1,
\]
so
\[
\boxed{x+y=4.}
\tag{3.1}
\]

This is an exact reduction of the full finite LP dual, not a relaxation.

# 4. Exact weighted distances

Let
\[
R:=\left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor.
\]
For a rim vertex `i`, direct use of its spoke is always a shortest hub-to-rim route, so
\[
d_\ell(h,i)=x.
\tag{4.1}
\]

For two rim vertices at ordinary cyclic separation `t`, where `1<=t<=R`, there are two relevant route types:

1. stay on the rim, cost `t y` along a shortest rim arc;
2. use the hub, cost `2x`.

Any route mixing a hub excursion with extra rim edges is no cheaper. Hence
\[
\boxed{
\delta_t:=d_\ell(i,i+t)=\min\{t y,2x\}.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

The hub-rim contribution to the ordered stationary-pair dual objective is
\[
2\left(\frac14\right)\left(\frac34\right)x
=\frac{3x}{8}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

For the rim-rim contribution:

- if `q=2R+1` is odd, every rim vertex has two vertices at every separation `1,...,R`, so
  \[
  \Phi_{\rm rim}(x)
  =\frac{9}{8q}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\delta_t;
  \tag{4.4a}
  \]
- if `q=2R` is even, separations `1,...,R-1` occur twice per origin and the antipodal separation `R` occurs once, so
  \[
  \Phi_{\rm rim}(x)
  =\frac{9}{16q}
  \left(2\sum_{t=1}^{R-1}\delta_t+\delta_R\right).
  \tag{4.4b}
  \]

Thus the full dual objective is
\[
\Phi_q(x)=\frac{3x}{8}+\Phi_{\rm rim}(x),
\qquad y=4-x,
\qquad 0\le x\le4.
\tag{4.5}
\]

# 5. Breakpoints and the exact formula

The switch for separation `t` occurs when
\[
t(4-x)=2x,
\]
namely at
\[
x_t=\frac{4t}{t+2}.
\tag{5.1}
\]

The objective `Phi_q` is concave and piecewise linear in `x`, so some maximizer lies at a breakpoint or endpoint. The endpoint `x=0` has objective `0`, while `x=4` has objective `3/2`. For `q>=4`, the breakpoint values dominate `3/2`, so it suffices to inspect
\[
x=x_k:=\frac{4k}{k+2},
\qquad
1\le k\le R.
\tag{5.2}
\]
At this breakpoint
\[
y=\frac8{k+2},
\qquad
\delta_t=\frac{8\min(t,k)}{k+2}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

A direct sum gives the same closed form for both parities of `q`:
\[
\boxed{
V_q(k):=\Phi_q(x_k)
=\frac{3k(4q-3k)}{2q(k+2)}.
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

For example, when `q=2R` is even,
\[
2\sum_{t=1}^{R-1}\min(t,k)+\min(R,k)
=k(q-k),
\]
and the odd case gives the same expression after its multiplicity factor is included.

Therefore finite LP strong duality yields the exact stationary product-flow optimum:

## Theorem 5.1 — exact wheel formula

For every `q>=4`,
\[
\boxed{
F(W_{q+1})
=\max_{1\le k\le\lfloor q/2\rfloor}
\frac{3k(4q-3k)}{2q(k+2)}.
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

No explicit primal routing is needed for exactness: (5.5) is the exact optimum of the full metric LP dual after a symmetry reduction that cannot decrease the objective, and the already archived primal-dual equivalence is a finite LP with strong duality.

# 6. Locating the maximizing breakpoint exactly

The discrete difference simplifies to
\[
V_q(k+1)-V_q(k)
=
\frac{3\bigl(8q-3k^2-15k-6\bigr)}
{2q(k+2)(k+3)}.
\tag{6.1}
\]
The numerator is strictly decreasing in `k`. Hence the sequence `V_q(k)` is unimodal.

One convenient maximizing index is
\[
\boxed{
k_q=
\min\left\{
\left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor,
\left\lceil\frac{\sqrt{96q+153}-15}{6}\right\rceil
\right\}.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]
If equality occurs in the numerator of (6.1), then the adjacent breakpoint ties and either maximizing index may be used.

As `q->infinity`,
\[
k_q=\sqrt{\frac{8q}{3}}+O(1).
\tag{6.3}
\]
From (5.4),
\[
F(W_{q+1})<\frac{3k\cdot4q}{2q(k+2)}<6
\tag{6.4}
\]
for every finite `q`, while (6.3) gives
\[
\boxed{
F(W_{q+1})\longrightarrow6.
}
\tag{6.5}
\]

Thus diameter two by itself does **not** force stationary product-flow congestion below `4` or even below any constant smaller than `6`.

# 7. Certified refutation of the stronger diameter-two `F<=4` conjecture

Take
\[
q=37.
\]
Then `R=18`. From (6.1),
\[
8q-3(7)^2-15(7)-6
=296-147-105-6
=38>0,
\]
while
\[
8q-3(8)^2-15(8)-6
=296-192-120-6
=-22<0.
\]
Hence the unique maximizing breakpoint is `k=8`. Formula (5.4) gives
\[
F(W_{38})
=\frac{3\cdot8\,(4\cdot37-3\cdot8)}{2\cdot37\cdot10}
=\frac{744}{185}
>4.
\tag{7.1}
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
D(W_{38})=2
\quad\text{but}\quad
F(W_{38})=\frac{744}{185}>4.
}
\tag{7.2}
\]

This is an exact rational counterexample to the **stronger auxiliary statement** `F<=4` for all diameter-two graphs. It is **not** a counterexample to the owner target `F<=2 tau_rel D`.

This also invalidates the previous planned attack that tried to prove the full diameter-two case solely by establishing a universal congestion-`4` routing. The correct diameter-two target must retain the spectral factor `tau_rel`.

# 8. The actual Aldous–Fill inequality still holds for every wheel

For the simple random walk on `W_{q+1}`, the rim Fourier modes with nonzero frequency have eigenvalues
\[
\frac23\cos\frac{2\pi j}{q},
\qquad j=1,\ldots,q-1,
\tag{8.1}
\]
and the two-dimensional hub/rim-constant subspace has eigenvalues `1` and `-1/3`.

For `q>=6`, the `j=1` mode gives
\[
\lambda_2(P)\ge
\frac23\cos\frac{2\pi}{q}
\ge\frac13,
\]
so
\[
\tau_{\rm rel}\ge\frac32.
\tag{8.2}
\]
Combining (6.4), (8.2), and `D=2` gives
\[
F(W_{q+1})<6
\le4\tau_{\rm rel}
=2\tau_{\rm rel}D
\qquad(q\ge6).
\tag{8.3}
\]

For `q=4,5`, formula (5.5) gives
\[
F(W_5)=\frac{15}{8}<4,
\qquad
F(W_6)=\frac{21}{10}<4.
\tag{8.4}
\]
Both graphs are connected and noncomplete, so the previously archived interlacing lemma gives `tau_rel>=1`; hence again
\[
F<4\le4\tau_{\rm rel}=2\tau_{\rm rel}D.
\]

Thus:

## Corollary 8.1

Every wheel graph satisfies the requested Aldous–Fill inequality, even though an infinite tail of wheels violates the stronger auxiliary `F<=4` diameter-two statement.

# 9. Structural lesson from the wheel family

The wheel calculation changes the campaign's diameter-two strategy in an important way.

Earlier exact examples had suggested that perhaps all diameter-two graphs satisfy `F<=4` because every connected noncomplete graph has `tau_rel>=1`. The wheel family shows that this inference is false: the spectral factor can be genuinely necessary even at diameter two.

The wheel optimum also demonstrates why edge-orbit weighting matters. The automorphism group has two edge orbits, and the optimal dual does **not** assign equal length to all physical edges. At the maximizing breakpoint `k_q`,
\[
x=\frac{4k_q}{k_q+2},
\qquad
y=\frac8{k_q+2},
\]
so
\[
\frac{x}{y}=\frac{k_q}{2}\asymp\sqrt q.
\]
The dual increasingly penalizes spokes relative to rim edges as the rim grows. The resulting shortest-path metric truncates rim distance at the hub cost `2x`.

This is analogous in spirit to the earlier cycle-power theorem: the optimized metric geometry can be very different from the ordinary unweighted graph metric, and proving the target requires working with the optimized metric rather than only graph-geodesic structure.
