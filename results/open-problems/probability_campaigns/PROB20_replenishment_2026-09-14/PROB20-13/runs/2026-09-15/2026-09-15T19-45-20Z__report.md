# 2. General cycle-power reduction

Let
\[
C_n^k
\]
denote the \(k\)-th power of the cycle on \(\mathbb Z_n\): vertices at ordinary cycle distance at most \(k\) are adjacent. Assume
\[
n\ge2k+1,
\]
so the jump types \(1,\dots,k\) are well defined; equality gives the complete graph \(K_{2k+1}\), while \(n>2k+1\) is noncomplete.

For \(n\ge2k+1\), the graph is \(2k\)-regular, hence
\[
\pi\equiv\frac1n,
\qquad
m=nk,
\qquad
c_e=\pi_xP_{xy}=\frac1{2nk}
\tag{2.1}
\]
on every physical edge.

Write \(d_C\) for ordinary cycle distance on \(C_n\), and
\[
\bar d_C:=\mathbb E[d_C(U,V)]
=\begin{cases}
\dfrac n4,&n\text{ even},\\[2mm]
\dfrac{n^2-1}{4n},&n\text{ odd},
\end{cases}
\tag{2.2}
\]
for independent uniform \(U,V\in\mathbb Z_n\).

Put
\[
S_k:=1+2+\cdots+k=\frac{k(k+1)}2.
\tag{2.3}
\]

## Definition 2.1 — balanced composition at length \(r\)
A **balanced \(k\)-composition distribution** of \(r\) is a probability distribution on finite compositions
\[
r=s_1+\cdots+s_t,
\qquad s_j\in\{1,\dots,k\},
\]
such that, if \(N_s\) denotes the number of parts equal to \(s\), then
\[
\mathbb E N_s=\frac r{S_k}
\qquad(1\le s\le k).
\tag{2.4}
\]
The consistency condition is automatic because
\[
\sum_{s=1}^k s\,\mathbb E N_s
=\frac r{S_k}\sum_{s=1}^k s=r.
\]

## Proposition 2.2 — balanced-composition criterion
Suppose balanced \(k\)-composition distributions exist for every integer
\[
r\ge k+1.
\tag{2.5}
\]
Then, for every \(n\ge2k+1\),
\[
\boxed{
F(C_n^k)=\frac4{k+1}\,\bar d_C.
}
\tag{2.6}
\]

### Proof: dual lower bound
Give every edge of jump type \(s\) the dual length
\[
\ell_s:=\frac{4s}{k+1}.
\tag{2.7}
\]
There are \(n\) physical edges of every jump type \(s\), and each has capacity \(1/(2nk)\). Therefore
\[
\sum_ec_e\ell_e
=\sum_{s=1}^k n\frac1{2nk}\frac{4s}{k+1}
=\frac{2S_k}{k(k+1)}=1.
\tag{2.8}
\]
So the length assignment is dual feasible.

For two vertices at ordinary cycle distance \(r\), every jump of type \(s\) costs \(4s/(k+1)\). Expanding each jump into \(s\) unit cycle steps shows that no path can cost less than
\[
\frac4{k+1}r.
\]
Conversely, a shortest ordinary cycle arc can be partitioned into jumps of sizes at most \(k\), attaining that cost. Hence the induced shortest-path metric is exactly
\[
d_\ell(u,v)=\frac4{k+1}d_C(u,v).
\tag{2.9}
\]
The metric LP dual therefore gives
\[
F(C_n^k)\ge\frac4{k+1}\bar d_C.
\tag{2.10}
\]

### Proof: matching primal routing
For an unordered pair at ordinary cycle distance \(r\le k\), route it directly on its jump-\(r\) edge.

For a pair at distance \(r\ge k+1\), select a shortest orientation around the underlying cycle and sample a balanced \(k\)-composition of \(r\). Route successively along jumps with those part sizes in that orientation. Every such path has total ordinary-cycle length exactly \(r\), so it is shortest for the dual metric (2.9), although it need not be shortest in the unweighted graph \(C_n^k\).

For even \(n\), antipodal pairs have two shortest ordinary-cycle orientations; split them equally between the two orientations before sampling the composition. This preserves translation/reflection symmetry.

The pair multiplicities at ordinary distances \(1,\dots,k\) are identical, and the direct routes at those distances contribute exactly one unit to each jump orbit. Every distance \(r\ge k+1\) contributes, by (2.4), equally in expectation to all \(k\) jump types. Therefore the total stationary product load summed over all physical edges of jump type \(s\) is the same number \(T\), independent of \(s\).

Every routed path of ordinary distance \(r\) satisfies
\[
\sum_{s=1}^k sN_s=r.
\]
Averaging over the stationary product demand gives
\[
S_kT=\bar d_C,
\qquad
T=\frac{\bar d_C}{S_k}.
\tag{2.11}
\]
Translation invariance spreads this orbit load uniformly over the \(n\) physical edges of that jump type. Thus every edge has load
\[
\frac Tn.
\]
Dividing by the capacity \(1/(2nk)\) gives congestion
\[
2kT
=\frac{2k}{S_k}\bar d_C
=\frac4{k+1}\bar d_C.
\tag{2.12}
\]
This matches the dual lower bound and proves (2.6). \(\square\)

The proposition isolates the entire cycle-power problem into a finite combinatorial statement about compositions of integers.

# 3. Exact theorem for third powers of cycles

Here \(k=3\), so
\[
S_3=6.
\]
We prove the balanced-composition hypothesis for every \(r\ge4\).

## Lemma 3.1
For every integer \(r\ge4\), there is a distribution on compositions of \(r\) with parts in \(\{1,2,3\}\) such that
\[
\mathbb EN_1=\mathbb EN_2=\mathbb EN_3=\frac r6.
\tag{3.1}
\]

### Proof
The base lengths are explicit.

For \(r=4\):
- with probability \(1/3\), use \(2+2\);
- with probability \(2/3\), use \(1+3\).

The expected count vector is
\[
(2/3,2/3,2/3)=\frac46(1,1,1).
\]

For \(r=5\):
- probability \(1/2\): \(2+3\);
- probability \(1/6\): \(1+2+2\);
- probability \(1/3\): \(1+1+3\).

The expected count vector is
\[
(5/6,5/6,5/6)=\frac56(1,1,1).
\]

For \(r=6\), use the deterministic composition
\[
1+2+3,
\]
whose count vector is \((1,1,1)=6(1,1,1)/6\).

Finally, there is a balanced composition distribution of \(3\): with probability \(1/2\) use \(3\), and with probability \(1/2\) use \(1+2\). Its expected count vector is
\[
(1/2,1/2,1/2)=\frac36(1,1,1).
\]
Therefore, if a balanced distribution exists at length \(r\), independently append this random composition of \(3\). The new total length is \(r+3\), and every expected count increases by \(1/2=3/6\). Starting from the bases \(4,5,6\), this reaches every integer \(r\ge4\). \(\square\)

## Theorem 3.2
For every \(n\ge7\),
\[
\boxed{
F(C_n^3)=\bar d_C
=\begin{cases}
\dfrac n4,&n\text{ even},\\[2mm]
\dfrac{n^2-1}{4n},&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
}
\tag{3.2}
\]

This follows immediately from Proposition 2.2 and Lemma 3.1 because \(4/(3+1)=1\).

At \(n=7\), \(C_7^3=K_7\), and (3.2) gives \(12/7\), the known complete-graph equality value.

For \(n\ge8\), the graph is connected and noncomplete, so the previously archived interlacing lemma gives
\[
\tau_{\rm rel}\ge1.
\tag{3.3}
\]
Its graph diameter is
\[
D(C_n^3)=\left\lceil\frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{3}\right\rceil.
\tag{3.4}
\]
Writing \(R=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), one has
\[
\bar d_C\le\frac{R+1}{2}\le\frac{2R}{3}\le2D(C_n^3)
\]
for \(R\ge3\). Hence
\[
F(C_n^3)\le2D(C_n^3)\le2\tau_{\rm rel}D(C_n^3).
\tag{3.5}
\]
So the full Aldous–Fill target holds on every third power of a cycle.

# 4. Exact theorem for fourth powers of cycles

Now \(k=4\), so
\[
S_4=10.
\]
Again the required balanced compositions can be built explicitly.

## Lemma 4.1
For every integer \(r\ge5\), there is a distribution on compositions of \(r\) with parts in \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) such that
\[
\mathbb EN_1=\mathbb EN_2=\mathbb EN_3=\mathbb EN_4=\frac r{10}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

### Proof
First construct a balanced random composition of \(4\):
- probability \(2/5\): \(4\);
- probability \(1/5\): \(2+2\);
- probability \(2/5\): \(1+3\).

Its expected count vector is
\[
(2/5,2/5,2/5,2/5)=\frac4{10}(1,1,1,1).
\tag{4.2}
\]
This will be the induction increment.

Base \(r=5\):
- probability \(1/2\): \(1+4\);
- probability \(1/2\): \(2+3\).

Expected counts are \((1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)=5(1,1,1,1)/10\).

Base \(r=6\):
- probability \(1/5\): \(3+3\);
- probability \(3/5\): \(2+4\);
- probability \(1/5\): \(1+1+1+3\).

Expected counts are \((3/5,3/5,3/5,3/5)=6(1,1,1,1)/10\).

Base \(r=7\):
- probability \(7/10\): \(3+4\);
- probability \(1/10\): \(1+2+2+2\);
- probability \(1/5\): \(1+1+1+2+2\).

The expected count of every part size is \(7/10\).

Appending an independent copy of the balanced length-4 composition (4.2) increases total length by \(4\) and each expected count by \(2/5=4/10\). Starting from lengths \(4,5,6,7\), this produces a balanced distribution for every \(r\ge4\), in particular every \(r\ge5\). \(\square\)

## Theorem 4.2
For every \(n\ge9\),
\[
\boxed{
F(C_n^4)=\frac45\bar d_C
=\begin{cases}
\dfrac n5,&n\text{ even},\\[2mm]
\dfrac{n^2-1}{5n},&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

At \(n=9\), \(C_9^4=K_9\), and the formula gives \(16/9\), again the complete-graph equality value.

For \(n\ge10\), \(\tau_{\rm rel}\ge1\) by the archived noncomplete-graph lemma, and
\[
D(C_n^4)=\left\lceil\frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{4}\right\rceil.
\tag{4.4}
\]
Writing \(R=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\ge4\),
\[
F(C_n^4)
=\frac45\bar d_C
\le\frac{2(R+1)}5
\le2\left\lceil\frac R4\right\rceil
=2D(C_n^4).
\tag{4.5}
\]
Thus
\[
F(C_n^4)\le2\tau_{\rm rel}D(C_n^4).
\tag{4.6}
\]

# 5. Structural interpretation

The earlier cycle-square result had an orbit-dual with jump lengths in ratio \(1:2\). The present argument shows this was not an isolated coincidence. For \(C_n^k\), the natural dual candidate assigns length proportional to the underlying cycle displacement,
\[
\ell_s\propto s.
\]
Optimality reduces exactly to whether product demand can be routed so that the **aggregate expected number of used edges is equal across every jump orbit**. The balanced-composition distributions perform precisely that equalization while keeping every routed path shortest for the weighted dual metric.

For \(k=3,4\), this equalization is now proved for all required displacement lengths. The resulting optimum is strictly different from the uniform graph-metric dual on many non-distance-regular cycle powers; thus the correct dual geometry is the hidden underlying cycle metric, not the unweighted metric of the powered graph.

This gives a concrete combinatorial target for the whole family:

> **Balanced-composition conjecture.** For every \(k\ge2\) and every integer \(r\ge k\), the vector
> \[
> \left(\frac r{S_k},\dots,\frac r{S_k}\right)
> \]
> lies in the convex hull of count vectors of compositions of \(r\) with parts in \(\{1,\dots,k\}\).

If this conjecture holds, Proposition 2.2 immediately gives
\[
F(C_n^k)=\frac4{k+1}\bar d_C
\]
for **all** powers of cycles. Finite LP experiments from this run found no counterexample to the balanced-composition conjecture for \(2\le k\le8\) and \(k\le r<40\); this is evidence only, not proof.
