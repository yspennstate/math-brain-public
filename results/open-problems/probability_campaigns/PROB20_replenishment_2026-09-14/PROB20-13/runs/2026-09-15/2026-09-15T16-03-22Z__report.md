## 2. Exact Aldous--Fill normalization and LP retained

Aldous--Fill Lemma 4.21 uses the same source-to-stationary flow normalization, and Open Problem 4.49(ii) asks whether diameter can replace the \(\log n\) factor in
\[
\inf_{\mathbf f}\psi(\mathbf f)\le K\tau_2\log n.
\]
Primary source:

https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/RWG/book.pdf

For a finite simple unweighted graph with \(m=|E|\),
\[
\pi_xP_{xy}=\frac1{2m}
\]
on every oriented physical edge.

With \(g^a=\pi_af^a\), the problem is exactly the undirected fractional multicommodity instance with unordered pair demands
\[
h_{ab}=2\pi_a\pi_b\qquad(a<b)
\]
and edge capacity \(1/(2m)\).

The finite metric LP dual is
\[
F(G)=
\max_{\ell_e\ge0,\ \sum_e c_e\ell_e\le1}
\sum_{x,y}\pi_x\pi_y d_\ell(x,y),
\tag{2.1}
\]
where \(c_e=1/(2m)\) and \(d_\ell\) is shortest-path pseudometric. Equivalently,
\[
F(G)=2\sup_{d\in\mathsf{Met}(V)}
\frac{\mathbb E_{\pi\otimes\pi}d(X,Y)}
{\mathbb E_{\pi P}d(X_0,X_1)}.
\tag{2.2}
\]

This exact product-demand equivalence is used below; no weighted-chain or altered-demand generalization is being substituted.

# 3. New exact theorem: squares of cycles

For \(n\ge5\), let
\[
G_n:=C_n^2
\]
be the square of the \(n\)-cycle: its vertices are \(\mathbb Z_n\), and vertices are adjacent exactly when their cyclic difference is \(\pm1\) or \(\pm2\).

There are two physical edge orbits:

- **type 1:** the \(n\) cycle edges of cyclic step \(1\);
- **type 2:** the \(n\) edges of cyclic step \(2\).

Thus \(G_n\) is 4-regular,
\[
m=2n,
\qquad
\pi\equiv\frac1n,
\qquad
c_e=\frac1{4n}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Let
\[
\bar d_C:=\mathbb E[d_{C_n}(U,V)]
\]
for independent uniform \(U,V\in\mathbb Z_n\). Elementary cycle counting gives
\[
\bar d_C=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac n4,&n\text{ even},\\[1mm]
\dfrac{n^2-1}{4n},&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\tag{3.2}
\]

## Theorem 3.1
For every \(n\ge5\),
\[
\boxed{
F(C_n^2)=\frac43\bar d_C
=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac n3,&n\text{ even},\\[1mm]
\dfrac{n^2-1}{3n},&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
}
\tag{3.3}
\]

The proof has a matching exact dual certificate and an explicit translation-invariant primal routing.

# 4. Exact dual lower bound

Assign edge lengths
\[
\ell_1=\frac43
\]
to every type-1 edge and
\[
\ell_2=\frac83
\]
to every type-2 edge.

Because there are \(n\) edges of each type and each edge has capacity \(1/(4n)\),
\[
\sum_ec_e\ell_e
=
n\frac1{4n}\frac43
+n\frac1{4n}\frac83
=1.
\tag{4.1}
\]
Thus the length assignment is dual-feasible.

For any \(x,y\),
\[
d_\ell(x,y)=\frac43d_{C_n}(x,y).
\tag{4.2}
\]
Indeed, a type-2 step has length exactly twice a type-1 step. Expanding every type-2 move into two ordinary cycle moves preserves total length, so every \(C_n^2\)-path has length at least \((4/3)d_{C_n}(x,y)\). Conversely, an ordinary shortest cycle path uses only type-1 edges and attains exactly that cost.

Therefore the dual objective is
\[
\sum_{x,y}\frac1{n^2}d_\ell(x,y)
=\frac43\bar d_C.
\tag{4.3}
\]
Hence
\[
F(C_n^2)\ge\frac43\bar d_C.
\tag{4.4}
\]

# 5. Matching explicit primal routing

Use the equivalent ordered product-demand formulation: every ordered pair \((u,v)\) carries demand \(1/n^2\).

Fix an ordered pair \(u\ne v\), and write
\[
r=d_{C_n}(u,v).
\]
Choose a shortest orientation of the cycle from \(u\) to \(v\). If \(n\) is even and \(r=n/2\), choose the two shortest orientations with probability \(1/2\) each. We route monotonically along this chosen shortest cycle arc using only steps of size 1 or 2.

Let \(N_1(r)\) and \(N_2(r)\) denote the numbers of type-1 and type-2 edges used. They always satisfy
\[
N_1(r)+2N_2(r)=r.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Define the routing as follows.

### Distance 1
Use the single type-1 edge:
\[
(N_1,N_2)=(1,0).
\tag{5.2}
\]

### Distance 2
Use the single type-2 edge:
\[
(N_1,N_2)=(0,1).
\tag{5.3}
\]

### Distance \(r\ge3\)
Choose an integer random variable
\[
B_r\in\left\{\left\lfloor\frac r3\right\rfloor,
\left\lceil\frac r3\right\rceil\right\}
\]
with
\[
\mathbb E B_r=\frac r3.
\tag{5.4}
\]
Set
\[
N_2(r)=B_r,
\qquad
N_1(r)=r-2B_r.
\tag{5.5}
\]
This is always a valid nonnegative integer composition for \(r\ge3\), because
\[
\left\lceil\frac r3\right\rceil\le\left\lfloor\frac r2\right\rfloor.
\]
Randomize the order of the \(N_1\) one-steps and \(N_2\) two-steps in any translation-invariant way along the chosen shortest orientation (uniformly over their orderings is one concrete choice).

From (5.4)--(5.5), for every \(r\ge3\),
\[
\mathbb E N_1(r)=\mathbb E N_2(r)=\frac r3.
\tag{5.6}
\]

## 5.1 Global equality of the two edge-type loads

For every \(n\ge5\), each source has exactly two vertices at cycle distance 1 and exactly two at cycle distance 2. Thus the excess
\[
N_1-N_2=+1
\]
from the distance-1 pairs is exactly canceled, in total stationary product mass, by the excess
\[
N_1-N_2=-1
\]
from the distance-2 pairs.

All pairs at distance \(r\ge3\) have equal expected type-1 and type-2 counts by (5.6). Therefore the total expected numbers of type-1 and type-2 edges traversed by a random ordered product-demand pair are equal. Call their common value \(T\):
\[
\mathbb E N_1=\mathbb E N_2=T.
\tag{5.7}
\]

Taking expectations in the exact path-length identity (5.1),
\[
3T
=\mathbb E[N_1+2N_2]
=\mathbb E d_{C_n}(U,V)
=\bar d_C.
\]
Hence
\[
T=\frac{\bar d_C}{3}.
\tag{5.8}
\]

## 5.2 Edge congestion

The routing rule is translation-invariant. Therefore the total type-1 load \(T\) is spread equally over the \(n\) physical type-1 edges, and the total type-2 load \(T\) is spread equally over the \(n\) physical type-2 edges. Every physical edge therefore has path load
\[
\frac{T}{n}.
\]
Since its capacity is \(1/(4n)\), its congestion is
\[
\frac{T/n}{1/(4n)}
=4T
=\frac43\bar d_C.
\tag{5.9}
\]

Thus
\[
F(C_n^2)\le\frac43\bar d_C.
\tag{5.10}
\]
Together with the dual lower bound (4.4), this proves Theorem 3.1 exactly.

# 6. Verification of the sharp diameter target on \(C_n^2\)

For \(n=5\),
\[
C_5^2=K_5,
\]
so this is the already-known complete-graph equality case:
\[
F=\frac85,
\qquad
\tau_{\rm rel}=\frac45,
\qquad
D=1.
\]

Now assume \(n\ge6\). The simple-random-walk Fourier eigenvalue at frequency 1 is
\[
\lambda^{(1)}
=\frac12\left(
\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}
+
\cos\frac{4\pi}{n}
\right).
\tag{6.1}
\]
For \(n\ge6\), this is nonnegative, because \(2\pi/n\le\pi/3\), so
\[
\cos(2\pi/n)\ge\frac12,
\qquad
\cos(4\pi/n)\ge-\frac12.
\]
Hence \(\lambda_2(P)\ge0\), and therefore
\[
\tau_{\rm rel}\ge1.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The graph distance in \(C_n^2\) is
\[
d_{C_n^2}(x,y)=\left\lceil\frac{d_{C_n}(x,y)}2\right\rceil,
\]
so
\[
D(C_n^2)=
\left\lceil
\frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{2}
\right\rceil.
\tag{6.3}
\]

If \(n\) is even,
\[
F(C_n^2)=\frac n3
<\frac n2
\le2D.
\tag{6.4}
\]
If \(n\) is odd, \(n\ge7\),
\[
F(C_n^2)=\frac{n^2-1}{3n}
\le\frac{n-1}{2}
\le2D.
\tag{6.5}
\]
Therefore, for every \(n\ge5\),
\[
\boxed{
F(C_n^2)\le2\tau_{\rm rel}D.
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

# 7. New obstruction: vertex transitivity does not imply uniform-metric optimality

The preceding distance-regular theorem proved
\[
F(G)=2\mathbb E d_G(X,Y)
\]
for every distance-regular graph, with the uniform graph metric as an optimal dual certificate.

It is tempting to replace distance-regularity by vertex transitivity. The family above disproves that extension.

Take \(G=C_8^2\). This is a Cayley graph of \(\mathbb Z_8\), hence vertex-transitive. But it is not distance-regular: adjacent edges of the two generator types have different numbers of common neighbors. Explicitly,

- vertices \(0,1\) have common neighbors \(\{2,7\}\), two of them;
- vertices \(0,2\) have common neighbor \(\{1\}\), only one.

Thus the distance-regular intersection number \(a_1\) is not well-defined.

The uniform graph-metric dual gives
\[
2\mathbb E d_{C_8^2}(X,Y)=\frac52.
\tag{7.1}
\]
But Theorem 3.1 gives
\[
F(C_8^2)=\frac83.
\tag{7.2}
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{
F(C_8^2)=\frac83>\frac52
=2\mathbb E d_{C_8^2}(X,Y).
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

So the uniform graph metric is **not** dual-optimal on all vertex-transitive graphs. Any attempt to extend the distance-regular radial proof using vertex transitivity alone is therefore invalid.

The optimal dual on \(C_n^2\) instead weights the two edge orbits in ratio
\[
1:2,
\]
which reconstructs the underlying cycle metric rather than the graph metric of the square.

# 8. A broader conjectural pattern, not a theorem

The calculation suggests a possible extension to the \(k\)-th cycle power \(C_n^k\), \(1\le k<n/2\). The natural dual assigns orbit length proportional to cyclic jump size:
\[
\ell_s=\frac{4s}{k+1},
\qquad s=1,\dots,k,
\tag{8.1}
\]
which is exactly normalized and induces the scaled cycle metric
\[
\frac4{k+1}d_{C_n}.
\]
This gives the lower bound
\[
F(C_n^k)\ge\frac4{k+1}\bar d_C.
\tag{8.2}
\]

Finite LP checks for small \(k,n\) are consistent with equality, yielding the conjectural formula
\[
F(C_n^k)\stackrel{?}{=}
\frac4{k+1}\bar d_C.
\tag{8.3}
\]

For \(k=1\) this is the cycle formula; for \(k=2\) it is Theorem 3.1 above. I do **not** currently have the needed general primal balancing lemma that decomposes every cyclic distance into jump sizes \(1,\dots,k\) while equalizing the aggregate load of all jump orbits. Therefore (8.3) remains explicitly **CONJECTURAL** and is not used in the proved ledger.
