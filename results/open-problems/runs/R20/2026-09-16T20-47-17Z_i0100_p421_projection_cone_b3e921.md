## 4. Move #14 — use the actual stabilizer, then stop asking it for more than it has

Write the vertices of `G_{p,1}` as

\[
Q^*=\{\alpha^j:j\in\mathbb Z_n\},
\]

where `alpha` generates the nonzero squares. Multiplication by a square acts simply transitively on `Q^*` and preserves whether a difference is a square. In exponent coordinates this is translation

\[
j\mapsto j+k.
\]

There is also a reflection. For `x,y in Q^*`,

\[
x^{-1}-y^{-1}=\frac{y-x}{xy}.
\]

Here `xy` is a square and, because `p == 1 mod 4`, `-1` is a square. Hence inversion preserves adjacency. In exponent coordinates it is

\[
j\mapsto-j.
\]

Therefore the dihedral action

\[
\boxed{D_n=\mathbb Z_n\rtimes\{\pm1\}}
\tag{4.1}
\]

acts by automorphisms. Averaging the theta SDP over translations gives the circulant LP; reflection makes the Fourier variables real and even. This is the exact group-theoretic reason for the familiar cosine reduction.

**Outcome of move #14.** The stabilizer action explains and exhausts the robust symmetry reduction presently available: after the dihedral quotient the p=421 exact optimizer still lives in a degree-24 real cyclotomic field and has a 54-point half-frequency support. I found no further generally valid group quotient that collapses the arithmetic active-set problem. Repeating symmetry reduction alone is therefore not a route to the asymptotic constant.

## 5. Move #11 — a stronger dualization: theta is an l1 minimum in a positive orthant cut by an arithmetic orthogonal projection

The previous run rewrote theta as a zero-autocorrelation/Turan problem. The useful new dual language is a positive-cone projection problem.

Let

\[
H_p=G_{p,1}=\operatorname{Cay}(\mathbb Z_n,S_p),
\]

where `S_p=-S_p` is the set of nonzero edge differences. Put

\[
\Omega_p=\{0\}\cup\bigl(\mathbb Z_n\setminus(\{0\}\cup S_p)\bigr),
\tag{5.1}
\]

the zero lag together with all nonedge differences. Let `F` be the unitary DFT on `Z_n`, let `P_Omega` be coordinate projection onto `Omega_p`, and define

\[
K_p=F P_{\Omega_p}F^*.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Then `K_p` is an orthogonal projection.

### Theorem 5.1 — exact positive-cone projection formulation

For every admissible prime,

\[
\boxed{
\vartheta(\overline{G_{p,1}})
=
\min\left\{
\mathbf 1^Tq:
q\in\mathbb R_+^n,
\ q_0=1,
\ K_pq=q
\right\}.
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

Moreover, if `lambda_r` denotes the adjacency eigenvalue of the circulant local graph at frequency `r`, then

\[
\boxed{
(K_p)_{m,m'}=
\begin{cases}
|\Omega_p|/n,&m=m',\\
-\lambda_{m-m'}/n,&m\ne m'.
\end{cases}
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

Here

\[
|S_p|=\frac{p-5}{4},
\qquad
|\Omega_p|=\frac{p+3}{4},
\qquad
\frac{|\Omega_p|}{n}=\frac{p+3}{2(p-1)}=\frac12+O(p^{-1}).
\tag{5.5}
\]

### Proof

In the standard Fourier dual of the circulant theta LP, let `q_m` denote the nonnegative dual trigonometric polynomial evaluated at frequency `m`, normalized so that

\[
q_0\ge1,
\qquad q_m\ge0\quad(m\ne0).
\]

The inverse Fourier transform of `q` is supported exactly on the permitted dual coefficient lags, namely `Omega_p`. Conversely every real nonnegative `q` whose inverse Fourier transform is supported on `Omega_p` gives such a dual certificate after averaging with the reflection `m -> -m` if necessary. The objective is

\[
n f(0)=\sum_m q_m.
\]

At an optimum `q_0=1`, since otherwise positive scaling down preserves feasibility and strictly lowers the objective.

The support condition on the inverse DFT is equivalent to

\[
q\in F\operatorname{Ran}(P_{\Omega_p})
=\operatorname{Ran}(K_p),
\]

and because `K_p` is a projection this is exactly `K_pq=q`. This proves (5.3).

For (5.4), unitary Fourier conjugation gives

\[
(K_p)_{m,m'}
=
\frac1n\sum_{d\in\Omega_p}
\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i(m-m')d}{n}\right).
\]

When `m=m'` this is `|Omega_p|/n`. When `r=m-m' != 0`, the complete character sum over `Z_n` is zero, so

\[
\sum_{d\in\Omega_p}e^{2\pi i r d/n}
=-\sum_{d\in S_p}e^{2\pi i r d/n}
=-\lambda_r.
\]

This proves (5.4). QED.

### Corollary 5.2 — an exact conserved first moment

For every feasible `q` in (5.3), the zero coordinate of `K_pq=q` gives

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{m\ne0}(-\lambda_m)q_m
=|S_p|=\frac{p-5}{4}.
}
\tag{5.6}
\]

Indeed,

\[
1=\frac{|\Omega_p|}{n}
-\frac1n\sum_{m\ne0}\lambda_mq_m.
\]

Since the objective is

\[
T_p=1+\sum_{m\ne0}q_m,
\]

(5.6) says that the `q`-weighted mean of `-lambda_m` is exactly

\[
\frac{|S_p|}{T_p-1}.
\tag{5.7}
\]

The conjecture `T_p ~ sqrt(n)` is therefore asking for a nonnegative fixed vector of the arithmetic projection whose spectral first moment is of order `sqrt(n)` with the exact constant forced by (5.7).

A one-frequency relaxation of (5.6) gives only

\[
T_p\ge1+\frac{|S_p|}{-\lambda_{\min}},
\tag{5.8}
\]

which is precisely the extreme-eigenvalue/Hoffman ceiling. The full conjecture needs the **entire fixed-point equation** `K_pq=q`, not merely the smallest adjacency eigenvalue.

## 6. Jacobi-phase form of the projection kernel

The off-diagonal kernel in (5.4) has an exact multiplicative-character description. Let `chi` be the quadratic character on `F_p`, and for a nontrivial Fourier frequency `m` let `rho_m` be a multiplicative character whose restriction to the square subgroup is the corresponding character of `Z_n`. A direct indicator expansion gives

\[
\lambda_m
=-\frac12+rac14
\left(
J(\rho_m,\chi)+J(\rho_m\chi,\chi)
\right).
\tag{6.1}
\]

For the nonexceptional frequencies here, the standard Gauss-sum identity gives

\[
J(\rho_m,\chi)J(\rho_m\chi,\chi)
=G(\chi)^2=p,
\]

while both Jacobi sums have modulus `sqrt(p)`. Thus they are conjugates and

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_m
=-\frac12+rac12\Re J(\rho_m,\chi).
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

This character identity is standard Jacobi-sum mechanism and is **not claimed novel**. Its use here is diagnostic: `K_p` is an orthogonal projection with diagonal asymptotic `1/2` and off-diagonal entries of Jacobi-sum scale `1/sqrt(n)`. But the entries are highly arithmetic and correlated, not Haar-random.

## 8. New route suggested by 5.3: arithmetic projected-orthant geometry

Equation (5.3) changes the category of the problem without changing its content. It asks for the minimum `l1` mass of a nonnegative vector in a fixed roughly half-dimensional subspace, normalized at one coordinate. Random-projection and nonnegative-linear-programming theory studies closely related orthant sections and face geometry.

For a Haar-random half-dimensional subspace, conic integral geometry and the Donoho--Tanner projected-orthant theory provide sharp threshold phenomena. The Paley subspace is not Haar-random: `K_p` is circulant, its eigenvectors are deterministic Fourier characters, and its kernel entries are Jacobi sums. Therefore those random results cannot simply be quoted.

The analytic opportunity is nevertheless precise. It would suffice to prove a **Paley orthant-section theorem** strong enough to show

\[
\min\{\|q\|_1:q\ge0,\ q_0=1,\ K_pq=q\}
=(1+o(1))\sqrt n.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Kunisky's weak empirical spectral convergence does not provide this: `K_p` itself has only eigenvalues 0 and 1, so the missing information is high-dimensional cone geometry/eigenvector arithmetic, not the empirical eigenvalue law.

A useful next intermediate target is to control the Paley projection on the positive orthant in a way that uses the full Jacobi kernel, e.g. a deterministic analogue of random projected-orthant neighborliness or a restricted lower bound on `\|q\|_1` and a matching construction for nonnegative fixed vectors. This is genuinely different from another extreme-eigenvalue lemma.

## 9. Why the obvious quadratic Jacobi certificate does not close

The local edge-sign sequence

\[
\sigma_d=\chi(\alpha^d-1),\qquad \sigma_0=0,
\]

has Fourier transform

\[
\widehat\sigma(m)=2\lambda_m+1.
\]

Its periodic autocorrelation admits the exact character-sum reduction

\[
A_\sigma(d)
=\sum_j\sigma_j\sigma_{j+d}
=-1+\frac12
\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}
\chi\bigl(x(x-1)(\alpha^d x-1)\bigr),
\tag{9.1}
\]

for `d != 0`. The second term is a Legendre-family elliptic-curve trace up to the conventional sign, so Hasse gives only square-root variation.

This kills the simplest nonlinear spectral ansatz. If one tries a nonnegative quadratic Fourier certificate

\[
q_m=(\widehat\sigma(m)+c)^2,
\]

then its inverse Fourier coefficient at a nonzero edge lag is

\[
A_\sigma(d)+2c\sigma_d
=-1+\frac12E_d+2c,
\qquad \sigma_d=1,
\]

where `E_d` is the varying elliptic trace. No single constant `c` can cancel all edge lags unless those traces are constant, which they are not in the exact covered examples. Thus a degree-two polynomial in the adjacency spectrum is structurally too rigid. Any explicit Jacobi-phase certificate must use more than the first real Jacobi coordinate.

This is a route failure, not a new conjecture.
