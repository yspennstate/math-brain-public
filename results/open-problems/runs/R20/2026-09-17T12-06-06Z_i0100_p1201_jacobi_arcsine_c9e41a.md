## 4. Methods #9 and #22 — random-phase behavior becomes rigorous: Jacobi/arcsine law

Let `g` be primitive in `F_p^*`, let

\[
\alpha=g^2,
\]

so `alpha` generates the nonzero squares, and let `chi` be the quadratic character. Define

\[
\sigma(d)=\chi(\alpha^d-1),\qquad d\in\mathbb Z_n,
\]

with `sigma(0)=0`. Then

\[
\mathbf 1_N(d)=\frac{1-\delta_0(d)-\sigma(d)}2.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Let `rho` generate the multiplicative character group of `F_p^*`, normalized by

\[
\rho(g)=e^{2\pi i/(p-1)},
\]

and for `1<=x<=n-1` put `A_x=rho^x`. Define

\[
F_p(x)=\sum_{d\in\mathbb Z_n}\sigma(d)e^{2\pi i xd/n}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

### Theorem R20-ARC1 — exact Jacobi formula and arcsine limit

For every `1<=x<=n-1`,

\[
\boxed{
F_p(x)=\frac12\bigl(J(A_x,\chi)+J(A_x\chi,\chi)\bigr)
      =\operatorname{Re}J(A_x,\chi).
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Consequently, for every `x!=0`,

\[
\boxed{
r_p^{(0)}(x)
=-\frac{1+\operatorname{Re}J(A_x,\chi)}{n}.
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

As `p -> infinity` through primes `p=1 mod 4`, the empirical law over `x=1,...,n-1` of

\[
Z_p(x):=\sqrt n\,r_p^{(0)}(x)
\]

converges to the centered arcsine law on `[-sqrt(2),sqrt(2)]`, with density

\[
\boxed{\frac1{\pi\sqrt{2-z^2}}\,\mathbf 1_{|z|<\sqrt2}.}
\tag{4.5}
\]

More quantitatively,

\[
\boxed{
\frac1{n-1}\#\left\{x\ne0:r_p^{(0)}(x)<-\frac1{\sqrt n}\right\}
=\frac14+O(p^{-1/4}).
}
\tag{4.6}
\]

Finally,

\[
\boxed{
-\sqrt n\min_{x\ne0}r_p^{(0)}(x)\longrightarrow\sqrt2.
}
\tag{4.7}
\]

Thus the raw projection's factor-`sqrt(2)` loss is not merely an artifact of applying the Weil bound to a potentially atypical maximum: the raw certificate actually reaches the Jacobi/Weil endpoint asymptotically.

### Proof of R20-ARC1

Because the squares have indicator `(1+chi)/2`, and because `chi(-1)=1` for `p=1 mod 4`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
F_p(x)
&=\sum_{q\in Q}\chi(q-1)A_x(q)\\
&=\frac12\sum_{t\in\mathbb F_p^*}
(1+\chi(t))A_x(t)\chi(1-t)\\
&=\frac12\bigl(J(A_x,\chi)+J(A_x\chi,\chi)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]

For `1<=x<=n-1`, the characters `A_x`, `chi`, and `A_x chi` are all nontrivial. The standard Gauss/Jacobi identity gives

\[
J(A_x,\chi)J(A_x\chi,\chi)=G(\chi)^2=p,
\]

because `chi(-1)=1`, while both Jacobi sums have modulus `sqrt(p)`. Hence

\[
J(A_x\chi,\chi)=\overline{J(A_x,\chi)},
\]

which proves (4.3).

For `x!=0`, summing (4.1) against the additive character on `Z_n` gives

\[
2\sum_{d\in N}e^{2\pi ixd/n}=-1-F_p(x),
\]

and (4.4) follows from the definition `r_p^(0)=2P_N delta_0`.

Now use Ping Xi, *Equidistributions of Jacobi sums* (arXiv:1809.04286), Theorem 1.1. For a nontrivial fixed second character `eta`, Xi proves that the arguments of `J(A,eta)/sqrt(p)`, as `A` ranges over the nonexceptional multiplicative characters, are equidistributed on the unit circle with discrepancy `O(p^{-1/4})`. The theorem is stated for arbitrary nontrivial `eta`, so take `eta=chi`.

There are exactly `2(n-1)=p-3` nonexceptional characters `A` here, paired by `A <-> A chi`. Equation (4.3) shows that the real part is identical on each pair, and `A_x`, `1<=x<=n-1`, selects exactly one representative from each pair. Therefore the empirical distribution of

\[
\frac{F_p(x)}{\sqrt p}
=\frac{\operatorname{Re}J(A_x,\chi)}{\sqrt p}
\]

is the pushforward of Xi's asymptotically uniform circle law by `z -> Re z`, namely the arcsine law on `[-1,1]`.

From (4.4),

\[
Z_p(x)
=-\frac1{\sqrt n}
-\sqrt{\frac pn}\,\frac{\operatorname{Re}J(A_x,\chi)}{\sqrt p},
\tag{4.8}
\]

and `p/n -> 2`, proving (4.5).

The inequality `r_p^(0)(x)<-1/sqrt(n)` is equivalent to

\[
\frac{\operatorname{Re}J(A_x,\chi)}{\sqrt p}
>
\frac{\sqrt n-1}{\sqrt p}
=\frac1{\sqrt2}+O(p^{-1/2}).
\]

Under uniform angle, the probability that `cos(theta)>1/sqrt(2)` is exactly `1/4`; Xi's discrepancy gives (4.6).

Finally the Jacobi modulus bound gives the upper endpoint in (4.7), while equidistribution puts characters in every fixed positive-length arc around argument zero for all sufficiently large `p`, giving the matching lower endpoint. QED.

There is also a useful exact energy identity requiring no asymptotics. Parseval and `|N|=n/2` give

\[
\sum_x\bigl(r_p^{(0)}(x)\bigr)^2
=\frac1n\sum_{k\in N}|2|^2=2.
\]

Since `r_p^(0)(0)=1`,

\[
\boxed{\sum_{x\ne0}\bigl(r_p^{(0)}(x)\bigr)^2=1.}
\tag{4.9}
\]

So the conjectured `1/sqrt(n)` scale was already exactly the RMS scale of the raw projection; the obstruction is its arcsine **extreme tail**, not excess `L^2` energy.

### Why this is method #9 rather than another numerical analogy

Before this run, treating the Jacobi phases as random was only a heuristic route. Xi's theorem makes the relevant one-point random-phase model rigorous: the raw Paley projection genuinely has the arcsine empirical law. What randomization does **not** provide is the joint information needed to choose the free `S`-Fourier coefficients so as to lift the negative tail simultaneously. The random route therefore identifies the correct failure mode rather than solving the flattening problem by itself.

## 5. A new dense-correction obstruction

R20-ARC1 says the sharp correction cannot be sparse or perturbative.

Fix any

\[
0<\delta<\sqrt2-1
\]

and define

\[
\gamma_\delta
:=\frac1\pi\arccos\left(\frac{1+\delta}{\sqrt2}\right)>0.
\]

Then R20-ARC1 gives

\[
\frac1n\#\left\{x\ne0:
 r_p^{(0)}(x)<-\frac{1+\delta}{\sqrt n}\right\}
\longrightarrow\gamma_\delta.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Suppose a correction `w_p` lies in the same Paley half-Fourier space, has `w_p(0)=0`, and achieves the conjecturally sharp floor

\[
\min_x(r_p^{(0)}+w_p)(x)
\ge-\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt n}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

On the set in (5.1), for all sufficiently large `p`, necessarily

\[
w_p(x)\ge\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt n}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
|\{x:w_p(x)>0\}|\ge(\gamma_\delta+o(1))n,
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

and, because `w_p` has zero mean,

\[
\boxed{
\|w_p\|_1\ge(\gamma_\delta\delta+o(1))\sqrt n,
\qquad
\|w_p\|_2\ge\frac\delta2\sqrt{\gamma_\delta}+o(1).
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

I label this consequence **R20-DENSE1**.

This is a meaningful route elimination. Any proof that hopes to start from the raw projection and repair only `o(n)` exceptional coordinates, or add an `o(1)`-energy perturbation, cannot reach the sharp constant. The correction has to be a global reorganization of the free spectrum. That makes the maximum-entropy / moment-ray route from the preceding run more plausible than a sparse golfing repair.

## 6. Method #13 — the monotone quantity on the entropy ray, and why it is not yet enough

The previous run parameterized the flat-spectrum feasible ray by `a` and introduced the unique maximum-entropy interior solution

\[
y_a(x)=\frac{e^{\langle\lambda(a),\Phi(x)\rangle}}{Z(\lambda(a))},
\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}_{y_a}(\Phi)\lambda'(a)=-b,
\]

with

\[
H''(a)=-I(a),
\qquad
I(a):=b^T\operatorname{Cov}_{y_a}(\Phi)^{-1}b\ge0.
\]

The natural conserved-baseline quantity is the relative entropy from the uniform-off-zero starting point,

\[
D(a):=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(y_a\|y_{\mathrm{base}})
=\log(n-1)-H(a).
\]

Since `lambda(a_0)=0`, one gets the exact identities

\[
\boxed{
D'(a)=\int_{a_0}^a I(t)\,dt,
\qquad
D(a)=\int_{a_0}^a(a-t)I(t)\,dt.
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

Thus `D'(a)` is nonnegative and monotone increasing. This is the clean monovariant requested by method #13.

But it also diagnoses a limitation: the moment ray hits the boundary when the **vector** parameter `lambda(a)` escapes, whereas (6.1) only controls the scalar projection `b dot lambda`. A covariance matrix can become badly conditioned in directions almost orthogonal to `b`, allowing `||Cov^{-1}b||` to be much larger than `b^T Cov^{-1}b`. Therefore a scalar entropy-curvature estimate alone does not certify survival of the full exponential-family path to `a=(1-o(1))/sqrt(n)`.

The sharpened analytic target is now vectorial:

\[
\boxed{
\int_{a_0}^{(1-\varepsilon)/\sqrt n}
\left\|\operatorname{Cov}_{y_a}(\Phi)^{-1}b\right\|\,da
<\infty
\quad\text{with a Paley-uniform control strong enough to keep all }y_a(x)>0.
}
\tag{6.2}
\]

R20-ARC1/DENSE1 says such a mechanism must move a positive density of coordinates by the target time; a local/sparse covariance argument is not enough.
