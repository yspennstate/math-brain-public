# 6. Method #27 — SWAP THE QUANTIFIERS: why ordinary LP duality is too weak, and what stronger duality must do

The construction form of `(BLOCK-12)` is
\[
\exists\,12\text{ centers}\quad\forall\,2048\text{ quotient points}:\ \text{covered}.
\]
Its negation is
\[
\forall\,12\text{-center sets}\quad\exists\,\text{an uncovered quotient point}.
\]
That suggests a set-cover dual certificate. However the **first-order fractional LP** cannot be enough. In the folded quotient, the radius-3 ball size is
\[
|B|=\sum_{j=0}^3\binom{12}{j}=299,
\]
and translation symmetry makes the standard fractional covering optimum exactly the uniform sphere bound
\[
\frac{2^{11}}{299}=\frac{2048}{299}\approx6.8495,
\]
far below 12. The same phenomenon occurs at `n=22`, where the corresponding uniform fractional value is
\[
\frac{2^{21}}{280600}=\frac{262144}{35075}\approx7.4738,
\]
far below 22.

This is route analysis, **not a new R02 theorem claim**: it simply explains why swapping quantifiers and writing the elementary LP does not solve the problem. Any useful impossibility dual must encode **integrality and higher-order intersection information**. That is exactly what the Gijswijt--Polak SDP/Lasserre constraints are designed to add.

Outcome of method #27: simple LP duality is ruled out as too weak; the correct swapped-quantifier object is a higher-order SDP or exact branch-and-bound certificate.
