## 2. The covering-code dictionary retained from the preceding run

Identify signs with binary words. For a row `r` and signing `x`,

\[
|r\cdot x|
=
 n-2\min\{d_H(r,x),d_H(-r,x)\}.
\]

Therefore the rows and their complements form an antipodal binary code, and

\[
\operatorname{disc}(A)=n-2R(C),
\]

where `R(C)` is the covering radius of the row/complement code. After quotienting by complementation, an `n x n` sign matrix is represented by `n` folded Hamming-ball centers in `F_2^{n-1}`.

For `n=22`, the first discrepancy that would improve the present global lower bound is `d=8`, since

\[
\frac8{\sqrt{22}}=1.705605730844883\ldots
>
\frac7{\sqrt{17}}.
\]

By the dictionary, `d=8` is equivalent to an antipodal 44-word code of length 22 having covering radius at most 7, or equivalently 22 folded radius-seven centers covering the quotient cube `F_2^{21}`.

## 3. Method #16 — COMPUTE EVERY SMALL CASE: every order-24 Hadamard matrix has discrepancy 6

The source itself treats Hadamard matrices as a natural discrepancy family. Instead of testing one familiar construction, I used Brendan McKay's public classification data for **all equivalence classes at order 24**.

McKay's data page defines Hadamard equivalence by row permutations, column permutations, row negations and column negations, and lists exactly **60 equivalence classes at order 24**. The public hexadecimal representatives were parsed exactly; the checker first verifies `H H^T=24I` for every representative.

### R02-I0095-C004 — exact order-24 Hadamard discrepancy

**STATUS: PROVED for the full class of order-24 Hadamard matrices; PARTIAL relative to Open Problem 11.**

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{disc}(H)=6
\quad\text{for every Hadamard matrix }H\text{ of order }24.
}
\tag{C004}
\]

### Proof

Let `H` be an arbitrary `24 x 24` Hadamard matrix and `x in {+-1}^{24}`. Since

\[
H^T H=24I,
\]

we have the exact energy identity

\[
\|Hx\|_2^2
=x^TH^THx
=24\|x\|_2^2
=24^2
=576.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Every coordinate of `Hx` is a sum of 24 signs and therefore is even. If `||Hx||_infty<=4`, then

\[
\|Hx\|_2^2
\le 24\cdot4^2
=384,
\]

contradicting (3.1). Hence for every signing

\[
\|Hx\|_\infty\ge6,
\]

and so

\[
\operatorname{disc}(H)\ge6.
\tag{3.2}
\]

For the converse, the exact checker stores one signing for each of McKay's 60 public order-24 representatives and verifies in integer arithmetic that its maximum row sum is exactly 6. Thus every equivalence-class representative has discrepancy at most 6.

Finally, discrepancy is invariant under Hadamard equivalence. Row permutations and row sign changes preserve `||Hx||_infty`. A column permutation or column sign change merely bijects the signing set `x in {+-1}^{24}`. Therefore every Hadamard matrix equivalent to one of the 60 representatives has the same discrepancy as its representative. Since the 60 representatives exhaust the equivalence classes, (3.2) and the explicit witnesses prove C004. `square`

## 4. Method #18 — SAY IT IN ANOTHER LANGUAGE: no 22 x 22 circulant can reach the first improving threshold

A particularly economical way to seek an `n=22`, discrepancy-eight matrix is a circulant sign matrix: choose one binary seed `a in F_2^{22}`, take its 22 cyclic shifts as rows, and include complements implicitly through the absolute value in discrepancy.

In the covering-code language this becomes a one-orbit covering problem.

### R02-I0095-C005 — cyclic-orbit exclusion at n=22

**STATUS: PROVED restricted-family exclusion; PARTIAL relative to Open Problem 11.**

\[
\boxed{
A\text{ circulant }22\times22\text{ sign matrix cannot have discrepancy }\ge8.
}
\tag{C005}
\]

Since all row sums against signings are even when `n=22`, this says every such circulant matrix has discrepancy at most 6.

### Proof

Suppose a circulant `22 x 22` sign matrix generated by seed `a` had discrepancy at least 8. Then for every binary word `x in F_2^{22}`, some cyclic shift `sigma^k a` would satisfy

\[
|22-2d_H(x,\sigma^k a)|\ge8.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\min\{d_H(x,\sigma^k a),\,22-d_H(x,\sigma^k a)\}\le7.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Thus the cyclic orbit of `a`, together with complements, would cover the entire 22-cube to radius 7.

Apply (4.1) to `x=0`. Some cyclic shift of `a` or its complement must have Hamming weight at most 7. Cyclic shift does not change weight, so after complement normalization we may assume

\[
\operatorname{wt}(a)\le7.
\tag{4.2}
\]

We may also quotient by cyclic rotation and choose the lexicographically least rotation as a canonical seed. The exact checker exhausts all such canonical binary necklaces of length 22 and weight at most 7. There are exactly

\[
12{,}766
\]

of them. For each canonical seed it tests all `2^{21}` words after quotienting by global complement and checks whether one of the 22 cyclic shifts covers the word in the folded radius-seven sense. **None** of the 12,766 seeds covers the quotient cube.

Therefore no cyclic seed satisfies (4.1), proving C005. `square`

This is deliberately labelled a restricted-family theorem. It rules out the most symmetric one-orbit construction at the smallest order that could improve the global metric; it does **not** rule out a non-circulant `22 x 22` matrix with discrepancy 8.
