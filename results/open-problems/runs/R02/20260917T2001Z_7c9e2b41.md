## 3. Method #9 — RANDOMIZE: pass an arbitrary syndrome through a perfect finite sharing stage

The previous run used the zero-sum parity block

\[
B_1+\cdots+B_N=0
\]

to show that exact erasure reconstruction can coexist with zero HGR between every proper pair of visible child collections. The natural randomized extension is to let the parity be a **parent syndrome** rather than a fixed zero.

Let `G` be a finite abelian group, let `S` be an arbitrary `G`-valued random variable, and conditional on `S=s` sample

\[
(B_1,\ldots,B_N)
\]

uniformly from

\[
\mathcal C_s:=\{(b_1,\ldots,b_N)\in G^N:b_1+\cdots+b_N=s\}.
\]

### Lemma 3.1 — arbitrary-syndrome perfect sharing

For every proper coordinate set `I subsetneq {1,...,N}`:

1. `(B_i)_{i in I}` is Haar-uniform on `G^I`;
2. `(B_i)_{i in I}` is independent of `S`;
3. all `N` children recover `S` exactly by
   \[
   S=\sum_{i=1}^N B_i.
   \]

In particular every strict subcollection has zero mutual information and zero maximal correlation with the parent syndrome, while the complete collection determines it.

**Proof.** Fix `s` and values `b_i`, `i in I`, with `|I|=r<N`. There are exactly

\[
|G|^{N-r-1}
\]

completions of these prescribed coordinates to a vector in `\mathcal C_s`: choose all but one remaining coordinate freely, then the last is forced by the sum. Since `|\mathcal C_s|=|G|^{N-1}`, the conditional probability of the prescribed visible coordinates is

\[
|G|^{N-r-1}/|G|^{N-1}=|G|^{-r},
\]

independent of `s`. Thus the proper projection is uniform and independent of `S`; the final assertion is the defining sum relation. ∎

This is standard additive secret-sharing/coding folklore; I make **no novelty claim** for the gadget. Its relevance to D0031 is sharper than the fixed-zero block: an arbitrary higher-scale state can be hidden perfectly from every incomplete collection of lower-scale shares.

### What this suggests

A global phase failed in the preceding run because each one-sided tail could infer the same phase. Lemma 3.1 suggests a different stationarization philosophy:

> never expose a marker/syndrome directly; store it only as a jointly reconstructible secret split among several spatial pieces.

At one scale this completely removes the HGR cost of the marker from any strict subset of shares. The question is whether these hidden syndromes can be linked through infinitely many scales without either becoming one-sided-tail visible or disappearing from all finite cylinder laws.

## 4. Method #18 — SAY IT IN ANOTHER LANGUAGE: hidden hierarchy as an HGR strong-data-processing problem

The previous D0038 handoff formulated a stringent `cut-blind hierarchy` condition: conditional on a hidden hierarchy `H`, the two separated sides should be independent and each side's marginal law should not depend on `H`. That condition is sufficient but stronger than necessary.

The natural language for rho is the operator norm of conditional expectation.

### Lemma 4.1 — HGR contraction through a latent hierarchy

Let `L,H,R` be random elements such that

\[
L\perp R\mid H.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\rho(L,R)\le \rho(L,H)\,\rho(H,R).
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The same statement holds for sigma-fields.

**Proof.** For centered square-integrable `f(L)` and `g(R)`, conditional independence gives

\[
\mathbb E[f(L)g(R)]
=
\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E(f\mid H)\,\mathbb E(g\mid H)\big].
\]

By Cauchy–Schwarz and the operator-norm characterization of HGR maximal correlation,

\[
\|\mathbb E(f\mid H)\|_2
\le \rho(L,H)\|f\|_2,
\qquad
\|\mathbb E(g\mid H)\|_2
\le \rho(H,R)\|g\|_2.
\]

Taking the supremum over normalized centered `f,g` yields (4.1). ∎

This is standard HGR/data-processing machinery; no novelty claim is made.

### Consequence for the construction problem

A successful marker hierarchy need not be literally invisible to each side. It is enough that every hierarchy mode undergo a strict `L^2` contraction before reaching the left and right tails, with the product tending to zero at large separation.

The syndrome-sharing gadget of Lemma 3.1 is the extreme endpoint: a proper share collection has

\[
\rho(\text{shares},S)=0.
\]

Thus exact bilateral reconstruction and small rho are not in local conflict. The real issue is **stationary multiscale composition**.

This also explains why ordinary rare-event/marker estimates are inadequate. For alpha-mixing, making a bridge event rare may be useful. For rho, a nonconstant common latent variable that is exactly measurable from both sides forces rho to one, however rarely the coding feature that reveals it appears. A positive route must contract every common `L^2` mode, not merely make shared structures sparse.

## 5. Method #25 — COUNT THE SAME THING TWICE: pure additive syndrome recursion collapses to one parity check

The obvious next idea after Lemma 3.1 is to recurse it: encode a parent syndrome into `N` children, each child into `N` grandchildren, and so on. This looks multiscale, but an exact count shows that **nothing genuinely multiscale is created**.

Consider a rooted `N`-ary tree of depth `d`. Give the root value `S=s in G`. At every internal node `v` with value `V_v`, sample its `N` child values uniformly subject to

\[
\sum_{u\text{ child of }v}V_u=V_v,
\]

independently across distinct nodes conditional on their parent values.

Let `L_1,...,L_{N^d}` be the leaf values.

### Proposition 5.1 — telescoping collapse of the additive hierarchy

Conditional on `S=s`, the leaves are **uniform over exactly the one-level parity code**

\[
\boxed{
\left\{(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_{N^d})\in G^{N^d}:
\sum_{j=1}^{N^d}\ell_j=s\right\}.
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Hence the entire depth-`d` additive secret-sharing hierarchy has exactly the same leaf law as a single additive sharing of `s` into `N^d` shares.

**Proof by counting twice.** The sum constraints telescope, so every generated leaf configuration satisfies the root relation in (5.1).

Conversely, fix any leaf configuration satisfying (5.1). Every internal node value is then uniquely determined as the sum of the leaves in its descendant subtree. Hence there is exactly one compatible assignment of all internal values.

There are

\[
1+N+\cdots+N^{d-1}=\frac{N^d-1}{N-1}
\]

internal nodes. Each internal node chooses uniformly among `|G|^{N-1}` child tuples with the prescribed parent sum. Therefore every compatible full tree assignment has probability

\[
|G|^{-(N-1)(1+N+\cdots+N^{d-1})}
=|G|^{-(N^d-1)}.
\]

There are exactly `|G|^{N^d-1}` leaf vectors satisfying (5.1), so the conditional leaf law is uniform on that set. ∎

### Why this kills the naive recursive parity route

Pure nested additive sharing does not produce a hierarchy of independently useful hidden relations; it only pushes a **single parity constraint** to a larger scale.

There is then a basic dichotomy:

- keep the top syndrome fixed/coherently identifiable, and a large-scale relation remains that a stationarizing phase/marker can potentially reveal to both tails;
- average the top syndrome Haar-uniformly, in which case the leaf vector becomes fully iid Haar on `G^{N^d}` and the reconstruction relation disappears from the observed finite-level law;
- link the top syndrome to a higher level by the **same** additive mechanism, and Proposition 5.1 simply repeats at the larger scale.

Thus the attractive `syndrome passing` idea does **not** by itself solve the projective gluing problem. A successful finite-state hierarchy must be **non-telescoping**: overlapping checks, non-additive/quasigroup relations, or another architecture in which higher-scale reconstruction constraints survive without creating a one-sided common latent mode.

I have not found this exact D0031 formulation in the checked records. The algebra behind Proposition 5.1 is elementary secret-sharing folklore, so novelty is **UNVERIFIED and not claimed**.
