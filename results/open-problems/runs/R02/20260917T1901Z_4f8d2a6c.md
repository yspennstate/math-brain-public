## 3. B6 — DEFORM TO A SOLVED POINT: a parity-check stage is exactly HGR-safe

The neighboring D0038 work showed that a finite parity tile can simultaneously reconstruct an erased coordinate and keep separated visible pieces independent. The first task here was to put that observation in a form that can be iterated in a rho-mixing construction.

Let \(H\) be any nontrivial finite abelian group and let \(N\ge2\). Think of \(H\) as a whole child block alphabet; for example \(H=G^m\) lets each \(B_i\) represent an entire length-\(m\) block over a fixed finite alphabet \(G\).

Choose

\[
(B_1,\ldots,B_N)
\]

uniformly from the single-parity-check subgroup

\[
\mathcal C_N(H)
:=\{(b_1,\ldots,b_N)\in H^N:b_1+\cdots+b_N=0\}.
\]

### Lemma 3.1 — perfect erasure recovery with zero HGR across every proper split

For this law:

1. any \(N-1\) of the \(B_i\)'s are jointly independent and uniform on \(H^{N-1}\);
2. any missing child block is recovered exactly from the other \(N-1\) by
   \[
   B_j=-\sum_{i\ne j}B_i;
   \]
3. if \(I,J\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}\) are disjoint and
   \[
   |I\cup J|\le N-1,
   \]
   then
   \[
   \sigma(B_i:i\in I)\quad\text{and}\quad \sigma(B_j:j\in J)
   \]
   are independent, hence their maximal correlation is exactly zero.

**Proof.** Choose \(B_1,\ldots,B_{N-1}\) iid Haar-uniform on \(H\) and set \(B_N=-\sum_{i<N}B_i\). Deleting any one coordinate from the parity equation gives a bijective group homomorphism from \(H^{N-1}\) to the remaining \(N-1\) coordinates, so every \(N-1\)-coordinate marginal is Haar-uniform on \(H^{N-1}\). Items 2 and 3 follow immediately. ∎

### Why this is stronger than the previous one-time-pad diagnostic

In the preceding R02 run, the one-time-pad example only said that **one secret** can be reconstructed from two independent shares. Lemma 3.1 gives the block-coding form required by a hierarchy:

- the missing object may be an entire child block \(B_j\in G^m\), not a single bit;
- *every* proper collection of child blocks is fully independent;
- therefore a left collection and a right collection have exactly \(\rho=0\) whenever at least one complete child block remains in the central gap.

So the local HGR problem is not the obstruction. There exists a finite-alphabet reconstruction stage that is perfect in both senses:

\[
\boxed{\text{exact one-child erasure recovery + exact zero HGR between separated visible children}.}
\]

This single-parity-check mechanism is elementary coding/secret-sharing folklore and is already adjacent to D0038's finite tile. I make **no novelty claim** for the gadget itself.

The useful new route is the deformation: start from a solved bilateral-deterministic hierarchical elimination construction and replace its globally constrained child-type permutations by parity-check blocks of this form. The parity version has the same “recover the missing child from its siblings” logic while removing the most obvious HGR correlation at a fixed stage.

## 4. B14 — FIND THE GROUP ACTING: rho tensorizes by a supremum, and group Fourier modes quantify the cost of a share

Bradley's own survey gives exactly the tensorization principle one wants for a multiscale construction. His Theorem 5.1(b), attributed there to Csáki–Fischer, states that if the pairs \((\mathcal A_j,\mathcal B_j)\) are independent across \(j\), then

\[
\boxed{
\rho\!\left(\bigvee_j\mathcal A_j,\bigvee_j\mathcal B_j\right)
=\sup_j\rho(\mathcal A_j,\mathcal B_j).
}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Bradley's Theorem 5.2(b) then gives the process version: combining independent component sequences coordinatewise does not make rho larger than the worst component.

This is unusually favorable. For rho, independent layers cost a **maximum**, not a sum. Bradley's real-valued constructions can exploit that by storing countably many independent layer coordinates in one real random variable. The finite-state problem can now be viewed as a storage/compression problem:

\[
\boxed{
\text{replace countably many coordinatewise layers by a finite-alphabet time hierarchy without creating a shared tail mode.}
}
\]

That perspective is more precise than “quantize the real-valued witness”: the issue is not preserving rho — (4.1) makes independent-layer rho easy — but multiplexing infinitely many reconstruction scales through finitely many symbols.

### Lemma 4.1 — exact HGR cost of an additive share channel

Let \(G\) be a finite abelian group. Let \(U\) be Haar-uniform on \(G\), let \(S\) be independent with law \(\mu\), and set

\[
L=U,\qquad R=U+S.
\]

Then both \(L\) and \(R\) are uniform and

\[
\boxed{
\rho(L,R)=\max_{\chi\ne1}|\widehat\mu(\chi)|,
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

where the maximum is over nontrivial characters of \(G\).

**Proof.** On \(L^2(G)\) with Haar measure, the conditional-expectation operator from functions of \(R\) to functions of \(L\) is convolution by \(\mu\):

\[
(Tf)(u)=\mathbb E[f(R)\mid L=u]
=\sum_{s\in G}\mu(s)f(u+s).
\]

The characters diagonalize convolution, with eigenvalues \(\widehat\mu(\chi)\). Constants give the trivial eigenvalue 1. The HGR maximal correlation is exactly the operator norm of \(T\) on the mean-zero subspace, hence the largest nontrivial Fourier modulus. ∎

This formula makes the preceding secret-sharing route quantitatively honest:

- if the secret increment \(S\) is uniform, all nontrivial Fourier coefficients vanish and \(\rho(L,R)=0\);
- if \(S\) is deterministic, \(\rho(L,R)=1\);
- if \(S\) is supported on a proper coset/subgroup, some nontrivial Fourier mode can survive with modulus 1;
- “small Shannon information” is not enough. What matters to rho is an \(L^2\)/spectral-flatness condition.

So an arbitrary nonuniform central block cannot simply be one-time-padded and declared HGR-safe. A successful hierarchy must keep the reconstructed block **Fourier-flat at the share interface**, or use a more elaborate group action whose nontrivial representation modes are uniformly contracted.

Again, (4.2) is standard finite-group harmonic analysis / maximal-correlation machinery. I do not claim the formula itself as new. Its value here is that it gives the exact design variable for D0031.

## 5. B20 — FIND WHY THE ALGORITHM FAILS: global-phase stationarization destroys rho maximally

Lemma 3.1 leaves a tempting construction: tile \(\mathbb Z\) by parity blocks, then choose a random global phase so the tiling becomes stationary. This run checks that algorithm before trying to build a hierarchy on top of it.

It fails in the strongest possible way.

### Proposition 5.1 — a random global phase is a common past/future variable, so \(\rho(n)=1\)

Fix a nontrivial finite abelian group \(H\) and \(N\ge2\). For a phase

\[
\Theta\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\},
\]

partition \(\mathbb Z\) into blocks

\[
\Theta+kN+\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\]

Conditional on \(\Theta\), sample these blocks independently and uniformly from \(\mathcal C_N(H)\), the zero-sum parity code from Section 3. Finally take \(\Theta\) uniform and independent before sampling the blocks.

Then the resulting \(H\)-valued process is strictly stationary, but for every \(n\ge1\),

\[
\boxed{\rho(n)=1.}
\tag{5.1}
\]

In particular, this obvious stationarization of an HGR-perfect parity stage is not rho-mixing.

**Proof.** Stationarity is immediate because a unit shift cyclically changes the phase and the phase is uniform.

Now fix the true phase \(\Theta=\theta\). For the correct phase, every aligned length-\(N\) block has sum zero.

Consider a wrong candidate phase \(s\ne\theta\), and let \(d=s-\theta\pmod N\), so \(1\le d\le N-1\). Each length-\(N\) block aligned at phase \(s\) consists of a nonempty proper suffix of one true parity block and a nonempty proper prefix of the next true parity block.

Under the uniform zero-sum law on \(H^N\), the sum of any nonempty proper subcollection of coordinates is Haar-uniform on \(H\). The two pieces come from independent true blocks, so their total is Haar-uniform. Hence for every wrong-phase block,

\[
\Pr(\text{wrong-phase block sum}=0)=\frac1{|H|},
\]

and the violation probability is

\[
1-\frac1{|H|}>0.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Along blocks going to \(-\infty\), the wrong-phase violation process is a stationary finite-range function of the iid true blocks; by the ergodic theorem its empirical violation density is almost surely the positive number in (5.2). The correct phase has violation density zero. Thus \(\Theta\) is measurable from the infinite past alone. The same argument with blocks going to \(+\infty\) shows that \(\Theta\) is measurable from the future \(\sigma\)-field after **any** finite separation.

Therefore the past and every remote future share the same nonconstant random variable \(\Theta\). Taking any nonconstant centered function of \(\Theta\) on both sides gives correlation one, proving (5.1). ∎

### Consequence

This identifies the precise failure of the first finite-state parity algorithm:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the parity relation is HGR-safe; the global device used to make its block grid stationary is not.}
}
\]

This is sharper than the generic warning “a hidden hierarchy might create dependence.” Here the shared hierarchy variable is explicitly recoverable from each one-sided tail and forces the worst possible maximal correlation at every lag.

The same diagnosis applies to a deterministic nested block hierarchy made stationary only by one global odometer/phase: if that phase is asymptotically recognizable from either tail, it is an immediate common HGR mode.

I have not found this exact phase-obstruction proposition stated in the checked D0031/D0038 records. The ingredients are elementary, so **novelty is UNVERIFIED and not claimed**.

## 6. What the three methods say together

The run substantially narrows the constructive problem even though the fixed metric did not move.

### 6.1 What is now solved at one stage

For a whole block alphabet \(H=G^m\), the single-parity-check distribution gives simultaneously:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{one complete child block is exactly determined by its siblings},\\[2mm]
\text{every proper subset of child blocks is product-uniform},\\[2mm]
\text{left/right visible siblings have }\rho=0\text{ whenever at least one child is omitted.}
\end{array}}
\]

Thus D0031 is not blocked by a one-scale information inequality.

### 6.2 What fails in the obvious infinite/stationary gluing

A fixed block grid is not stationary. Randomizing it by a single global phase creates a tail-visible common variable and gives \(\rho(n)=1\) for all \(n\). Coordinatewise stacking of independent scales preserves rho beautifully by Csáki–Fischer, but countably many layers require an infinite coordinate alphabet unless one finds a finite-state multiplexing mechanism.

So the genuine remaining problem is now:

\[
\boxed{
\text{build a stationary *local/mixing* hierarchy of parity-check reconstruction stages, with no remotely recoverable global phase.}
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

The hierarchy/marker information must itself be protected by the same kind of balanced sharing; simply hiding a global marker does not help if each tail can estimate it.

### 6.3 A concrete next architecture

The most promising positive route after this run is no longer “quantize Bradley 1986” in the abstract. It is:

1. use a finite group alphabet \(G\);
2. at each reconstruction scale, group child blocks and impose an \(H=G^m\) parity check, so any one missing child is recovered but separated proper subcollections are exactly independent;
3. choose block boundaries by a **local stationary random marker process**, not a global phase or odometer;
4. encode the marker/hierarchy data recursively so it is itself recoverable from the double tail;
5. prove a uniform HGR bound by conditioning/decomposing into independent group layers and applying Bradley/Csáki–Fischer tensorization;
6. ensure that no marker variable measurable from each one-sided tail survives as a common mode.

The difficult item is 3–6 jointly. A stationary renewal hierarchy would remove the single global phase, but if its boundaries are explicitly present in the output then the marker process also has to satisfy bilateral determinism; if they are latent, conditional independence is not enough because mixing over a shared latent hierarchy can reintroduce HGR. Therefore a successful code must make the one-sided law **group-invariant with respect to the local hierarchy state**, rather than merely average over it.

That is a much more specific target than “find a nonlinear infinite-memory process.”
