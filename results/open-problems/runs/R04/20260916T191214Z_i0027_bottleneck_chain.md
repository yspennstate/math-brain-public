## 3. Method #22 — READ THE FAILURES: the factor-half closing inequality is false

The previous R04 run isolated the tempting statement

\[
K(G)\stackrel{?}{\le}\frac12 |E(G)|\,\operatorname{diam}(G),
\tag{3.1}
\]

because, if true, it would combine with the retained diameter-sensitive Gutman bound and close the source constant at `1/54`.

This run deliberately searched the literature for families designed to make fixed-order/fixed-diameter Kemeny bounds large. Ciardo–Dahl–Kirkland, *On Kemeny's constant for trees with fixed order and diameter* (2020/2021), prove an asymptotically sharp tree upper bound and identify broom-stars as the sharpness mechanism. That failure mode produces an exact counterexample to (3.1).

Define `BS(3,2,p)` as follows: a central root is joined to three arm vertices, and each arm vertex is joined to `p` pendant leaves. Then

\[
n=3p+4,\qquad m=3p+3,\qquad d=4.
\]

For a tree, deleting an edge with component size `s` gives component volumes `2s-1` and `2(n-s)-1`, hence

\[
K(T)
=\frac1{2(n-1)}
\sum_{e}(2s_e-1)(2(n-s_e)-1).
\tag{3.2}
\]

In `BS(3,2,p)`, the `3p` leaf edges have `s=1`, while the three root–arm edges have `s=p+1`. Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
K(BS(3,2,p))
&=\frac{3p(6p+5)+3(2p+1)(4p+5)}{6(p+1)}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{14p+5}{2}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.3}
\]

But

\[
\frac{md}{2}=6(p+1),
\]

so

\[
\frac{K}{md/2}
=\frac{14p+5}{12(p+1)}\longrightarrow\frac76>1.
\]

The smallest member of this subfamily violating (3.1) is `p=4`:

\[
n=16,\quad m=15,\quad d=4,\quad K=61/2>30=md/2.
\]

The exact checker independently recomputes `K=61/2` from the degree-weighted tree distance formula.

**R04-X1 (route refutation):** the exact universal factor-half inequality (3.1) is false. This does **not** refute Aldous–Fill 6.13. The broom-star failure is sparse and only has `K=Theta(n)`, far below the `Theta(n^3)` source scale. It kills the naive closing route while suggesting that an **asymptotic dense-extremizer** version may still be viable.

## 4. Method #11 — DUALIZE: Kemeny as a uniform-spanning-tree cut energy

Write

\[
R^*(G):=\sum_{u<v}d_ud_vR_{uv}=2mK(G),
\]

where `R_uv` is effective resistance and `m=|E|`.

Let `tau(G)` be the number of spanning trees. The standard two-forest form of the resistance identity says

\[
R_{uv}=\frac{F_{uv}}{\tau(G)},
\]

where `F_uv` is the number of spanning two-forests whose two components separate `u` and `v`. Summing first over vertex pairs gives

\[
R^*(G)
=\frac1{\tau(G)}
\sum_{F=(A,B)}\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(B).
\tag{4.1}
\]

Now count the same two-forest in a second way. A fixed two-forest `F=(A,B)` has exactly

\[
b_F:=|\partial A|
\]

spanning-tree preimages: add any one of its crossing edges. Therefore, if `T` is a uniform spanning tree and deleting `e in T` gives components `A_e,B_e`, then

\[
\boxed{
R^*(G)
=\mathbb E_T
\sum_{e\in T}
\frac{\operatorname{vol}(A_e)\operatorname{vol}(B_e)}{|\partial A_e|}.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
K(G)
=\frac1{2m}\mathbb E_T
\sum_{e\in T}
\frac{\operatorname{vol}(A_e)(2m-\operatorname{vol}(A_e))}{|\partial A_e|}.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

The two-forest/resistance mechanism is standard prior art; this report does not claim it as novel. Its value here is that it exposes exactly what a cubic Kemeny extremizer must contain: many large-volume cuts with very small edge boundary.

## 5. Method #25 — COUNT THE SAME THING TWICE: a necessary linear bottleneck chain

### R04-I0027-C3 — bottleneck-chain theorem

**STATUS: PROVED. Novelty: UNVERIFIED. Metric: structural, not the frozen coefficient-width metric.**

Fix `kappa>0`. Let `G_n` be any sequence of connected simple unweighted graphs with

\[
K(G_n)\ge \kappa n^3
\tag{5.1}
\]

for all sufficiently large `n`. Then there exists a spanning tree `T_n` and a nested chain of edge-subtree cuts

\[
A_1\supsetneq A_2\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq A_{L_n}
\]

such that

\[
\boxed{L_n\ge (4\kappa^3-o(1))n,}
\tag{5.2}
\]

and every cut in the chain satisfies

\[
\boxed{|\partial A_j|\le \frac1{4\kappa^2}+o(1),}
\tag{5.3}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{vol}(A_j),\operatorname{vol}(A_j^c)
\ge \kappa^2n^2,
}
\tag{5.4}
\]

and consequently

\[
\boxed{
|A_j|,|A_j^c|\ge (\kappa-o(1))n.
}
\tag{5.5}
\]

So every cubic-scale candidate extremizer has a **linearly long nested family of bounded-size edge cuts separating two macroscopic dense sides**.

### Proof

Let `m=|E(G_n)|` and `d=diam(G_n)`. The elementary retained bound `K<=dm` and `d<=n-1` imply

\[
m\ge \frac{\kappa n^3}{n-1}
=(\kappa+o(1))n^2.
\tag{5.6}
\]

Hence

\[
R^*(G_n)=2mK(G_n)
\ge (2\kappa^2-o(1))n^5.
\tag{5.7}
\]

For a spanning tree `T` and `e in T`, define

\[
Q_T(e):=
\frac{\operatorname{vol}(A_e)\operatorname{vol}(B_e)}{|\partial A_e|}.
\]

By (4.2),

\[
\mathbb E_T\sum_{e\in T}Q_T(e)=R^*(G_n).
\tag{5.8}
\]

Call `e` **high** if

\[
Q_T(e)\ge \kappa^2n^4.
\tag{5.9}
\]

There are only `n-1` tree edges, so the total contribution of all non-high edges is at most

\[
\kappa^2n^4(n-1)
=(\kappa^2+o(1))n^5.
\]

Equations (5.7)–(5.8) therefore imply that the expected high-edge contribution is at least

\[
(\kappa^2-o(1))n^5.
\tag{5.10}
\]

For every cut,

\[
\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(A^c)
\le m^2\le n^4/4,
\]

and `|partial A|>=1`, so `Q_T(e)<=n^4/4`. Combining with (5.10),

\[
\mathbb E_T N_{\rm high}(T)
\ge (4\kappa^2-o(1))n.
\tag{5.11}
\]

Choose a spanning tree with at least that many high edges.

For a high edge, (5.9) gives

\[
\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(A^c)
\ge \kappa^2n^4|\partial A|.
\tag{5.12}
\]

Since the product is at most `m^2<=n^4/4`,

\[
|\partial A|\le 1/(4\kappa^2)+o(1),
\]

which is (5.3). Since `|partial A|>=1`, (5.12) also gives product at least `kappa^2 n^4`. Each factor is at most `2m<n^2`, hence both factors are at least `kappa^2 n^2`, proving (5.4).

If `s=|A|`, then

\[
\operatorname{vol}(A)
=2|E(A)|+|\partial A|
\le s(s-1)+O_\kappa(1).
\]

Together with (5.4), this yields `s>=(kappa-o(1))n`; the same holds for the complement, proving (5.5).

Finally root the selected spanning tree. Every tree edge determines the child-subtree below it. These subtree sets form a laminar family: two are either disjoint or nested. An antichain of high subtrees is pairwise disjoint, and each has at least `(kappa-o(1))n` vertices, so the width of this laminar poset is at most `1/kappa+o(1)`. By Dilworth's chain decomposition theorem, some chain has length at least

\[
\frac{(4\kappa^2-o(1))n}{1/\kappa+o(1)}
=(4\kappa^3-o(1))n.
\]

This proves (5.2) and C3. QED.

The missing source-closing step is now more geometric:

> Given a graph with such a long laminar bounded-cut skeleton, compress the dense pieces to a weighted one-dimensional chain and prove that its Kemeny cut energy is asymptotically maximized by two terminal dense masses of total vertex mass `2/3` separated by path mass `1/3`.
