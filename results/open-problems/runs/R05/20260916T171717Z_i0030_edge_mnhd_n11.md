## Exact classification at order 11

### Theorem R05-I0030-N11

Every finite simple connected arc-transitive graph on 11 vertices is isomorphic to either the cycle C_11 or the complete graph K_11.

### Proof

Let G be a connected arc-transitive graph on 11 vertices and let A=Aut(G), acting transitively on V(G). Identify the vertex set with F_11 after choosing a regular 11-cycle supplied by the prime-degree action.

By Burnside’s theorem there are two cases.

**Case 1: A is doubly transitive.** Since G is connected it has an edge {x,y}. Double transitivity sends the ordered pair (x,y) to every ordered pair (u,v) with u != v. Automorphisms preserve adjacency, so every two distinct vertices are adjacent. Thus G=K_11.

**Case 2: A is not doubly transitive.** Müller’s prime-degree theorem puts A inside AGL(1,11): every automorphism has the form

\[
x\mapsto ax+b,\qquad a\in F_{11}^*,\ b\in F_{11}.
\]

Translation invariance makes G a circulant Cayley graph

\[
G=\operatorname{Cay}(F_{11},S),
\]

where \(\emptyset\ne S\subset F_{11}^*\) and simplicity/undirectedness give \(S=-S\). Let H be the point stabilizer of 0 in A. Then H is a subgroup of \(F_{11}^*\), acting by multiplication. Arc-transitivity means H is transitive on the neighbors of 0, so for any s in S,

\[
S=Hs.
\]

Because S=-S, the element -s belongs to Hs, hence -1 belongs to H. The cyclic group \(F_{11}^*\) has order 10 and subgroup orders 1,2,5,10. The subgroups containing -1 therefore have orders only 2 or 10. Since multiplication acts freely on nonzero field elements, \(|S|=|H|\). Thus either |S|=2, giving a connected 2-regular graph C_11, or |S|=10, giving K_11. This exhausts the non-doubly-transitive case.

Combining the two cases proves the theorem. QED.

This argument explicitly disposes of the possible exceptional/non-solvable prime-degree automorphism groups: they can only occur in Burnside’s doubly-transitive branch, and that branch forces K_11. That is the outcome of method #26.
