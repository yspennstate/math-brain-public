## 4. Method #1 — CHANGE THE CATEGORY: gauge the slope away

The first idea was to ask whether the difficult nonzero-slope problem is an artifact of the height representation.

For an integer slope `k in Z^d`, write

\[
\phi(x)=k\cdot x+\psi(x).
\]

Then `psi` is still integer-valued and has zero slope, while the interaction in coordinate `i` becomes

\[
W_i(r)=V_i(r+k_i).
\]

This gives a genuine exact gauge reduction. Moreover, adding an affine term `a_i r+b_i` to a nearest-neighbor gradient potential changes a fixed-boundary Hamiltonian only by a boundary constant. Thus whenever

\[
V_i(r+k_i)=U_i(r)+a_i r+b_i
\]

with `U_i` in a zero-slope localized class, the integer-slope model is exactly reducible to that zero-slope class.

This mechanism is useful, but it does **not** produce a new source closure. The primary paper already explicitly lists the integer-valued Gaussian free field at `s in Z^d` as a simple known example: it has the zero-slope law plus an integer-valued hyperplane. For a general convex interaction, the shifted `W_i` need not be even or belong to a presently solved zero-slope class. So the gauge route explains a known island of solvability rather than the full conjecture.

I also tested the stronger geometric temptation: use an integer unimodular change of lattice coordinates to turn a rational slope into one with two zero coordinates. Algebraically this can straighten a rational vector, but a general `GL_d(Z)` map does **not** preserve the nearest-neighbor coordinate-edge Hamiltonian: standard edges are sent to longer lattice vectors. Thus the source theorem cannot simply be transported by a lattice shear without first proving a finite-range/general-basis extension. That missing extension is itself theorem-sized.

Outcome of #1: **useful exact reduction, but it lands on source-known special cases and does not make I0007 CLOSABLE.**

## 5. Method #6 — DEFORM TO A POINT YOU CAN SOLVE: move off the two-zero-coordinate plane

The source theorem gives a solved manifold of parameters: two coordinates have slope zero, and those two interactions are even/strong. A natural deformation is to turn on tiny slopes `epsilon_1, epsilon_2` in those coordinates and attempt continuity from `epsilon=0`.

The source's proof architecture explains exactly why this is not currently perturbative. Its restricted reflection positivity works only in the zero-slope directions. Once a formerly horizontal slope is nonzero, the finite-volume state carries a positive density of macroscopic domain walls; the authors explicitly identify interference among those walls as the obstruction to ordinary Peierls/reflection arguments. At nonintegral slope there is also exponential multiplicity of ground states, so no single ground state supplies a stable expansion point.

Therefore a small numerical slope is not a small local perturbation in the relevant combinatorial variable: in a box of side `L`, a slope `epsilon` forces order `epsilon L` macroscopic walls. The limits `epsilon -> 0` and `L -> infinity` are singular. A deformation proof would need a uniform-in-`L` wall-gas estimate or a new reflection principle that survives positive wall density; the existing source estimate does not contain that ingredient.

Outcome of #6: **the nearest solved point is separated from the general problem by a singular wall-density transition, not by a missing epsilon-sized estimate.**

## 6. Method #27 — SWAP THE QUANTIFIERS: try to refute the universal conjecture first

The full source statement is universal over slopes and a broad class of convex interactions. I therefore reversed the burden: rather than prove all cases, look for one source-valid convex interaction/slope that delocalizes in `d>=3`.

The obvious benchmark families do not supply such a counterexample. The real-valued Gaussian free field localizes in `d>=3` at every slope; Brascamp--Lieb methods cover broad strictly convex real-valued models. In the integer-valued setting, the source reviews localization at zero slope for several `|grad phi|^p`, SOS/Gaussian and Lipschitz regimes, the integer-valued Gaussian free field at integer slopes, and a few special integer-slope interfaces. The paper's separate counterexample to **uniqueness** for a more general periodic interaction does not refute localization and therefore cannot be mislabelled as a counterexample to I0007.

A plausible negation search would have to exploit a very flat/degenerate convex integer interaction or a genuinely nonintegral slope while preserving the source's coercivity and nearest-neighbor assumptions. I did not find an exact candidate whose finite-volume variance can be proved to diverge in `d>=3`. In particular, no finite calculation can by itself refute the asymptotic tightness statement.

Outcome of #27: **no source-valid counterexample found; the failure mode would have to be genuinely infinite-volume rather than a small finite obstruction.**
