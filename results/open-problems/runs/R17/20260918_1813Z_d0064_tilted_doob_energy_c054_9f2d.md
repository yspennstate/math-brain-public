## 4. Setup

Let `M` be any stationary random Radon measure satisfying the lognormal star equation at one fixed scale `epsilon in (0,1)`:

\[
M(A)\ \stackrel{\rm law}=\ \int_A e^{\omega_\epsilon(x)}M^\epsilon(dx),
\qquad
M^\epsilon(A)\ \stackrel{\rm law}=\ M(A/\epsilon),
\]

with `M^epsilon` independent of the stationary Gaussian multiplier `omega_epsilon`.

For a nonnegative compactly supported admissible test function `f`, write

\[
M(f)=\int f\,dM,
\qquad
L(f)=\mathbb E[e^{-M(f)}],
\qquad
\Phi(f)=-\log L(f).
\]

Because `M` is Radon, `M(f)<infinity` almost surely for bounded compactly supported `f`, hence `0<L(f)<=1`.

For a direction `h` satisfying `|h|<=C f`, define the exponentially tilted expectation

\[
\mathbb E_f[X]
:=\frac{\mathbb E[Xe^{-M(f)}]}{L(f)}.
\]

The point of the domination `|h|<=Cf` is that it makes every polynomial moment of `M(h)` finite *under the tilted law*, regardless of the ordinary moments of `M`.

## 5. Method #5 — ADD A PARAMETER AND DIFFERENTIATE: exact moment-free Laplace calculus

### Lemma 1 (positive Laplace tilting creates finite directional moments)

Let `h_1,...,h_k` be bounded compactly supported directions with `|h_j|<=C_j f`. Then for every integer `r>=1`, every product of at most `r` variables `M(h_j)` is integrable against `e^{-M(f)}`. In particular `L` is twice Gateaux differentiable in these directions at `f`, with

\[
D L(f)[h]=-\mathbb E[M(h)e^{-M(f)}],
\]

\[
D^2L(f)[h,k]=\mathbb E[M(h)M(k)e^{-M(f)}].
\]

Consequently

\[
m_f(h):=D\Phi(f)[h]=\mathbb E_f[M(h)],
\]

and

\[
D^2\Phi(f)[h,k]
=-C_f(h,k),
\qquad
C_f(h,k):=\operatorname{Cov}_f(M(h),M(k)).
\]

#### Proof

From `|h_j|<=C_j f`,

\[
|M(h_j)|\le C_j M(f).
\]

Hence a product of `r` such terms times `e^{-M(f)}` is bounded by a constant multiple of

\[
M(f)^r e^{-M(f)}.
\]

The scalar function `x^r e^{-x}` is bounded on `[0,infinity)`, so the tilted product is integrable without any ordinary moment assumption on `M`.

For `|s|` sufficiently small, `f+s h` remains nonnegative. The difference quotients of `e^{-M(f+s h)}` are dominated by constants times `M(f)^r e^{-cM(f)}` for some fixed `c>0`; dominated convergence therefore gives the first and second derivative formulas. Differentiating `Phi=-log L` gives

\[
D\Phi(f)[h]
=-\frac{DL(f)[h]}{L(f)}
=\mathbb E_f[M(h)]
\]

and

\[
D^2\Phi(f)[h,k]
=-\left(
\frac{D^2L(f)[h,k]}{L(f)}
-\frac{DL(f)[h]DL(f)[k]}{L(f)^2}
\right)
=-\operatorname{Cov}_f(M(h),M(k)).
\]

QED.

### A scalar boundary observable

For any fixed nonzero `f>=0`, put `phi_f(q)=Phi(qf)` for `q>0`. Since `q -> phi_f(q)` is increasing and concave with `phi_f(0)=0`,

\[
0\le a_f(q):=\frac{q\phi_f'(q)}{\phi_f(q)}\le1.
\]

The derivative exists for every `q>0` by Lemma 1. If `phi_f(q)` is regularly varying at zero with index `alpha`, then the usual differentiable regular-variation criterion gives `a_f(q)->alpha`. Thus the source's mysterious `alpha in (0,1]` has a natural **moment-free observable at positive Laplace tilt**. Critical `alpha=1` is compatible with slowly varying logarithmic corrections, so this does not incorrectly assume a finite first moment.

I have **not** proved that `a_f(q)` has a limit independent of `f`. Establishing that is one precise form of the missing boundary theorem.

## 6. Star transfer operator

The scaling relation for `M^epsilon` gives, for a fresh copy of `M`,

\[
\int f(x)e^{\omega_\epsilon(x)}M^\epsilon(dx)
\ \stackrel{\rm law}=\
M(G_\omega f),
\]

where

\[
(G_\omega f)(y)
=f(\epsilon y)e^{\omega_\epsilon(\epsilon y)}.
\]

Therefore the Laplace functional satisfies the exact fixed-point identity

\[
L(f)=\mathbb E_\omega[L(G_\omega f)].
\tag{6.1}
\]

The same multiplier acts on a direction:

\[
G_\omega h(y)=h(\epsilon y)e^{\omega_\epsilon(\epsilon y)}.
\]

If `|h|<=Cf`, then `|G_omega h|<=C G_omega f` pathwise, so Lemma 1 remains available after every scale step.

## 7. Method #11 — DUALIZE: the Doob transform of the star equation

Equation (6.1) says that the positive function `L` is harmonic for the scale-transfer operator. Use it as a Doob `h`-function.

For a current test `f`, define a probability law on the next multiplier by

\[
Q_f(d\omega)
:=\frac{L(G_\omega f)}{L(f)}\,P_\omega(d\omega).
\tag{7.1}
\]

It is a probability measure because of (6.1).

### Lemma 2 (tilted first- and second-order star identities)

For directions `h,k` dominated by `f`, one has

\[
m_f(h)
=\mathbb E_{Q_f}\big[m_{Gf}(Gh)\big],
\tag{7.2}
\]

and

\[
C_f(h,k)
=\mathbb E_{Q_f}\big[C_{Gf}(Gh,Gk)\big]
+\operatorname{Cov}_{Q_f}
\big(m_{Gf}(Gh),m_{Gf}(Gk)\big).
\tag{7.3}
\]

#### Proof

Differentiate (6.1) once in direction `h`:

\[
-L(f)m_f(h)
=\mathbb E_\omega[-L(Gf)m_{Gf}(Gh)].
\]

Division by `L(f)` is exactly (7.2).

Differentiating twice gives

\[
L(f)\,\mathbb E_f[M(h)M(k)]
=\mathbb E_\omega\left[
L(Gf)\,\mathbb E_{Gf}[M(Gh)M(Gk)]
\right].
\]

After division by `L(f)`, decompose each second moment into covariance plus product of means. Equation (7.2) identifies the outer means, leaving precisely the law-of-total-covariance formula (7.3). All interchanges are justified by Lemma 1; no untitled first or second moment is used. QED.

This dualized law is the first point in the route where the old `1+delta` barrier genuinely disappears: every positive test `f` supplies its own finite-energy probability law `Q_f`.

## 8. Method #25 — COUNT THE SAME THING TWICE: an exact energy/cocycle theorem

Fix `epsilon` and iterate the same star step. Starting from `(F_0,H_0)=(f,h)`, define a Markov chain by sampling the next multiplier from `Q_{F_n}` and setting

\[
F_{n+1}=G_{\omega_{n+1}}F_n,
\qquad
H_{n+1}=G_{\omega_{n+1}}H_n.
\]

Let

\[
Z_n:=m_{F_n}(H_n),
\qquad
V_n:=C_{F_n}(H_n,H_n),
\]

and

\[
S_n:=\mathbb E_{F_n}[M(H_n)^2]
=V_n+Z_n^2.
\]

### Theorem 3 (tilted Doob-energy identity)

Under the above hypotheses:

1. `(Z_n)` is a martingale.
2. `(S_n)` is a nonnegative martingale.
3. `(Z_n)` is uniformly bounded in `L^2`:

\[
\sup_n\mathbb E[Z_n^2]
\le S_0
=\mathbb E_f[M(h)^2]<\infty.
\tag{8.1}
\]

Hence `Z_n` converges almost surely and in `L^2` to a finite `Z_infinity`.
4. The tilted covariance is an exact finite-energy budget:

\[
V_0
=\mathbb E[V_n]
+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}
\mathbb E[(Z_{j+1}-Z_j)^2].
\tag{8.2}
\]

Consequently

\[
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
\mathbb E[(Z_{j+1}-Z_j)^2]
\le V_0<\infty.
\tag{8.3}
\]

More precisely,

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_n)
=V_0-\mathbb E[V_n],
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Z_\infty)
=V_0-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E[V_n].
\tag{8.4}
\]

For two dominated directions `h,k`, if `Z_n^h,Z_n^k` denote the corresponding martingales driven by the same Doob chain, then

\[
C_f(h,k)-\mathbb E[C_{F_n}(H_n,K_n)]
=\operatorname{Cov}(Z_n^h,Z_n^k)
=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}
\mathbb E[\Delta Z_{j+1}^h\,\Delta Z_{j+1}^k].
\tag{8.5}
\]

#### Proof

Statement 1 is exactly (7.2) applied conditionally at the random state `(F_n,H_n)`.

For the raw tilted second moment, twice differentiating the Laplace fixed-point equation before taking logarithms gives

\[
S_f(h)=\mathbb E_{Q_f}[S_{Gf}(Gh)].
\tag{8.6}
\]

Thus `(S_n)` is a nonnegative martingale, proving statement 2. Since `Z_n^2<=S_n`, taking expectations in (8.6) gives (8.1). The `L^2` martingale convergence theorem yields statement 3.

Now apply (7.3) with `h=k` conditionally at time `n`. Because `Z_n=E[Z_{n+1}|\mathcal F_n]`, the second term in (7.3) is the conditional variance of the next martingale value:

\[
V_n
=\mathbb E[V_{n+1}|\mathcal F_n]
+\mathbb E[(Z_{n+1}-Z_n)^2|\mathcal F_n].
\tag{8.7}
\]

Take expectations and sum from `0` to `n-1`; the covariance terms telescope, giving (8.2) and (8.3).

Also `E[S_n]=S_0=V_0+Z_0^2`, while `E[S_n]=E[V_n]+E[Z_n^2]`. Since `E[Z_n]=Z_0`, subtraction yields

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_n)=V_0-E[V_n].
\]

Pass to the `L^2` limit for (8.4).

Finally, polarize (8.7), or apply (7.3) directly to `h,k`. Martingale increments at distinct times are orthogonal, so the telescoping mixed-covariance identity is exactly (8.5). QED.

### Why this is materially different from another transfer-matrix-style check

The old uniqueness proofs use an untitled `L^{1+delta}` hypothesis to make an iterated conditional-expectation martingale compact. The theorem above shows that **for every `q>0`, exponential tilting by `e^{-qM(f)}` creates an automatically `L^2`-compact scale martingale even in the critical/atomic regimes**. The proof uses only positivity, the star equation, and Radon finiteness on compact sets.

So the moment barrier can be moved from `obtain compactness` to the much more precise boundary problem

\[
\boxed{\text{control the family of tilted boundary martingales uniformly as }q\downarrow0.}
\]

That is a genuinely different route from simply deleting the `1+delta` line in the existing proof.

## 9. What the theorem does and does not give

The theorem does **not** yet imply conditional independent scattering. Equation (8.5) shows exactly what must vanish or converge for separated test sets: the residual fine-scale tilted covariance

\[
\mathbb E[C_{F_n}(H_n,K_n)].
\]

If a source-valid dependence hypothesis can force this residual to vanish for separated `h,k`, then their entire tilted covariance is represented by the accumulated scale-by-scale innovation sum. That is the natural entry point for a Levy--Khintchine / stable-noise disintegration.

But I do not promote that implication here, because the Rhodes--Vargas review phrases the needed condition only as a dependence decay stronger than ergodicity, while Rhodes--Sohier--Vargas gives a quantitative `good` condition on the *multiplier field*. A proof still has to propagate that condition through the low-moment fixed point strongly enough to control the residual term in (8.5).

Likewise, the scalar index

\[
a_f(q)=\frac{qD\Phi(qf)[f]}{\Phi(qf)}\in[0,1]
\]

is an exact finite-`q` observable, but I have not proved a common limit `alpha` as `q downarrow0`. A common limit plus a source-valid asymptotic additivity theorem for disjoint supports would be very close to Rhodes--Sohier--Vargas Conjecture 23 and would feed directly into Conjecture 24 / Rhodes--Vargas 6.4.
