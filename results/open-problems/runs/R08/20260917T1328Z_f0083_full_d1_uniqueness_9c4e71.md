## 4. Setup and the source lemma

By scaling the line, it is enough to prove appetite `alpha=1`. Let `Xi subset R` be discrete (locally finite) and let `psi` be an arbitrary stable allocation with appetite one. Define `F` up to an additive constant by

`F(t)-F(s)=N(s,t]-(t-s),     s<t`,

where `N(s,t]=#(Xi intersect (s,t])`. Thus `F` has slope `-1` off the centers and a jump of size `1` at every center:

`F(xi)=F(xi-)+1`.

For a center `xi`, put

`Gamma_+(xi)=psi^{-1}(xi) intersect (xi,infinity)`,
`Gamma_-(xi)=psi^{-1}(xi) intersect (-infinity,xi)`,

and let their lengths be `gamma_+(xi), gamma_-(xi)`. Their sum is at most one, with equality exactly when `xi` is sated.

HHP Lemma 8 says, without any satedness assumption:

- if `t>xi`, `psi(t)=xi`, and `beta=L(psi^{-1}(xi) intersect [xi,t])`, then
  `F(t)=F(xi)-beta`;
- if `s<xi`, `psi(s)=xi`, and `gamma=L(psi^{-1}(xi) intersect [s,xi])`, then
  `F(s)=F(xi-)+gamma`.

Consequently, modulo null endpoint pathologies, `F` maps the right territory of `xi` measure-preservingly onto

`I_+(xi)=[F(xi)-gamma_+(xi), F(xi)]`,

and the left territory measure-preservingly onto

`I_-(xi)=[F(xi-), F(xi-)+gamma_-(xi)]`.

Because `F(xi)=F(xi-)+1` and `gamma_-+gamma_+<=1`, these intervals have disjoint interiors. Their gap has length

`1-gamma_-(xi)-gamma_+(xi)`,

which is exactly the center's unsated appetite.

This is the stronger replacement for the preceding report's all-sated Proposition-7 step.

## 5. B3 — encode every stable allocation as level-wise stable partial matchings

For a level `u`, define

`C(u)={xi in Xi: F(xi-)<u<F(xi)}`

and

`D(u)={x in R\Xi: F(x)=u}`.

On each component `J` of `R\Xi`, there is a constant `kappa_J` such that

`F(x)=kappa_J-x,  x in J`,

so `D(u)` contains at most one point from each gap and is locally finite.

Exclude a null set of levels consisting of center jump endpoints, images of the allocation's null `Delta`-set, the null/at-most-countable exceptional values where a territorial monotone map has a nonunique inverse, and the distance-tie levels described in Section 7. Call the remaining levels **regular**.

### Lemma 1 — arbitrary stable allocations induce stable partial matchings

For every regular `u`, `psi` induces a bipartite partial matching `M_u^psi` between `C(u)` and `D(u)`:

- pair `xi` to the unique territorial point `x` with `F(x)=u` when `u` lies in `I_-(xi) union I_+(xi)`;
- leave `xi` unmatched when `u` lies in its central gap;
- leave a point of `D(u)` unmatched exactly when that site is unclaimed.

Moreover `M_u^psi` is stable for ordinary Euclidean-distance preferences, with unmatched worst.

**Proof.** The source lemma gives the center-to-level correspondence above. If a regular `x in D(u)` is claimed by `xi`, the same formulas put `u=F(x)` strictly inside the unit jump `(F(xi-),F(xi))`, so `xi in C(u)` and `x` is precisely the corresponding partner. If a center is unmatched at level `u`, its central gap is nonempty, hence its total territory has length `<1`, so the center is unsated.

Suppose a site `x in D(u)` and center `xi in C(u)` block `M_u^psi`. If `x` is unmatched then it is unclaimed in the continuum allocation; otherwise its level partner is exactly `psi(x)`, and the blocking inequality says `x` is closer to `xi` than to `psi(x)`. Thus the continuum site desires `xi`. If `xi` is unmatched then it is unsated and covets every site; otherwise its level partner `y` is an actual territory point of `xi`, and the second blocking inequality says `|x-xi|<|y-xi|`, so `xi` covets `x`. Hence `(x,xi)` would be an unstable continuum pair, contradiction. `square`

So **sated and unsated centers are treated uniformly**: unsatedness becomes an unmatched-center state on exactly the missing range of levels.

## 6. Corrected no-descending-chain theorem on every regular level

Fix `u` and write

`r(c)=F(c)-u in (0,1),     c in C(u)`.

### Lemma 2 — exact integer/fractional-part formula

For `c in C(u)` and `x in D(u)`:

- if `x>c`, then `|x-c|=m+r(c)`, where `m=N(c,x]`;
- if `x<c`, then `|x-c|=m+1-r(c)`, where `m=N(x,c)`.

Because `x` is not a center, in either case `m=floor(|x-c|)` is exactly the number of **other** centers strictly between `c` and `x`.

**Proof.** If `x>c`,

`u-F(c)=N(c,x]-(x-c)=-r(c)`,

so `x-c=N(c,x]+r(c)`. If `x<c`, use

`F(c-)-F(x)=N(x,c)-(c-x)`

and `F(c-)=u+r(c)-1` to obtain `c-x=N(x,c)+1-r(c)`. `square`

### Lemma 3 — no infinite descending alternating chain

There is no sequence

`c_0,x_0,c_1,x_1,...`,  `c_i in C(u)`, `x_i in D(u)`,

for which the successive Euclidean distances

`d_0=|c_0-x_0| > d_1=|x_0-c_1| > d_2=|c_1-x_1| > ... >0`.

**Proof.** The integers `floor(d_j)` form a nonincreasing nonnegative sequence, so on a tail they are all equal to one integer `m`.

At a center `c_i`, the adjacent level sites `x_{i-1}` and `x_i` must lie on opposite sides. If they were on the same side, Lemma 2 and the common floor `m` would give the same fractional part (`r(c_i)` on the right, `1-r(c_i)` on the left), hence equal adjacent distances, contradicting strict decrease.

At a level site `x_i`, the consecutive centers `c_i,c_{i+1}` must also lie on opposite sides. If they were on the same side, the farther center is `c_i` because `d_{2i}>d_{2i+1}`. The open interval from `x_i` to `c_i` then contains the nearer center `c_{i+1}`. But Lemma 2 says the common floor is exactly the number of intervening centers, so the farther edge would have floor at least one larger than the nearer edge, contradicting the common value `m`.

Therefore the chain is eventually geometrically monotone. There are two cases.

If

`c_i < x_i < c_{i+1} < x_{i+1}`,

then, writing `r_i=r(c_i)`,

`d_{2i}=m+r_i`,
`d_{2i+1}=m+1-r_{i+1}`,
`d_{2i+2}=m+r_{i+1}`.

Strict decrease gives

`r_i > 1-r_{i+1} > r_{i+1}`,

so `r_{i+1}<1/2`. Applying the same inequalities one step later gives

`r_{i+1} > 1-r_{i+2} > r_{i+2}`,

hence `r_{i+1}>1/2`, contradiction.

If instead

`c_i > x_i > c_{i+1} > x_{i+1}`,

then

`d_{2i}=m+1-r_i`,
`d_{2i+1}=m+r_{i+1}`,
`d_{2i+2}=m+1-r_{i+1}`.

Thus

`1-r_i > r_{i+1} > 1-r_{i+1}`,

which gives `r_{i+1}>1/2`; the next triple gives `r_{i+1}<1/2`, again a contradiction. `square`

This is the corrected proof. In particular, the preceding report's same-side claim at a level site is replaced by the exact intervening-center count above.

## 7. Strict preferences hold for almost every level

There are only countably many exceptional levels at which some vertex has a distance tie.

Indeed, on a gap `J`, write its level point as `x_J(u)=kappa_J-u`. For a fixed center `c` and two distinct gaps `J,K`,

`|c-x_J(u)|=|c-x_K(u)|`

has at most one solution in `u`; the gap constants `kappa_J` are distinct because crossing each center increments the constant by one. For a fixed gap `J` and distinct centers `c,c'`,

`|x_J(u)-c|=|x_J(u)-c'|`

also has at most one solution. There are countably many centers and gaps, hence only countably many such levels. Add the countable center jump endpoints and the allocation-specific null exceptional values from Section 5.

Thus on a common full-measure set of levels, every vertex of `C(u) union D(u)` has strict preferences.

## 8. Stable partial matching is unique on a regular level

### Lemma 4 — direct symmetric-difference argument

Let `C,D subset R` be locally finite, with strict cross-color distance preferences and no infinite strictly descending alternating chain. Then there is at most one stable partial matching between `C` and `D`.

**Proof.** Suppose `M` and `M'` are distinct. Treat unmatched as distance `infinity`. Choose a vertex `z_0` with different status/partners; by strictness, after swapping the names of `M,M'` if needed,

`|z_0-M(z_0)| < |z_0-M'(z_0)|`.

Put `z_1=M(z_0)`. Since `z_0` prefers `z_1` to its `M'` partner/status, stability of `M'` forces `z_1` to be matched under `M'` to a point `z_2` satisfying

`|z_1-z_2| < |z_1-z_0|`.

Now `z_1` prefers `z_2` to its `M` partner `z_0`, so stability of `M` forces `z_2` to be matched under `M` to `z_3` with

`|z_2-z_3| < |z_2-z_1|`.

Alternating the same argument produces an infinite alternating chain of strictly decreasing distances. Strict decrease prevents a terminating unmatched step and prevents a cycle. This contradicts the hypothesis. `square`

This is the deterministic core of the standard mutually-closest/descending-chain argument, but it also handles unmatched vertices directly.

## 9. Main theorem — all one-dimensional stable Lebesgue allocations are unique

### Theorem 5

Let `Xi subset R` be any discrete set and let `alpha in (0,infinity)` be finite. If `psi_1,psi_2` are stable Lebesgue allocations to `Xi` with appetite `alpha`, then

`psi_1 = psi_2` Lebesgue-a.e.

No hypothesis that all centers are sated, all sites are claimed, or that `Xi` is stationary/recurrent is needed.

**Proof.** Scale space by `alpha^{-1}` and reduce to appetite one. The function `F` depends only on `Xi`, not on the allocation.

For each `psi_j`, Lemma 1 produces, for almost every regular level `u`, a stable partial matching `M_u^{psi_j}` on the **same** bipartite sets `C(u),D(u)`. Intersect the two full-measure sets of regular levels. Lemmas 3 and 4 imply

`M_u^{psi_1}=M_u^{psi_2}`

for almost every `u`.

Now fix a component `J` of `R\Xi`. On `J`,

`F(x)=kappa_J-x`,

so `x -> u=F(x)` is an orientation-reversing isometry from `J` onto `F(J)` and preserves one-dimensional Lebesgue measure. For almost every `x in J`, the corresponding regular level has the same level partner (or the same unmatched status) under both allocations. Therefore `psi_1(x)=psi_2(x)` for almost every `x in J`.

A discrete subset of `R` is countable, hence `R\Xi` has only countably many components. Taking their union and adding the null center set gives a.e. equality on all of `R`. Undo the scaling. `square`
