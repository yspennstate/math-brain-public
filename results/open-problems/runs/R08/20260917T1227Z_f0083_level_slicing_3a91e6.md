## 3. B23 — attack the previous route's hidden restriction first

The previous active-ball reduction deliberately worked in the subclass where **all centers are sated and all territorial radii are finite**. That was useful, but it is not source-equivalent for an arbitrary deterministic center set. The source definition permits unsated centers, and the bounded-territory theorem in the HHP papers is tied to their stationary point-process setting rather than an arbitrary deterministic `Xi`.

The proposer also gave a non-Lebesgue atomic example in one dimension with two stable allocations. The structural feature worth preserving is not the atomicity alone: an unsated-center mechanism can participate in nonuniqueness. So instead of further polishing the all-sated active-ball equations, I first asked exactly what stability forces around an **unsated** center.

### Lemma 1 — an unsated center owns its strict Voronoi cell

Let `psi` be any stable Lebesgue allocation with finite appetite `alpha>0` to a discrete `Xi subset R^d`. If a center `xi` is unsated, then for Lebesgue-a.e.

`x in Vor^o(xi) := {x: |x-xi| < |x-eta| for all eta != xi}`

we have `psi(x)=xi`.

**Proof.** Suppose instead that a positive-measure set of such sites is not assigned to `xi`. Outside the allocation's null exceptional set, take such an `x`. If `x` is unclaimed, it desires every center, in particular `xi`. If it is assigned to `eta != xi`, strict Voronoi membership gives

`|x-xi| < |x-eta| = |x-psi(x)|`,

so again `x` desires `xi`. Since `xi` is unsated, by definition it covets every site. Thus `(x,xi)` is an unstable pair, contradiction. `square`

Hence

`Leb(Vor^o(xi)) <= alpha`

for every unsated center. In particular, if the strict Voronoi cell has volume at least `alpha`, that center cannot be unsated.

This is a useful correction to the previous route: a source counterexample need not live in the all-sated radius system at all. In one dimension, an unsated mechanism is geometrically possible only at a center whose local Voronoi interval is too small to fill its appetite. The source's atomic example therefore pointed toward a genuine mechanism that the active-ball route had excluded by design.

## 4. B18 — rewrite the one-dimensional continuum allocation as stable matchings on levels

I now switch to the all-sated `d=1` subclass and use the 2009 HHP function as a change of language.

By scaling space, it suffices to take appetite `alpha=1`. Let `Xi subset R` be discrete and define `F` up to an additive constant by

`F(t)-F(s) = N(s,t] - (t-s),     s<t`,

where `N(s,t] = #(Xi intersect (s,t])`.

Thus `F` has slope `-1` off the centers and an upward jump of size `1` at every center. Write

`F(xi)=F(xi-)+1`.

For a real level `u`, define the upcrossing centers

`C(u) = {xi in Xi : F(xi-) < u < F(xi)}`

and the downcrossing sites

`D(u) = {x in R\Xi : F(x)=u}`.

Because `Xi` is locally finite, both sets are discrete and locally finite. On every component `J` of `R\Xi`,

`F(x)=kappa_J-x`,

so `D(u)` contains at most one point from each gap.

Call a level **regular** if it avoids all center jump endpoints and the countable tie set specified in section 6 below.

### Lemma 2 — a stable all-sated allocation induces a stable bipartite matching on almost every level

Let `psi` be a stable appetite-one allocation to `Xi` in which every center is sated. Then for Lebesgue-a.e. regular `u`, `psi` induces a bipartite partial matching `m_u` between `C(u)` and `D(u)` such that every center in `C(u)` is matched; unmatched points, if any, lie on the `D(u)` side. Moreover `m_u` is stable for ordinary Euclidean-distance preferences.

**Proof.** Fix a center `xi`. HHP Proposition 3.2 and its proof split the territory into

`Gamma_+ = psi^{-1}(xi) intersect (xi,infinity)`,
`Gamma_- = psi^{-1}(xi) intersect (-infinity,xi)`.

If `gamma_+ = Leb(Gamma_+)` and `gamma_- = Leb(Gamma_-)`, then `gamma_-+gamma_+=1`; the restriction of `F` to `Gamma_+` is monotone decreasing and measure-preserving onto the interval

`[F(xi)-gamma_+, F(xi)]`,

while its restriction to `Gamma_-` is monotone decreasing and measure-preserving onto

`[F(xi-), F(xi-)+gamma_-]`.

These two intervals have disjoint interiors and meet at one endpoint because `F(xi)=F(xi-)+1`. Therefore, outside a null set of levels, every `u in (F(xi-),F(xi))` has exactly one territory representative `x` with `F(x)=u`. Match `xi` to this `x`. Since the centers are countable, one common full-measure set of levels works for all centers.

Conversely, if a regular `x in D(u)` is assigned by `psi` to a center `xi`, HHP Lemma 4.3 gives

`F(x) in [F(xi-),F(xi)]`.

Regularity excludes endpoints, so `xi in C(u)`. Hence every assigned `D(u)` point is exactly a level partner; the unmatched `D(u)` points are unclaimed sites (modulo null allocation values).

It remains to check stability. Suppose `x in D(u)` and `xi in C(u)` block the induced matching. Let `eta=m_u(x)` if `x` is matched, and let `y=m_u(xi)` be `xi`'s current level partner. Then

`|x-xi| < |x-eta|` (or `x` is unmatched)

and

`|x-xi| < |y-xi|`.

The first inequality says the continuum site `x` desires `xi`; the second says `xi` covets `x`, because `y` is an actual territory site of `xi` farther away. Thus `(x,xi)` would be an unstable continuum pair, contradiction. `square`

So the continuum problem has become a continuum of ordinary, symmetric-distance, two-color stable-matching problems.

## 5. B30 — one level at a time: prove every regular level has no descending chain

This is the key new step. Fix a regular level `u`. For `c in C(u)`, put

`r(c)=F(c)-u in (0,1)`.

The special one-dimensional form of `F` quantizes every center-to-level-site distance.

### Lemma 3 — integer/fractional-part formula

For `c in C(u)` and `x in D(u)`:

- if `x>c`, then `|x-c| = m + r(c)` for some integer `m>=0`;
- if `x<c`, then `|x-c| = m + 1-r(c)` for some integer `m>=0`.

**Proof.** If `x>c`, then

`u-F(c)=N(c,x]-(x-c)`.

The left side equals `-r(c)`, so `x-c=N(c,x]+r(c)`. If `x<c`, use

`F(c-)-F(x)=N(x,c)-(c-x)`

and `F(c-)=u+r(c)-1` to obtain `c-x=N(x,c)+1-r(c)`. `square`

### Lemma 4 — no infinite descending chain on a regular level

There is no infinite alternating sequence

`c_0,x_0,c_1,x_1,...`,  with `c_i in C(u)`, `x_i in D(u)`,

whose successive distances are strictly decreasing.

**Proof.** Write

`d_{2i}=|c_i-x_i|`,
`d_{2i+1}=|x_i-c_{i+1}|`,

and suppose `d_0>d_1>d_2>...>0`.

The integer sequence `floor(d_n)` is nonincreasing and nonnegative, so after deleting a finite initial segment it is constant, say equal to `k`. Work on that tail.

**Step 1: at each center, the incoming and outgoing level sites lie on opposite sides.** If `x_{i-1}` and `x_i` were on the same side of `c_i`, Lemma 3 would give both adjacent distances the same integer part `k` and the same fractional part (`r(c_i)` on the right or `1-r(c_i)` on the left). They would be equal, contradicting strict decrease.

**Step 2: at each level site, the consecutive centers lie on the same side.** Suppose first

`c_i < x_i < c_{i+1}`.

By Step 1, `x_{i-1}<c_i` and `x_{i+1}>c_{i+1}`. Hence the strict decrease at `c_i` gives

`k+1-r(c_i) > k+r(c_i)`,

so `r(c_i)<1/2`; similarly `r(c_{i+1})<1/2`. But the decrease across `x_i` gives

`k+r(c_i) > k+1-r(c_{i+1})`,

or `r(c_i)+r(c_{i+1})>1`, contradiction.

The case

`c_i > x_i > c_{i+1}`

is symmetric. Step 1 then gives `r(c_i)>1/2` and `r(c_{i+1})>1/2`, while decrease across `x_i` gives

`1-r(c_i)>r(c_{i+1})`,

again impossible.

Thus `c_i` and `c_{i+1}` lie on the same side of `x_i`. Since `d_{2i}>d_{2i+1}`, the nearer center `c_{i+1}` lies strictly between `c_i` and `x_i`, and therefore

`|c_{i+1}-c_i| = d_{2i}-d_{2i+1}`.

Summing,

`sum_i |c_{i+1}-c_i| <= sum_n (d_n-d_{n+1}) <= d_0 < infinity`.

Hence `(c_i)` is Cauchy. But `Xi` is locally finite, so a Cauchy sequence of its points is eventually constant. Consecutive centers cannot be equal because that would make the two edges through `x_i` equal, contradicting strict decrease. Contradiction. `square`

This completely closes the descending-chain obstruction for every fixed regular `F`-level; it is not a finite numerical experiment.

## 6. Tie removal and deterministic stable-matching uniqueness

The published `Poisson matching` Lemma 15 assumes a discrete non-equidistant set with no descending chains and concludes that the stable partial matching is unique and produced by iterated mutually closest pairs. Here we only need the weaker pointwise condition that every vertex have a unique closest remaining opposite-color vertex. The same proof works, and regular levels satisfy it outside a countable exceptional set.

### Lemma 5 — only countably many levels have a relevant distance tie

Index the components of `R\Xi` by `J`; on `J`, `D(u)` has point

`x_J(u)=kappa_J-u`

when the level crosses that gap.

For a fixed center `c` and two distinct gap points `x_J(u),x_K(u)`, the equality

`|c-x_J(u)|=|c-x_K(u)|`

holds for at most one `u` (apart from the impossible identity `kappa_J=kappa_K` for distinct ordered gaps). For a fixed gap point and two distinct centers `c,c'`,

`|x_J(u)-c|=|x_J(u)-c'|`

also holds for at most one `u`. There are only countably many centers and gaps. Adding the countable set of center jump endpoints gives a countable exceptional set `E`.

Thus every `u notin E` has strict distance preferences at each vertex.

### Lemma 6 — mutual-closest algorithm under pointwise strictness

Let `C,D subset R` be disjoint, discrete, locally finite two-color sets. Assume:

1. each vertex has strict distances to distinct opposite-color vertices; and
2. there is no infinite strictly descending alternating chain.

Then there is a unique stable partial matching between `C` and `D`; it is produced by repeatedly matching mutually closest opposite-color pairs. All unmatched vertices, if any, have one color.

**Proof.** At every stage, a mutually closest pair is forced in every stable partial matching: if they were not matched to each other, each would be unmatched or matched to a strictly farther surviving opposite-color vertex, making the pair blocking. By induction every pair removed by the algorithm is therefore forced.

Let `S_C,S_D` be the survivors after all finite stages. If both are nonempty, start with any survivor of one color and alternately move to its unique closest survivor of the opposite color. No consecutive pair can be mutually closest, because local finiteness implies such a pair would become mutually closest after the finitely many nearer blockers in its two bounded neighborhoods were removed, and hence would have been matched at a finite stage. Therefore every second nearest-neighbor step is strictly shorter than the preceding step, producing an infinite descending alternating chain, contradiction. Hence survivors have at most one color.

The forced algorithmic pairs together with unmatched one-color survivors form a stable partial matching: if two opposite-color vertices blocked it, consider the first finite stage at which one of them was matched. The other was then still present, while the matched vertex chose a partner no farther away, contradicting strict blocking. Since every stable partial matching must contain every forced algorithmic pair and cannot match one-color survivors, it is unique. `square`

This is the deterministic core of HPPS Lemma 15 with exactly the tie hypothesis needed here.

Combining Lemmas 4--6, **every regular level has a unique stable partial matching**.

## 7. Main restricted theorem: all-sated stable allocations are unique in one dimension

### Theorem 7

Let `Xi subset R` be any discrete set and let appetite be `alpha=1`. If `psi_1` and `psi_2` are stable Lebesgue allocations to `Xi` and every center is sated under both allocations, then

`psi_1 = psi_2` Lebesgue-a.e.

By scaling the line, the same statement holds for every finite appetite `alpha>0`.

**Proof.** Construct the common function `F` from `Xi`; it does not depend on the allocation. For almost every regular level `u`, Lemma 2 turns each `psi_j` into a stable partial matching `m_{j,u}` between the same sets `C(u),D(u)`. Lemmas 4--6 imply that this stable partial matching is unique, hence

`m_{1,u}=m_{2,u}`

for almost every `u`.

Now take a component `J` of `R\Xi`. There is a constant `kappa_J` with

`F(x)=kappa_J-x,  x in J`.

Thus `x -> u=F(x)` is an isometry (orientation reversing) from `J` onto `F(J)` and preserves one-dimensional Lebesgue measure. At every regular `u`, the unique point `x_J(u)=kappa_J-u` has the same level-match status and partner under `psi_1` and `psi_2`. Therefore

`Leb{x in J: psi_1(x) != psi_2(x)}=0`.

A discrete subset of `R` is countable, so `R\Xi` has countably many components. Taking their union and adding the null center set proves a.e. equality. `square`

### Corollary 8 — what a one-dimensional F0083 counterexample must look like

If F0083 has a counterexample in `d=1`, then **at least one of the two stable allocations has an unsated center**. By Lemma 1, every such unsated center must have strict Voronoi interval length at most the appetite.

This is a real restriction on the source counterexample class. It also shows why the previous all-sated finite-radius route, although mathematically coherent, can never by itself find a one-dimensional counterexample.

## 8. Stress checks and limitations

I checked the new argument against the main possible failure modes.

1. **This does not assume stationarity.** `Xi` is an arbitrary deterministic discrete subset of the line. Stationary uniqueness is stronger in a different direction and already known from the source.
2. **This does not assume territories are bounded.** Proposition 3.2's level map only needs stability, appetite one, and sated centers. The proof above never introduces a finite territorial radius.
3. **This does not claim all `D(u)` sites are matched.** Unclaimed level sites are allowed. Lemma 6 is a partial-matching statement, exactly as required.
4. **Ties are not ignored.** They occur on at most a countable set of levels and therefore do not affect the a.e. continuum conclusion.
5. **The no-descending-chain proof is deterministic.** It uses the integer/fractional-part form forced by the HHP level function, not a Poisson or ergodic assumption.
6. **The result is restricted.** It says nothing directly about a pair where one allocation has an unsated center, and nothing about `d>=2`. Therefore F0083 is not solved or refuted.
7. **Novelty is unverified.** The theorem may be an implicit or folklore synthesis of HHP Proposition 3.2 and HPPS Lemma 15. The fresh bounded search did not locate this exact deterministic all-sated 1D statement, but a negative search is not a publication-level novelty certificate.

No exact-arithmetic checker is appropriate here: there is no finite core or finite exhaustive claim. The theorem-sized claim is instead written in full on the page, as required by the protocol.
