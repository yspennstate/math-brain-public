## 4. B18 — say the contact problem in cavity-message language

For the `q=3` ACG functional, change variables from `x in [0,1]` to

\[
t=\frac{1-x}{3}\in[0,1/3],\qquad x=1-3t.
\tag{4.1}
\]

For a finite incoming multiset `N={t_i}`, put

\[
A(N)=\prod_i(1-t_i),\quad
B(N)=\prod_i(1-2t_i),\quad
C(N)=\prod_i(1-3t_i),
\tag{4.2}
\]

so the vertex factor is

\[
S(N)=3A(N)-3B(N)+C(N).
\tag{4.3}
\]

Adding one message `t` is exactly affine:

\[
S(N+\delta_t)
=S(N)-3t\,[A(N)-2B(N)+C(N)].
\tag{4.4}
\]

Define the outgoing cavity message

\[
\boxed{
\mathscr R(N):=
\frac{A(N)-2B(N)+C(N)}{3A(N)-3B(N)+C(N)}.}
\tag{4.5}
\]

Then, with `F=log S`, equation (4.4) gives

\[
\boxed{F(N+\delta_t)-F(N)=\log(1-3t\mathscr R(N)).}
\tag{4.6}
\]

The edge kernel has exactly the same form:

\[
1-\frac{(1-x)(1-y)}3=1-3ts.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Thus the apparent mismatch between the Poisson vertex term and quadratic edge term disappears in message coordinates.

### Lemma 4.1 — the cavity map stays in the message interval

For every finite multiset `N` with `t_i in [0,1/3]`,

\[
0\le\mathscr R(N)\le\frac13.
\tag{4.8}
\]

**Proof.** Let

\[
f(u)=\prod_i(1-u t_i).
\]

For `1<=u<3`, all factors are nonnegative and

\[
f''(u)=2f(u)\sum_{i<j}
\frac{t_it_j}{(1-ut_i)(1-ut_j)}\ge0,
\]
with endpoint cases obtained by continuity. Hence convexity on `[1,3]` yields

\[
A-2B+C=f(1)-2f(2)+f(3)\ge0.
\tag{4.9}
\]

Also `0<=C<=B`, so

\[
[3A-3B+C]-3[A-2B+C]=3B-2C\ge0.
\tag{4.10}
\]

Since `S(N)>0`, (4.9)--(4.10) imply (4.8). QED.

Now let `a` be a probability law on `[0,1/3]` and `N_a` a Poisson point process of intensity `d a`. Define the canonical cavity operator

\[
\mathcal C_d(a):=\operatorname{Law}(\mathscr R(N_a)).
\tag{4.11}
\]

Lemma 4.1 shows that this is again a probability law on `[0,1/3]`.

The ACG functional is

\[
\Sigma_d(a)=\mathbb E\log S(N_a)
-\frac d2\iint\log(1-3st)\,a(ds)a(dt).
\tag{4.12}
\]

Using (4.6), the mass-insertion/contact function from the preceding runs becomes **exactly**

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_a(t)=
\int\log(1-3ts)\,\mathcal C_d(a)(ds)
-
\int\log(1-3ts)\,a(ds).}
\tag{4.13}
\]

Accordingly the previous first-variation identity becomes

\[
D\Sigma_d(a)[\delta_t-a]
=d\left(\Phi_a(t)-\int\Phi_a(s)a(ds)\right).
\tag{4.14}
\]

Equation (4.13) is the main structural advance of this run: the dual contact residual is precisely a logarithmic-transform discrepancy between the outgoing cavity law and the incoming law.

## 5. B27 — strengthen stationarity to an exact distributional fixed point

The stronger equation suggested by (4.13) is

\[
\boxed{a=\mathcal C_d(a).}
\tag{5.1}
\]

Every solution of (5.1) has `Phi_a(t)=0` for all `t` and therefore vanishing first variation in every mass-transfer direction. More importantly, for accumulating-support stationary laws the converse holds.

### Theorem 5.1 — accumulating-support stationarity forces cavity self-consistency

Suppose `a` is stationary for all probability-mass transfer directions of (4.12), and the support of `a` has an accumulation point. Then

\[
a=\mathcal C_d(a).
\tag{5.2}
\]

**Proof.** By (4.14), stationarity implies that `Phi_a` is equal to a common contact level `c` on the support of `a` (equivalently, equality holds `a`-a.e.; continuity promotes it to the support). Because the support has an accumulation point, the real-analytic function `Phi_a-c` has an accumulating zero set and is identically zero.

Analyticity is immediate on a neighborhood of `[0,1/3]`: for `0<=s,t<=1/3`, `1-3st>=2/3`. Hence

\[
\Phi_a(t)
=-\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{3^k t^k}{k}
\left[
\int s^k\,\mathcal C_d(a)(ds)-\int s^k\,a(ds)
\right].
\tag{5.3}
\]

Also `Phi_a(0)=0`, so the constant identity `Phi_a\equiv c` forces `c=0`. Therefore every coefficient in (5.3) vanishes. The two measures have identical moments of every order. The Hausdorff moment problem on the compact interval `[0,1/3]` is determinate, hence `a=\mathcal C_d(a)`. QED.

This theorem separates two possible optimizer geometries cleanly:

1. an infinite/continuous-support stationary law is an exact cavity fixed point;
2. a finite-support stationary witness may have only a discrete contact set and should be understood as a finite-dimensional approximation to the distributional equation rather than an arbitrary atom fit.

No claim is made that all fixed points are global minimizers, that a nontrivial fixed point exists at every `d`, or that (5.1) by itself proves threshold-location convergence.
