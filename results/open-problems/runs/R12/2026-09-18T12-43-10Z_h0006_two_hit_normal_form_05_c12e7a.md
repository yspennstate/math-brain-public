# 4. Method #6 — DEFORM TO A POINT YOU CAN SOLVE: from bond percolation to a two-hit cascade

The preceding run identified ordinary bond percolation on a random `d`-regular graph as the solvable finite-graph completion of the one-type Bethe skeleton. This run deforms that model in the smallest direction that an interacting threshold cascade naturally supplies: a vertex can respond differently to its **second distinct failed-neighbor contact** than to its first.

Fix `d>=3`. Consider the configuration-model exploration of a component. Let

- `p_N` = probability that a previously untouched vertex activates on its first failed-neighbor contact;
- `q_N` = conditional probability that a vertex which survived one failed-neighbor contact activates on its second distinct failed-neighbor contact;
- `A_j` = number of active failed half-edges after `j` pairings;
- `U_j` = number of untouched vertices;
- `W_j` = number of vertices contacted exactly once by the failed cluster and still inactive.

This is a reduced two-hit model, **not** an assertion that the source SDE has history-independent scalar `q_N`. The point is to isolate the minimal state that disappears on the infinite tree and can reappear at finite size.

Tune first-contact criticality by

\[
(d-1)p_N=1+\lambda N^{-1/3}+o(N^{-1/3}). \tag{4.1}
\]

Write `p_c=1/(d-1)` and assume `q_N->q`.

## Proposition 4.1 — two-hit exploration drift

Uniformly for `j<=T N^{2/3}` on any fixed `T` before truncating the exploration at a macroscopic exceptional event,

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E[\Delta A_j\mid\mathcal F_j]
=
\lambda N^{-1/3}
+
\Gamma_d(q)\frac{j}{N}
+o_{\mathbb P}(N^{-1/3}),
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

with

\[
\boxed{
\Gamma_d(q)=\frac{d-2}{d}\big((d-2)q-1\big).
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

Moreover, at exact first-contact criticality,

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta A_j\mid\mathcal F_j)=d-2+o_{\mathbb P}(1).
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

### Proof

When the partner half-edge belongs to an untouched vertex, activation leaves `d-1` other half-edges on that vertex. The chosen active half-edge is consumed, so

\[
\Delta A_j=
\begin{cases}
 d-2,&\text{with probability }p_N,\\
 -1,&\text{with probability }1-p_N.
\end{cases}
\]

Hence the untouched-vertex mean increment is

\[
\mu_U=(d-1)p_N-1
=\lambda N^{-1/3}+o(N^{-1/3}). \tag{4.5}
\]

When the partner belongs to `W_j`, that vertex has already used one incident half-edge. If it activates on the second contact, `d-2` half-edges remain to be inserted in the frontier; if it does not activate, no new active half-edge is created. Thus

\[
\Delta A_j=
\begin{cases}
 d-3,&\text{with probability }q_N,\\
 -1,&\text{with probability }1-q_N,
\end{cases}
\]

and

\[
\mu_W=(d-2)q_N-1. \tag{4.6}
\]

For `j=O(N^{2/3})`, standard bounded-increment exploration bookkeeping gives

\[
U_j=N-j+O_{\mathbb P}(j^2/N+\sqrt j), \tag{4.7}
\]

because every first contact removes one untouched vertex, while repeat contacts contribute only the birthday-scale correction. Likewise

\[
W_j=(1-p_c)j+O_{\mathbb P}(j^2/N+\sqrt j). \tag{4.8}
\]

After `j` pairings the total number of unmatched half-edges is `dN-2j+O(1)`. Therefore

\[
\Pr(\text{partner in }W_j\mid\mathcal F_j)
=
\frac{(d-1)W_j}{dN-2j+O(1)}
=
\frac{d-2}{d}\frac{j}{N}+o_{\mathbb P}(N^{-1/3}), \tag{4.9}
\]

and

\[
\Pr(\text{partner untouched}\mid\mathcal F_j)
=1-rac{d-2}{d}\frac{j}{N}+o_{\mathbb P}(N^{-1/3}). \tag{4.10}
\]

The probability of hitting the current active set or a vertex already contacted at least twice is `O_{\mathbb P}(N^{-2/3})` on this scale; bounded increments make its contribution negligible in (4.2). Combining (4.5)--(4.10) yields (4.2)--(4.3).

At `p_c`, the untouched-contact increment has mean zero and second moment

\[
\frac{1}{d-1}(d-2)^2+rac{d-2}{d-1}=d-2. \tag{4.11}
\]

Repeat-contact events occur with probability `O(N^{-1/3})`, so they do not change the limiting one-step quadratic variation. This proves (4.4). QED.

### 4.1 The solvable point and the deformation

For ordinary independent bond percolation, the second contact has the same activation probability as the first:

\[
q=p_c=\frac1{d-1}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\Gamma_d(p_c)=-\frac{d-2}{d(d-1)}<0,
}
\tag{4.12}
\]

which is the negative parabolic depletion drift underlying the ordinary `N^{-1/3}` critical window.

Turning on cooperative second-contact response moves this coefficient linearly. It vanishes exactly at

\[
\boxed{
q_*=\frac1{d-2}.
}
\tag{4.13}
\]

For `d>=4`, this is a genuine interior probability. For `d=3`, `q_*=1` lies at the endpoint.

Thus even after the one-parent criticality condition `rho=1` is frozen, the finite-size normal form has three regimes:

\[
\Gamma_d(q)<0\quad\text{ordinary stabilizing curvature},
\]

\[
\Gamma_d(q)=0\quad\text{quadratic/tricritical cancellation},
\]

\[
\Gamma_d(q)>0\quad\text{cooperative finite-size instability at the one-parent threshold}.
\]

This is the key deformation result of the run.

## 4.2 Brownian-parabolic normal form for the reduced model

Because the increments are bounded, (4.2)--(4.4) feed directly into the martingale functional CLT for the standard configuration-model exploration. On the scale

\[
j=\lfloor tN^{2/3}\rfloor,\qquad A_j=N^{1/3}\,\mathcal A_N(t),
\]

the driving exploration process has the normal form

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal A_N(t)
\Longrightarrow
\sqrt{d-2}\,B_t+\lambda t+\frac{\Gamma_d(q)}2t^2,
}
\tag{4.14}
\]

with the usual reflection/restart convention when one explores all components. I use (4.14) only for the reduced two-hit model; I do **not** transplant it to Kumar's interacting diffusion without proving the needed response-kernel and coupling estimates.

# 5. Method #12 — FIND WHAT IS CONSERVED: the Perron coordinate cancels the tree drift, exposing a quadratic response tensor

The one-type calculation suggests the correct finite-type invariant. Let the critical mean matrix satisfy

\[
Mh=h,\qquad \pi M=\pi,\qquad \pi h=1.
\tag{5.1}
\]

At the tree level, projection of the exploration frontier onto the critical Perron coordinate kills the linear mean drift at `rho(M)=1`. This is the finite-type analogue of the one-type identity `(d-1)p_c-1=0`.

Finite-graph repeat contacts then appear one order later. Abstractly, if `x` is the small vector of front exposure densities and the conditional drift map of the finite-population exploration has an expansion

\[
F(x)= (M-I)x+B(x,x)+O(\|x\|^3), \tag{5.2}
\]

then on the critical ray `x=th` the Perron projection obeys

\[
\pi F(th)
=
\boxed{\Gamma_2 t^2+O(t^3)},
\qquad
\boxed{\Gamma_2:=\pi B(h,h)}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

The scalar `Gamma_2` is therefore the finite-type replacement for (4.3). The first-generation matrix `M` determines whether the tree is subcritical/critical/supercritical, but **it does not by itself determine the finite-size critical normal form**. One needs the two-contact response encoded in `B`.

For Kumar's interacting SDE, `B` should not be guessed. A second failed neighbor can change the conditional first-passage law through additive forcing, elapsed time and inherited hazard type. The object to derive is consequently a type/history-resolved two-contact kernel, followed by its Perron contraction `pi B(h,h)`.

This is the conserved-quantity payoff: the Perron projection removes the already-solved one-parent term and isolates exactly the next source-valid coefficient that layer 3 needs.

# 6. Method #24 — FIND THE EXTREMAL OBJECT: the leading bad history is a second contact, not a surplus cycle inside the failed cluster

The previous run emphasized surplus edges in critical finite components. That is enough to show that direct infinite-tree TV transfer cannot resolve finite-size scaling, but it does not identify the dominant history seen by the *front*.

The right count is more revealing. Suppose `s` first-contact explorations have occurred in a sparse configuration model.

A previously contacted but inactive vertex occupies a fraction `Theta(s/N)` of available half-edges. Summing over `s` attempts gives

\[
\boxed{
\#\{\text{second contacts to weakened vertices}\}
=\Theta_{\mathbb P}(s^2/N).
}
\tag{6.1}
\]

At the ordinary critical component scale `s=N^{2/3}`,

\[
\boxed{s^2/N=N^{1/3}.}
\tag{6.2}
\]

But `N^{1/3}` is exactly the active-front fluctuation scale. Thus a history in which a vertex receives a second failed-neighbor contact is not an `o(1)` nuisance. There are order `N^{1/3}` such events in a critical exploration, each capable of changing the frontier by order one.

By contrast, vertices reached for a **third** time occur at the next birthday order

\[
\boxed{s^3/N^2=O(1)}
\tag{6.3}
\]

when `s=N^{2/3}`. Hence, in the ordinary class, two-hit histories are the unique leading repeat-contact mechanism; three-hit and higher histories are lower order.

This distinction matters. A critical component may have only `O(1)` internal surplus edges while the exploration has already generated `Theta(N^{1/3})` contacts to externally weakened vertices. Those contacts can later join the cluster if the second forcing triggers failure. The frontier remembers them even though the eventual failed subgraph need not yet contain a cycle.

### 6.1 Connection to the source's proposed front-width repair

The source suggests that a critical-window front-decoupling theorem should accumulate error over a **front width** rather than over the full cluster volume. The count above explains why that scale is natural:

\[
|C|\asymp N^{2/3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sqrt{|C|}\asymp N^{1/3}
\asymp \#\{\text{leading second contacts}\}.
\tag{6.4}
\]

However, (6.4) also shows that not every front-width contribution is an “echo error” that should be forced to zero. A second failed neighbor creates a genuine finite-population interaction. Even if the one-parent front-decoupling error is made `o(N^{-1/3})` per relevant scale, the aggregate second-contact response can survive in the limiting drift through `Gamma_2`.

Thus the finite-size theorem needs **both**:

1. a quantitative front-decoupling/error bound strong enough that approximation errors vanish on the chosen critical scale;
2. retention and calculation of the genuine two-contact response rather than deleting it as an error.

**Outcome of method #24:** success. The extremal history at ordinary mean-field finite size is “contacted once and not failed, then contacted again.” This identifies the state variable that the one-parent GW skeleton cannot see.

# 7. Tricritical power counting and a prior-art consistency check

Equation (4.13) opens a second finite-size scale. If the quadratic Perron coefficient vanishes and the first stabilizing term is cubic, then the generic exploration drift per step is of order `(j/N)^2`. For an excursion of length `s`,

- martingale fluctuation is `sqrt(s)`;
- cumulative cubic drift is `s^3/N^2`.

Balancing them gives

\[
\sqrt{s}\asymp s^3/N^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{s\asymp N^{4/5}}.
\tag{7.1}
\]

A near-critical linear drift `epsilon s` competes with `sqrt(s)` at this scale precisely when

\[
\boxed{\epsilon\asymp N^{-2/5}}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

More generally, if the first nonzero stabilizing per-step drift is order `(j/N)^m`, the same balance gives

\[
\boxed{
s\asymp N^{2m/(2m+1)},
\qquad
|1-\rho|\asymp N^{-m/(2m+1)}.
}
\tag{7.3}
\]

For `m=1`, this is the ordinary `N^{2/3}` / `N^{-1/3}` window. For `m=2`, it is `N^{4/5}` / `N^{-2/5}`.

This power count is not being advertised as new: the generalized-epidemic-process literature already has ordinary and tricritical mean-field finite-size classes. In particular Chung--Baek--Ha--Jeong report `bar nu=3` in the ordinary continuous regime and `bar nu=5/2` at tricriticality, i.e. control-parameter windows `N^{-1/3}` and `N^{-2/5}`. The agreement is a strong sanity check on the two-hit normal form, not a novelty claim.

For H0006 the implication is conceptual but important:

\[
\boxed{
rho(M)=1\text{ locates one-parent criticality, but the sign of }\Gamma_2
\text{ determines the leading finite-size universality class.}
}
\tag{7.4}
\]

Unless the source SDE forces `Gamma_2<0` throughout the intended parameter regime, there cannot be one universal `N^{-1/3}` layer-3 theorem stated only in terms of `rho(M)` and `N`.
