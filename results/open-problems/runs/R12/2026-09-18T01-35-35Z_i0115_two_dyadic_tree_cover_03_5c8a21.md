## 3. Method #7 — TAKE THE LIMIT: a single stationary dyadic center tree has an exact logarithmic mean-length divergence

The previous two runs found the same critical phenomenon in the Poisson-line language: one first-separation scale has a `1/t` inverse-scale singularity. I changed category to the simplest random hierarchical tree where the entire tail can be calculated exactly.

### 3.1 Random nested dyadic grid

Fix an orientation and a dyadic phase `c in [1,2)`. Put

\[
 s_n=c2^n,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\]

Use a standard stationary random nested dyadic grid in `R^2`: at each scale `s_n`, cells are axis-parallel squares of side `s_n`; the offset in each coordinate is uniform modulo `s_n`; the offsets are coupled across scales so the partitions are nested. The usual random-bit construction gives this. A final uniform rotation and log-uniform phase `dc/(c log 2)` would restore rotation and full scale invariance; neither changes the calculation below.

Connect the center of every dyadic square to the center of its parent. For an arbitrary point `x`, the centers of its shrinking cells converge to `x`, so the center tree has a natural metric-completion ray from `x` to infinity. For two points `x,y`, let `S(x,y)` be the side of their smallest common dyadic square and let `R_T(x,y)` be the tree path through that common-square center.

Let the coordinate difference in the fixed grid orientation be

\[
(a,b)=(|x_1-y_1|,|x_2-y_2|),\qquad
D=a+b,\quad P=ab,\quad r_\infty=\max(a,b).
\]

Let `s_0` be the smallest scale in the phase sequence with `s_0>=r_infinity`, and `s_n=2^n s_0` for `n>=0`.

### Theorem R12-S5A — exact common-ancestor tail and one-tree divergence

For every `n>=0`,

\[
\boxed{
\Pr\{S>s_n\}
= q_n
:=1-\left(1-\frac{a}{s_n}\right)
       \left(1-\frac{b}{s_n}\right)
=\frac{D}{s_n}-\frac{P}{s_n^2}.
}
\tag{3.1}
\]

Moreover the Euclidean length of the center-tree route is exactly

\[
\boxed{L_T(x,y)=\sqrt2\,S(x,y),}
\tag{3.2}
\]

and consequently

\[
\boxed{\mathbb E L_T(x,y)=\infty\qquad(x\ne y).}
\tag{3.3}
\]

#### Proof

At a fixed scale `s>=r_infinity`, a uniform one-dimensional offset puts two coordinates distance `a` apart in the same interval with probability `1-a/s`; similarly the second coordinate gives `1-b/s`, independently. Hence the two points are in the same square with probability `(1-a/s)(1-b/s)`. By nesting, the event `{S<=s}` is exactly the event that they are in the same square at scale `s`, which proves (3.1).

If a child square has side `u`, its center differs from its parent's center by `(+-u/2,+-u/2)`, hence by Euclidean distance `u/sqrt(2)`. On one arm from an endpoint to the common-square center, the child side lengths are

\[
S/2,S/4,S/8,\ldots,
\]

so the arm length is

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(\frac S2+\frac S4+\cdots\right)=\frac S{\sqrt2}.
\]

There are two arms, giving (3.2).

For a nonnegative variable supported on `s_0,2s_0,4s_0,...`, the dyadic tail-sum identity gives

\[
\mathbb E S
=s_0+\sum_{n\ge0}(s_{n+1}-s_n)\Pr(S>s_n)
=s_0+\sum_{n\ge0}s_nq_n.
\]

But

\[
s_nq_n=D-\frac{P}{s_n}\longrightarrow D>0,
\]

so the series diverges. Equation (3.2) gives (3.3). QED.

### Interpretation

This is the dyadic counterpart of the preceding Poisson-line `E[1/T_1]=infinity` obstruction. A **single** stationary nested hierarchy has a critical padding tail of order `distance/scale`; forcing a route to climb all the way to the first common ancestor converts that tail into a logarithmically divergent expected route length.

## 4. Method #13 — FIND WHAT ONLY INCREASES: two independent hierarchies kill the critical tail exactly

The common-ancestor scale is a clean monovariant: once two points are in the same cell, every coarser scale keeps them together. Rather than change the within-tree path, I added independent copies of the entire hierarchy and asked how the minimum common-ancestor scale changes.

Let `S_1,...,S_m` be the common-square sides for `m` independent stationary dyadic grids with the same phase/orientation, and put

\[
S_{(m)}=\min_{1\le j\le m}S_j.
\]

### Theorem R12-S5B — exact multi-tree cure; threshold `m=2`

For every `n>=0`,

\[
\boxed{\Pr\{S_{(m)}>s_n\}=q_n^m.}
\tag{4.1}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E S_{(m)}
=s_0+\sum_{n\ge0}s_nq_n^m.
}
\tag{4.2}
\]

The expectation is infinite for `m=1` and finite for every `m>=2`. In fact

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E S_{(m)}
\le s_0+
\frac{D^m s_0^{1-m}}{1-2^{1-m}},\qquad m\ge2.
}
\tag{4.3}
\]

For two trees the series sums in closed form:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E S_{(2)}
=s_0+rac{2D^2}{s_0}
-\frac{8DP}{3s_0^2}
+\frac{8P^2}{7s_0^3}.
}
\tag{4.4}
\]

If one simply chooses, for each pair, the shorter of the `m` center-tree routes, then

\[
L_{(m)}=\sqrt2\,S_{(m)}.
\]

For `m=2`, using `s_0<2r_infinity`, `r_infinity>=D/2`, gives the uniform bound

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E L_{(2)}<6\sqrt2\,D\le12|x-y|_2.
}
\tag{4.5}
\]

Thus **two independent dyadic hierarchies are the exact threshold at which this center-tree mechanism acquires finite expected pair stretch**.

#### Proof

Conditional on phase and orientation, the grids are independent, so (3.1) immediately gives (4.1). The same dyadic tail-sum identity gives (4.2). Since `q_n<=D/s_n`,

\[
\sum_{n\ge0}s_nq_n^m
\le D^m\sum_{n\ge0}s_n^{1-m}
=\frac{D^m s_0^{1-m}}{1-2^{1-m}},
\]

which proves (4.3). For `m=2`, expand

\[
s_nq_n^2
=\frac{D^2}{s_n}-\frac{2DP}{s_n^2}+\frac{P^2}{s_n^3}
\]

and use

\[
\sum_{n\ge0}s_n^{-1}=\frac2{s_0},\quad
\sum_{n\ge0}s_n^{-2}=\frac4{3s_0^2},\quad
\sum_{n\ge0}s_n^{-3}=\frac8{7s_0^3},
\]

giving (4.4). Finally `D<=2r_infinity<=2s_0` implies `D^2/s_0<=2D`, while `s_0<2r_infinity<=2D`; dropping the negative term and the positive `P^2` term in (4.4) is not by itself an upper bound, so instead use (4.3) at `m=2`:

\[
\mathbb E S_{(2)}\le s_0+2D^2/s_0<2D+4D=6D.
\]

Multiplying by `sqrt(2)` and using `D<=sqrt(2)|x-y|_2` proves (4.5). QED.

### What this accomplishes

This is a real improvement in the **candidate geometry**, but not in the frozen source metric. A common log-uniform phase and uniform global rotation make the distribution similarity-covariant; independent stationary shifts give translation invariance; (4.5) solves the finite-mean route-length axiom at the pair level. What remains is the genuinely SIRSN-specific gluing: pairwise route compatibility and the main-road intensity condition.

## 5. Method #21 — ASK WHO ELSE HAS THIS SHAPE: tree covers point to the right cure, but SIRSN compatibility breaks it

## 6. What survived the three attacks

The run changes the design picture substantially even though `K_explicit` does not move.

1. **Single tree is provably the wrong scale object.** The exact `distance/scale` padding tail forces infinite mean route length for the center-tree embedding.
2. **Two independent hierarchies cure the length obstruction exactly.** The tail squares, and the best-of-two pair route has a completely explicit finite expected stretch, with exact formula (4.4).
3. **Per-pair handshaking is not enough for SIRSNs.** The exact witness shows that low-stretch tree-cover logic by itself violates the route-coalescence axiom.

This points to a much narrower surviving construction: take the **overlay of two hierarchical trees and define routes as unique geodesics in one globally weighted overlay**, rather than choosing a tree independently for each pair. Unique geodesics would automatically enforce the two-meeting-points compatibility property, and their length can potentially be compared with the best single-tree candidate. The nontrivial remaining issue is the SIRSN main-road condition: allowing switching at crossings may let arbitrarily fine-scale edges appear in the interior of long routes and could make `p(1)` infinite. That is now the exact gluing obstruction worth understanding.

I am not promoting this overlay idea to a theorem. It is the concrete next object exposed by this run.
