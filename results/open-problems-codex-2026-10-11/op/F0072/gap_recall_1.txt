For statistical mechanics models on a torus whose Hamiltonian and lattice satisfy reflection positivity across midplanes between sites, the Gaussian domination (infrared) bound gives an upper bound on the Fourier transform of the two-point function: hat{G}(k) <= C / (beta E(k)), where E(k) is the quadratic energy form of the interaction (e.g. E(k)=sum_i (1-cos k_i) for nearest-neighbor coupling) and C is an explicit constant (often 1/2; exact value depends on normalization and is not always recalled precisely across variants). Combined with a spin-wave/Gaussian domination inequality this yields, for models such as the classical Heisenberg or XY model in dimension d>=3, or the Ising model in sufficiently high dimension, existence of long-range order at low temperature via a second-moment argument applied to the infrared-bounded Fourier integral. It does not give critical exponent values, does not apply in dimension d<=2 where the infrared integral diverges (consistent with the Mermin-Wagner theorem ruling out continuous-symmetry breaking there), and requires the specific reflection-positive algebraic structure; it gives no control for models lacking this symmetry, such as many disordered, frustrated, or non-bipartite systems. Typical failure: Assuming reflection positivity survives a seemingly minor modification of the Hamiltonian, such as adding next-nearest-neighbor terms, a random field, or working on a non-bipartite lattice, without re-checking the checkerboard decomposition; reflection positivity is a delicate algebraic property that most natural perturbations destroy, so the infrared bound and everything built on it, including the Frohlich-Simon-Spencer long-range order conclusion, silently stops applying.
