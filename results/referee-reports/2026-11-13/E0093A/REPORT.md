# E0093: random generating sets and mixing on finite groups

Research date: 13 November 2026. The broad question, with no restriction on the number of generators, is not resolved here. Its large-generator cutoff version has a very recent literature answer: **Haoran Zhu, arXiv:2610.41347v2, Theorem 1.2, PDF p. 4 (2 November 2026)** proves cutoff for every finite group for $k\ge 2^{14}\log |G|$, $\log k=o(\log|G|)$, in the signed-tuple model specified below. This is an arXiv preprint; no journal publication was established. The label PARTIAL distinguishes that precise result from a resolution at every $k$, or of Diaconis's separate conjecture about every generating pair of $A_n$. We do not claim a new general solution or that the explicit constants below are optimal.

The deliverables are plain-text source downloads, an explicit-constant proof of a logarithmic-degree mixing bound, deterministic obstructions from abelian quotients, a complete analysis of generating pairs of prime dihedral groups, and exact TV threshold computations on small groups. The logarithmic-degree theorem and abelian obstruction are known in stronger/general forms in print; their proofs here make the walk conventions and constants explicit.

## What Diaconis actually asked

The source is P. Diaconis, *Some things we've learned (about Markov chain Monte Carlo)*, Bernoulli **19** (2013), 1294–1305, [arXiv:1309.7754](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.7754). Question 2 is on **PDF p. 9**, in Section 5, not an independently quantified conjecture about a specified $k$. Its central sentence is:

> If a generating set S is chosen at random, what is the typical rate of convergence?

Question 2 gives **no** function $k(|G|)$, no probability law on sets or tuples, no laziness or inverse convention, and no choice between a gap, TV mixing time, and cutoff. Those details cannot be attributed to the source. It follows the discussion of Wilson's random walks on the binary cube, whose distance there is TV. It points to Hildebrand [37] and to diameter estimates converted to convergence bounds using Diaconis–Saloff-Coste [26]. Its last sentences add a different, worst-case conjecture: every generating pair of $A_n$ should mix within order $n^3\log n$ steps. The quantifier there is **every pair**, not a typical pair.

Diaconis's reference [4], Babai–Beals–Seress (SODA 2004, 1108–1112), concerns **symmetric/alternating groups**. It is not a diameter theorem for every finite group. Its small-support condition and random-permutation consequences must not be generalized to arbitrary groups. The original paper could not be retrieved here; the precise small-support theorem is also reproduced as Theorem 1.4 in Helfgott–Seress, downloaded author PDF p. 4. The bibliographic reference [26] is *Comparison techniques for random walk on finite groups*, Ann. Probab. **21** (1993), 2131–2156.

The closest interpretation of the main question is a **programme of classifying typical mixing as a function of both $G$ and $k$**. It is broader than Alon–Roichman, which was already known when this question was written. The growing-degree cutoff version follows the preceding Wilson discussion; fixed-degree nonabelian cases connect to the closing alternating-group conjecture. The following three versions isolate useful parts of that programme.

## Models and precise versions

All logarithms below are natural unless a base is shown. Put $N=|G|$, let $R_s f(x)=f(xs)$, and start at the identity. Write $\pi(x)=1/N$, $d_P(t)=\frac12\sum_x|P^t(e,x)-1/N|$, $t_P(\epsilon)=\min\{t:d_P(t)\le\epsilon\}$, and $\gamma_P=1-\lambda_2(P)$. For lazy symmetric walks all eigenvalues lie in $[0,1]$. Translation invariance makes the starting vertex irrelevant.

There are two different symmetric measures:

* **Tuple measure:** sample $Z_1,\ldots,Z_k$ independently uniformly, with replacement, and set $Q_Z=(2k)^{-1}\sum_i(R_{Z_i}+R_{Z_i^{-1}})$, $P_Z=(I+Q_Z)/2$.
* **Set measure:** sample a uniform $k$-subset $S\subseteq G$, put $T=S\cup S^{-1}$, and set $Q_T=|T|^{-1}\sum_{s\in T}R_s$, $P_T=(I+Q_T)/2$. This is the uniform-on-the-union convention in the task.

Both allow the identity among the sampled elements. Thus an additional holding probability can arise. Generation is conditioned on explicitly for fixed $k$; otherwise, many groups cannot be generated at all. For example, $C_2^d$ cannot be generated by fewer than $d$ elements. We do not replace a failed generating set by a walk on its subgroup and call it a walk mixing on $G$.

The two measures can differ even when the $Z_i$ are distinct: involutions receive twice the weight of an unpaired non-involution in $Q_Z$. For a mixed rotation/reflection pair in a dihedral group the difference survives as the group grows. Conditioning independent tuples to be distinct costs $o(1)$ for $k=N^{o(1)}$, since the collision probability is at most $k(k-1)/(2N)$; this does **not** remove the involution weighting difference. All computations below include both measures.

**A. Uniform logarithmic-degree mixing.** There is an absolute $C$ such that, for every sequence of finite groups, $k=\lceil C\log N\rceil$ random generators give $t(1/4)=O(\log N)$ with probability tending to one. **Settled in print:** Alon–Roichman and subsequent refinements; see also Pak and Hildebrand. Theorem A1 below supplies the sufficient choice $k\ge16\log(2N)$ for the tuple measure, failure probability at most $N^{-1}$, and an explicit time. For the uniform set measure the same degree gives an asymptotic statement via conditioning and comparison, with a factor two in the gap bound. These are sufficient constants, not exact optimal thresholds. For $k=(\log N)^c$, $c>1$, these bounds apply eventually; for $c<1$ a uniform polylogarithmic mixing bound is false.

**B. Fixed-degree polylogarithmic mixing.** For fixed $k=2$ or $3$, condition on generation and ask whether $t(1/4)\le(\log N)^{C_k}$ with high probability for every $G$. **False**, already for cyclic groups, deterministically for every choice of the generators. Theorem B1 proves $t(1/4)\ge\frac18((N/2)^{1/k}-1)^2$. It also applies with $N$ replaced by the size of any abelian quotient. Theorem B2 gives a nonabelian counterexample: every generating pair of $D_{2p}$, $p$ odd prime, has mixing time $\Theta(p^2)$, with explicit bounds and spectra. Neither counterexample is claimed as a new phenomenon.

**C. Cutoff with logarithmic or larger degree.** In the tuple model ask whether there is a deterministic $t_*(G,k)$ such that $t(\epsilon)/t_*\to1$ in probability for each fixed $0<\epsilon<1$. **Answered by Zhu's Theorem 1.2** whenever $k\ge2^{14}\log N$ and $\log k=o(\log N)$. It covers nonlazy discrete time and rate-one continuous time, directed and undirected walks. Its entropic time is defined separately for the directed and undirected kernels:

$$
 h_m=\mathbb E_Z H(Q_Z^m(e,\cdot)),\qquad
 t_* =\min\{m\ge0:h_m\ge\log N-2(\log N)^{2/3}\}.
$$

Here $Q_Z$ is the appropriate **nonlazy** kernel, and the expectation averages over the sampled generators. The time is deterministic but depends on the group structure. The theorem is not a closed formula in $N,k$. Theorem 1.4 in that preprint gives window information. A rate-one continuous-time walk using the half-lazy jump kernel $P_Z$ has generator $P_Z-I=(Q_Z-I)/2$; its distribution at time $t$ is exactly the nonlazy continuous-time distribution at $t/2$. Thus its leading cutoff time is $2t_*$. This exact clock identity is distinct from the discrete-time convention used for the finite computations.

The theorem does not cover every fixed proportionality constant in $k\asymp\log N$, or $k\ll\log N$. At fixed $k$, even cutoff itself fails on many abelian and nilpotent families. For the literal uniform-union measure in groups having a substantial mixture of involutions and other elements, Zhu's tuple theorem alone is insufficient to assert cutoff or transfer its exact time. It applies directly when the two measures agree (for example all elements are involutions, or there are no sampled involutions/inverse collisions). Our comparison proves A for the set measure; we do not infer C from a constant-factor comparison.

For $k/\log N\to\infty$, Zhu's Lemma 5.6 gives the useful range
$$
 (1-o(1))\frac{\log N}{\log k}\le t_*(G,k)
 \le(1+o(1))\frac{\log N}{\log(k/\log N)}.
$$
For $k=(\log N)^c$, $c>1$, these are constants $1/c$ and $1/(c-1)$ times $\log N/\log\log N$. Their difference can be real. For example, compare $U_{p,3}$ with $C_{p^3}$, both of order $N=p^3$, using $k=\lceil(\log N)^4\rceil$. The upper-triangular theorem gives the lower endpoint for $U_{p,3}$, whereas abelian cutoff gives the upper endpoint for $C_{p^3}$. The leading times differ by a factor $4/3$. Both families have negligible sampled involutions and inverse collisions, so this comparison also applies to uniform-union sampling. These are consequences of the cited theorems, not a new universality claim.

## Proof A1: an explicit logarithmic-degree bound

For $0<\eta<1$ define
$$
 J(\eta)=\tfrac12[(1+\eta)\log(1+\eta)+(1-\eta)\log(1-\eta)].
$$
For independent uniform generators, let $r=\|Q_Z\|_{\mathbf1^\perp}$. Then
$$
 \Pr(r\ge\eta)\le 2(N-1)e^{-kJ(\eta)}. \tag{A1}
$$
In particular $J(1/2)=0.130812035941137\ldots$. If
$$
 k\ge \frac{2\log N+\log2}{J(1/2)},
$$
the exceptional probability is at most (1/N). The simpler $k\ge16\log(2N)$ suffices. On the complementary event the generators generate $G$, $\gamma_{P_Z}\ge1/4$, and
$$
 t_{P_Z}(1/4)\le
 \left\lceil\frac{\log(2\sqrt{N-1})}{\log(4/3)}\right\rceil. \tag{A2}
$$

Proof. On the ((N-1))-dimensional space $\mathbf1^\perp$, put $X_i=(R_{Z_i}+R_{Z_i}^{*})/2$. Each $X_i$ is self-adjoint, has norm at most one, and has expectation zero, since averaging all right translations projects onto the constants. Convexity of the scalar exponential on ([-1,1]), followed by functional calculus, gives
$$
 e^{\theta X_i}\preceq \cosh\theta\,I+\sinh\theta\,X_i,
 \qquad \mathbb E e^{\theta X_i}\preceq\cosh\theta\,I.
$$
The standard finite-dimensional Golden–Thompson trace inequality gives, conditioning on $X_1,\ldots,X_{j-1}$,
$$
 \mathbb E\operatorname{tr}e^{\theta\sum_{i\le j}X_i}
 \le\mathbb E\operatorname{tr}\left(e^{\theta\sum_{i<j}X_i}\mathbb Ee^{\theta X_j}\right)
 \le (\cosh\theta)\mathbb E\operatorname{tr}e^{\theta\sum_{i<j}X_i}.
$$
Induction bounds this by $(N-1)(\cosh\theta)^k$. Markov's inequality bounds the probability of a largest eigenvalue at least $k\eta$ by $(N-1)\exp[-k(\theta\eta-\log\cosh\theta)]$. Choose $\theta=\operatorname{arctanh}\eta$, and apply the same argument to $-X_i$; this proves (A1). This argument uses a classical trace inequality, not an assumption that the matrices commute.

If generation fails there is a nonconstant function constant on the cosets $xH$ of the generated subgroup $H$, giving an eigenvalue one; hence $r<1$ ensures generation. The nonconstant eigenvalues of $P_Z$ are at most $(1+\eta)/2$ in absolute value. Symmetry and translation invariance give
$$
 N\sum_x(P_Z^t(e,x)-1/N)^2=\sum_{j=2}^N\lambda_j^{2t}.
$$
Cauchy–Schwarz therefore yields $d_{P_Z}(t)\le\frac12\sqrt{N-1}((1+\eta)/2)^t$, proving (A2). Without laziness the corresponding bound uses $\eta^t$. This completes the proof.

For a **distinct** tuple underlying $S$, write $m=|S\cup S^{-1}|$ and let $c_s\in\{1,2\}$ be each support element's multiplicity among the (2k) signed entries. Then
$$
 Q_T(s)=1/m\ge (k/m)\,c_s/(2k)=(k/m)Q_Z(s).
$$
The Dirichlet-form variational principle gives $\gamma_{P_T}\ge(k/m)\gamma_{P_Z}\ge\gamma_{P_Z}/2$. Conditional on distinctness, failure in (A1) is at most its right-hand side divided by the probability of distinctness. For $k=\lceil16\log(2N)\rceil$, that denominator tends to one. Thus the genuine uniform set walk has, with probability tending to one,
$$
 \gamma_{P_T}\ge1/8,\qquad
 t_{P_T}(1/4)\le
 \left\lceil\frac{\log(2\sqrt{N-1})}{\log(8/7)}\right\rceil. \tag{A3}
$$
This comparison handles involutions; omitting it would identify two different walks.

The order $\log N$ in this uniform worst-case upper bound is necessary when $k\asymp\log N$. To see it without using Wilson's sharp theorem, take $G=C_2^d$, $d\le k\le Ad$, for fixed $A\ge1$. In $t$ steps the position can be the sum of at most $t$ of the $k$ generators, since repeated uses cancel modulo two. Consequently
$$
 d(t)\ge1-2^{-d}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{k}{j}.
$$
For $0\le u\le1/2$, the binomial sum is at most $e^{k h(u)}$ when $t\le ku$, where $h(u)=-u\log u-(1-u)\log(1-u)$. Indeed, set $x=u/(1-u)\le1$ and compare $x^t\sum_{j\le t}\binom{k}{j}$ with $(1+x)^k$. Choose $u\in(0,1/2)$ with $Ah(u)=\log2/2$. At $t=\lfloor ku\rfloor$, $d(t)\ge1-2^{-d/2}$. Thus the worst-case time is $\Omega_A(d)=\Omega_A(\log N)$, matching A2–A3 in order.

For completeness, the diameter-to-mixing conversion suggested in Question 2 can also be made explicit. If the symmetric support has size $m$ and Cayley diameter $D$, its half-lazy uniform walk satisfies
$$
 \gamma_P\ge\frac1{2mD^2},\qquad
 t_P(1/4)\le\left\lceil2mD^2\log(2\sqrt{N-1})\right\rceil. \tag{A4}
$$
To prove it, choose for each $g\in G$ a word of length at most $D$. Write
$$
 \operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f=\frac1{2N^2}
 \sum_{g,x}|f(x)-f(xg)|^2.
$$
Telescope along the chosen word and apply Cauchy–Schwarz. After summing over $x$, translating the intermediate vertex leaves each edge sum $\sum_x|f(x)-f(xs)|^2$ unchanged. For each support element $s$, its total coefficient is at most $ND^2$. It follows that
$$
 \operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f
 \le \frac{D^2}{2N}\sum_{s,x}|f(x)-f(xs)|^2
 =mD^2\mathcal E_Q(f)=2mD^2\mathcal E_P(f).
$$
The variational principle gives the gap bound; the TV estimate from A1 and $(1-\gamma)^t\le e^{-\gamma t}$ give the time bound. Thus a diameter theorem really does imply a mixing bound, with a potentially substantial loss. On the cycle examples below A4 has an unnecessary logarithmic factor.

## Proof B1: obstruction from any abelian quotient

Let $G\twoheadrightarrow A$ be an abelian quotient of order $M\ge2$. Consider any half-lazy symmetric walk supported on $k$ inverse pairs, with arbitrary nonnegative weights summing to one across those pairs. The tuple measure and uniform-union measure are both included, using at most $k$ representatives of inverse orbits. Then
$$
 t(1/4)\ge \frac18\left((M/2)^{1/k}-1\right)^2. \tag{B1}
$$
If $q=\lceil M^{1/k}\rceil-1\ge1$, also
$$
 \gamma_P\le \frac{\pi^2}{q^2}. \tag{B2}
$$

Proof of (B1). Lift the projected walk to $W_t\in\mathbb Z^k$: independently at each step hold with probability (1/2), or add $\pm e_i$ with half the weight assigned to pair (i). Involutions cause no problem because their two signed lifts have the same image. Then $\mathbb E\|W_t\|_2^2=t/2$, since the increments have mean zero. For $t>0$, Markov's inequality gives $\Pr(\|W_t\|_2\le\sqrt{2t})\ge3/4$. This ball has at most $(2\lfloor\sqrt{2t}\rfloor+1)^k$ integer points. Its image $B_t\subseteq A$ therefore satisfies
$$
 d_{P_A}(t)\ge P_A^t(e,B_t)-\pi_A(B_t)
 \ge3/4-\frac{(2\sqrt{2t}+1)^k}{M}.
$$
If $d_P(t)\le1/4$, then $d_{P_A}(t)\le1/4$ by data processing, so $M/2\le(2\sqrt{2t}+1)^k$. Rearranging proves (B1). Mixing cannot already occur at time zero for a nontrivial quotient, except that the displayed lower bound is then zero when $M=2$.

Proof of (B2). For each of the $M$ characters of $A$, record the arguments of its values on the $k$ generators in ([0,1)^k). Partition that cube into $q^k<M$ boxes of side (1/q). Two distinct characters lie in one box; their quotient is a nontrivial character $\chi$ whose generator arguments admit representatives $\theta_i$ with $|\theta_i|<1/q$. If the pair weights are $w_i$, the associated eigenvalue is
$$
 \lambda_\chi=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sum_iw_i\cos(2\pi\theta_i),\qquad
 1-\lambda_\chi\le\pi^2\sum_iw_i\theta_i^2\le\pi^2/q^2.
$$
The character lifts to an eigenfunction on $G$, proving (B2).

Taking $A=C_p$ and $p\to\infty$, for each fixed $k=2,3$, every generating set has $t(1/4)\ge c_kp^{2/k}$ eventually. The conditional probability of a polylogarithmic upper bound is therefore zero eventually, not merely bounded away from one. For $k=(\log p)^c,0<c<1$, the same bound grows like $\exp(2(\log p)^{1-c})/8$ up to subleading factors, faster than every power of $\log p$.

Examples beyond abelian groups: the Heisenberg group $H_3(\mathbb F_p)$ has abelianization $C_p^2$, so for $k=2$ the bound is $\frac18(p/\sqrt2-1)^2$. The wreath product $C_p\wr C_m$ has abelianization $C_p\times C_m$; for fixed $m\ge2$, the same argument gives order $p^{2/k}\$, a power of $|C_p\wr C_m|=mp^m$. A large abelian **normal subgroup** alone does not imply the required large quotient; the proof uses an actual homomorphism onto an abelian group.

## Proof B2: all generating pairs of prime dihedral groups

Let $G=D_{2p}=\langle r,s:r^p=s^2=e,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle$, $p\ge3$ prime. Choose any two-element generating subset $S$, and use the uniform-union half-lazy walk $P_T$. Then
$$
 (\log2)\left(\frac{3p^2}{2\pi^2}-1\right)
 \le t_{P_T}(1/4)\le 2p^2. \tag{D1}
$$
Moreover no sequence of these generating-pair walks exhibits TV cutoff. Conditional on generation, the scaled gap has a nondegenerate two-point limiting law:
$$
 p^2\gamma_{P_T}\ \Longrightarrow
 \begin{cases}\pi^2/4&\text{with probability }1/3,\\2\pi^2/3&\text{with probability }2/3.\end{cases} \tag{D2}
$$
This illustrates a further difficulty with a single concentrated answer at fixed degree.

Proof. A generating pair is either two distinct reflections or one nonidentity rotation and one reflection. Two rotations cannot generate. Distinct reflections multiply to a nonidentity rotation, which has order $p$. In the first case the Cayley graph is a connected graph of degree two on (2p) vertices, hence a (2p)-cycle. Its lazy eigenvalues are
$$
 \lambda_j=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos(\pi j/p),\quad0\le j<2p,
 \qquad\gamma=\sin^2(\pi/(2p)). \tag{D3}
$$

In the second case write the pair as $r^a,r^b s$, $a\ne0$. The two rotation cosets each have the two cycle edges $r^{\pm a}$; after relabeling one coset, the reflection edges form the matching between them. Thus the graph is the prism $C_p\square K_2$. Fourier transform in the cyclic coordinate and diagonalization in the two layers give
$$
 \lambda_{j,\sigma}=\tfrac12+\frac{2\cos(2\pi j/p)+\sigma}{6},
 \quad0\le j<p,\ \sigma=\pm1,
$$
$$
 \gamma=\min\{(1-\cos(2\pi/p))/3,\ 1/3\}. \tag{D4}
$$
For the **tuple** lazy measure instead, the two-reflection case is unchanged, while a mixed pair has eigenvalues $\tfrac12+[\cos(2\pi j/p)+\sigma]/4$ and gap $\min\{(1-\cos(2\pi/p))/4,1/2\}$. This also mixes on order $p^2$.

For the lower bound, every nontrivial real eigenfunction normalized to sup norm one, started where its absolute value is maximal, gives $d(t)\ge\lambda^t/2$. Transitivity removes the restriction on the start. Thus $t(1/4)\ge\log2/[-\log(1-\gamma)]\ge(\log2)(\gamma^{-1}-1)$; the latter inequality follows from $-\log(1-x)\le x/(1-x)$. Equations D3–D4 imply $\gamma\le2\pi^2/(3p^2)$, giving D1's lower bound.

For the upper bound use $4d(t)^2\le\sum_{\lambda\ne1}\lambda^{2t}$, established in A1. In the cycle case, for $j\le p$, $1-\lambda_j=\sin^2(\pi j/(2p))\ge j^2/p^2$, so the sum is at most $2\sum_{j\ge1}e^{-2tj^2/p^2}$. At $t=2p^2$ this is at most $2e^{-4}/(1-e^{-12})<1/4$. In the prism case the $\sigma=1$ modes satisfy $1-\lambda_{j,+}\ge8j^2/(3p^2)$ for $j\le p/2$, and all $\sigma=-1$ modes are at most $2/3$. The sum is at most
$$
 2\sum_{j\ge1}e^{-16tj^2/(3p^2)}+p(2/3)^{2t}.
$$
At $t=2p^2$ this is less than $1/4$ for every $p\ge3$: bound the series by $2e^{-32/3}/(1-e^{-32})$ and the other term by $3(2/3)^{36}$. This proves the upper bound, with room to spare.

There are $p(p-1)/2$ reflection–reflection subsets and $p(p-1)$ generating mixed subsets. Their conditional probabilities are $1/3,2/3$. Taking the limits in D3–D4 proves D2.

Finally $t(1/4)\gamma\le4\pi^2/3$ by D1 and the gap upper bound, while $\gamma\to0$. The same eigenfunction bound gives, for every fixed $\epsilon<1/2$, $t(\epsilon)\ge\log(1/(2\epsilon))/[-\log(1-\gamma)]$. Choose a fixed sufficiently small $\epsilon$, so that this lower bound exceeds (2t$1/4$) for all sufficiently large $p$, uniformly over both pair types. Cutoff would force that ratio to tend to one. This contradiction proves the assertion.

## Computation: evidence, with exact TV crossings

The experiments used seed **930093**, uniform $k$-subsets sampled without replacement, and rejection until they generated the **whole specified group**. There are **144 accepted sets**, two per degree per group, and **288 walk records** because both measures were evaluated. This sample size is deliberately small: it checks formulas and exposes model differences, not an asymptotic probability claim.

The 24 groups were $C_{31},C_{101},C_{251}$; $C_p^2,p=5,7,11$; $C_2^d,d=4,6,8$; $D_{14},D_{34},D_{62}$; $S_n,3\le n\le8$; $\mathrm{PSL}(2,p),p=5,7,11$; and $H_3(\mathbb F_p),p=3,5,7$. The degrees were $2,3,\lceil2\log N\rceil$, except $C_2^d$, which used $d,d+2,2d$. All group elements were enumerated; no walk trajectories were sampled.

For $m=|T|$, the set walk uses integer transition numerator $mI+\sum_{s\in T}R_s$ with denominator $2m$. The tuple walk uses $2kI+\sum_i(R_{Z_i}+R_{Z_i^{-1}})$ with denominator $4k$. If $c_t(x)$ is the integer vector after $t$ such numerator multiplications and $D$ is the denominator, then
$$
 d(t)=\frac{\sum_x|N c_t(x)-D^t|}{2ND^t}.
$$
The program first locates the crossings numerically, then recomputes the whole evolution using Python arbitrary-precision integers, checking the inequality at the crossing and the strict reverse inequality at its predecessor. TV contracts under a Markov kernel, so these are the **exact integer first mixing times** for thresholds $9/10,1/2,1/4,1/10$. Integer arithmetic, not a floating-point tolerance, decides the reported times. Spectral gaps are numerical eigenvalue computations, not algebraically exact numbers.

`data/samples.jsonl` stores every generating set, rejection count, both kernels, four certified crossing times, threshold margins, gap, eigenvector residual, and comparison bound. `data/summary.csv` groups the results. `code/audit.py` independently checks representative group laws, recomputes gaps with a different eigenvector start, repeats exact TV crossings by a forward-scatter recurrence, and compares full dihedral spectra with dense diagonalization.
