## 1. Lemma 2.1 — SOUND

**Statement, lines 76–80.** The hypotheses are sufficient: the feasible set is a nonempty closed polyhedron, the objective is a quadratic polynomial, and boundedness below means a finite infimum, not boundedness of the feasible set. The assertion includes existence of a minimizer in K, not just membership of the value in K. Both are correct. Adding a constant term in K changes nothing.

**Attainment, line 83.** The Frank–Wolfe attainment theorem is the appropriate result: a quadratic polynomial bounded below on a nonempty polyhedron attains its minimum. Compactness of F and positive definiteness of Q are not needed. Continuity alone would not justify this step on an unbounded set, but the cited quadratic-programming result does. The original reference was not supplied, so this is an assessment of the standard theorem invoked, not a check of a page in the original article.

**KKT necessity, line 83.** Correct without a Slater assumption. The normal cone of a polyhedron at x* is generated by the active constraint normals, including when F has empty interior. Consequently there are nonnegative multipliers supported on I with Qx*+q+A_I^T y*=0. Here y* should be understood as restricted to I in display (2.1).

**The system T, lines 85–86.** This is a linear system of equalities and weak inequalities over K. Fixing the active set of one minimizer removes complementarity: A_I x=b_I and y>=0, with all other multipliers zero, enforce it. Q being singular causes no problem; the proof never inverts Q or the KKT matrix.

**The minimal-face argument, line 87.** Correct, although one explanatory sentence would help. A minimal nonempty face of a polyhedron is an affine subspace, possibly a point and possibly containing lines. Otherwise a nonconstant defining inequality would produce a smaller nonempty face. Its affine hull is therefore the face itself and is specified by the original equations together with its tight inequalities. Gaussian elimination gives a K-point of that affine subspace, and every point of that subspace belongs to T. One must use this affine-subspace fact; Gaussian elimination on an arbitrary face's tight equations alone would not guarantee feasibility. The use of a *minimal* face makes the authors' argument valid.

**Optimality and field membership, line 87.** For any feasible z and any (x,y) in T,

\[
g(z)-g(x)=\tfrac12(z-x)^TQ(z-x)-y^TA_I(z-x)\ge0.
\]

Thus every such x is optimal. Evaluating the polynomial at a K-point gives a value in K. No vertex of F or nonsingular optimal basis is required.

**Attempts to break the lemma.** My program independently checks four examples: a singular quadratic with an affine line of minimizers; a strip whose optimal points include no vertex of the feasible set; an optimal affine face in a polyhedron having no vertices; and a zero objective with lineality. None breaks the lemma. Irrational minimizers can coexist with rational ones, but the lemma asserts existence of a rational minimizer, not rationality of every minimizer.

The affine-constraint restriction matters. The rational-data convex *quadratically constrained* problem min x subject to x²<=2 has value -sqrt(2). It is not a counterexample to this lemma, whose feasible set must be polyhedral.

## 2. Theorem 1.1 — SOUND for the ordinary LP/QP class; narrower than the open question

Under the standard meaning of convex quadratic program—minimize a convex quadratic polynomial subject to finitely many affine equalities and weak inequalities—the theorem follows from Lemma 2.1. Rational functions must be defined at the chain to which the program is assigned. A finite real optimum excludes infeasibility and unbounded objective value. Linear maximization is covered by changing the objective's sign.

The program's dimension, graph-specific structure, and active set may depend on P. None of these freedoms defeats the argument: at a fixed rational P every allowed coefficient is rational. Explicitly stating the ordinary QP definition would remove the possible confusion with the quadratically constrained counterexample above.

The stationary-law observation at line 55 is also correct. For a finite irreducible chain, replacing one dependent stationary equation by sum pi_i=1 gives a nonsingular system over Q(P). Thus pi and all conductances belong to Q(P).

My exact characteristic-polynomial calculation for P6 gives

\[
\det(zI-P)=\frac{(z-1)(z+1)(4z^2-2z-1)(4z^2+2z-1)}{16}.
\]

Its second largest eigenvalue is (1+sqrt(5))/4, hence tau_2=3+sqrt(5). A second exact check verifies the proposed cosine eigenvector directly. The minimal polynomial of tau_2 is z²-6z+4, irreducible over Q because its discriminant is 20. The rational witness is decisive. Its periodicity is irrelevant to the explicitly stated definition using lambda_2 rather than the largest absolute nontrivial eigenvalue.

The theorem therefore excludes the specified LP/QP representations, including finite maxima of rational linear flow loads with polyhedral routing constraints, and quadratic flow energies. It does **not** exclude arbitrary congestion-like nonlinear objectives, optimized auxiliary path weights that leave this class, conic constraints, infinitely many constraints, or limits of programs. An infimum over flows is not, just by its name, a finite LP or convex QP.

The paper acknowledges the nonlinear limitation explicitly at line 72, and the theorem's statement is restricted. Accordingly it does not answer Open Problem 3.44 as literally quoted at line 47. The unqualified title and the abstract's claim about “the kind their flow bounds have” should be narrowed: the proof establishes the stated algebraic class, not an exhaustive classification of the flow formulations in Aldous–Fill. Lines 49 and 97 should specify fixed linear load functionals or fixed quadratic energies rather than infer LP/QP structure merely from the words “congestion” and “energy.”

## 3. Proposition 1.2(i) — GAP, with a complete repair

The proposition itself is correct with nonnegative symmetric distances, zero diagonal, and the triangle inequality; distinct vertices may have zero distance. The directed-flow primal at line 103 correctly counts demands for **ordered** pairs and charges both directions against one undirected edge capacity. Its shortest-path dual has the corresponding normalization. Feasibility and finite optimal values follow from connectedness and positive edge conductances.

The next assertion at line 103 is false: C(delta_l) need not equal 1 when C(l)=1. On the three-cycle take pi_i=1/3, c_e=1/6, and edge lengths (4,1,1). Then C(l)=1, while the shortest-path distances on those edges are (2,1,1), giving C(delta_l)=2/3. This is an exact counterexample to that proof sentence, not to the proposition.

**Repair.** Write M for the normalized semimetric maximum. For every normalized length vector l, delta_l is a semimetric and

\[
C(\delta_l)\le C(l)=1.
\]

If C(delta_l)>0, the semimetric delta_l/C(delta_l) is feasible for M, so N(delta_l)<=M. If C(delta_l)=0, connectedness and nonnegativity force delta_l to vanish everywhere; its dual value is zero and is also <=M. Conversely, for any normalized semimetric d, set l_ij=d(i,j) on graph edges. Every path has length at least d(a,b) by the triangle inequality, so delta_l>=d and the length dual's maximum is at least N(d). The two maxima therefore coincide. Strong LP duality supplies kappa.

Both uses of “maximum” are justified. The length simplex is compact. The normalized semimetric set is closed and bounded: every edge distance is at most 1/c_e, and a fixed graph path bounds every other distance. This also supplies the attainment detail omitted from the printed proof.

## 4. Proposition 1.2(ii) — SOUND

The factor 2 in lines 105–109 is correct. N sums ordered pairs, whereas C sums undirected edges once. For every coordinate f,

\[
\sum_{a,b}\pi_a\pi_b(f_a-f_b)^2=2\operatorname{Var}_\pi(f),
\quad
\sum_Ec_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2=\mathcal E(f).
\]

Summing the scalar Poincare inequality over coordinates proves N(d)<=2 tau_2 C(d). Constant coordinates contribute zero and cause no exception. A nonconstant lambda_2 eigenvector attains equality. Connectedness ensures its energy is positive; multiplication by its energy's inverse square root enforces C(d)=1. The embedding coordinates need not belong to Q(P), which is precisely why this SDP is outside Theorem 1.1.

Allowing arbitrary finite k causes no closure or attainment problem: n points have affine span of dimension at most n-1, and equality already occurs in dimension one. Anchoring one point at zero identifies all squared Euclidean distance arrays with positive semidefinite (n-1)-by-(n-1) Gram matrices. The normalized Gram set is compact because the anchored conductance Laplacian is positive definite. The claimed nonpolyhedrality for n>=3 follows from this identification with a PSD cone of matrix size at least two.

The formal statement describes embeddings directly and is unambiguous. The surrounding use of “semimetric” is not: the triangle inequality is essential in (i), but squared Euclidean distances in (ii) can violate it. See the corrections below.
