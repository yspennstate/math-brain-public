For precision, the requested original statements follow. Mathematical typography and line wrapping are normalized here; the linked text extracts preserve the actual extraction.

- **Problem (i), p. 31:** “(i) Nonuniqueness. Does there exist a discrete set of centers in \(\mathbb R^d\) which admits two stable allocations (differing on a nonnull set)? By Theorems 2 and 3, such a set must have greater than quadratic growth, and may not arise from a translation-invariant point process.”
- **Theorem 1 (Existence), p. 2:** “For any discrete set of centers \(\Xi\subseteq\mathbb R^d\), and any appetite \(\alpha\in[0,\infty]\), there exists a stable allocation.”
- **Theorem 2 (Uniqueness for recurrent configurations), p. 3:** “Given discrete \(\Xi\subseteq\mathbb R^d\), let \(G_b\) be the graph with vertex set \(\Xi\) and with an edge between every pair of centers at distance at most \(b\) from each other. Suppose \(\Xi\) is such that, for all \(b>0\), the graph \(G_b\) is recurrent for simple symmetric random walk. Then for any \(\alpha\in(0,\infty)\), there exists an \(\mathcal L\)-a.e. unique stable allocation.”
- **Theorem 3 (Almost sure uniqueness), p. 5:** “For a translation-invariant point process \(\Pi\) of finite intensity on \(\mathbb R^d\) and for any \(\alpha\in(0,\infty)\), there exists \(\mathbf P\)-a.s. an \(\mathcal L\)-a.e. unique stable allocation \(\Psi_\Pi\) to \(\Xi=[\Pi]\). Furthermore, \(\Psi_\Pi\) can be chosen to be a measurable, isometry-equivariant factor of \(\Pi\).” The surrounding point-process setup is simple and has intensity \(\lambda\in(0,\infty)\). Ergodicity is not a hypothesis of this theorem.
- **Proposition 13 (Transience from capacities), pp. 13–14:** “Let \(H=(V,E)\) be a locally finite, directed graph with no directed cycles, and with a distinguished vertex \(\rho\in V\). Suppose there exists a ‘capacity’ function \(q:E\to[0,\infty)\) which satisfies the following three conditions:
  \[
  \text{(i) For every vertex }v\ne\rho,\qquad
  \sum_{u:(u,v)\in E}q(u,v)\le\sum_{w:(v,w)\in E}q(v,w).
  \]
  \[
  \text{(ii)}\qquad \sum_{u:(u,\rho)\in E}q(u,\rho)=0
  \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{w:(\rho,w)\in E}q(\rho,w)>0.
  \]
  (iii) There exists \(K<\infty\) such that, for every directed path \((u_0,u_1,\ldots)\) in \(H\), starting at \(u_0=\rho\), we have
  \[
  \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}q(u_i,u_{i+1})\le K.
  \]
  Then simple symmetric random walk on the undirected version of \(H\) starting at \(\rho\) is transient.”
- **Lemma 14, p. 15:** “For any \(\Xi\), the center-optimal Gale–Shapley algorithm yields a stable allocation.”
- **Lemma 15 (Optimality), p. 16:** “Fix a set of centers \(\Xi\), let \(\psi\) be any stable allocation, and let \(\psi^{\rm GS},\psi^{\rm GSc}\) be respectively the site-optimal and center-optimal Gale–Shapley allocations to the same \(\Xi\). For all \(r\in[0,\infty]\), we have the following:
  (i) for \(\mathcal L\)-a.e. site \(x\),
  \[
  g_x(\psi^{\rm GS},r)\ge g_x(\psi,r)\ge g_x(\psi^{\rm GSc},r);
  \]
  (ii) for every center \(\xi\),
  \[
  \gamma_\xi(\psi^{\rm GSc},r)\ge\gamma_\xi(\psi,r)\ge\gamma_\xi(\psi^{\rm GS},r).
  \]”

Here HHP defines \(g_x(\psi,r)=\mathbf1\{|x-\psi(x)|<r\}\) off \(\psi^{-1}(\Delta)\), and \(\gamma_\xi(\psi,r)=\mathcal L(\psi^{-1}(\xi)\cap B(\xi,r))\). Unclaimed distance is infinity. Thus \(g_x(\psi,\infty)\) records whether the site is claimed, and \(\gamma_\xi(\psi,\infty)\) is its centre's total territory volume.

These existence and extremality assertions apply to an arbitrary locally finite deterministic set. Theorem 1 is proved in Section 2, pp. 8–9. The centre algorithm and Lemma 14 occur on pp. 15–16; Lemma 15 is stated before the point-process setup and proved using the deterministic rejection lemmas 18–19 on pp. 19–20. The section title “Almost sure uniqueness” does not impose stationarity on its earlier deterministic lemmas. None of these results uses recurrence, density bounds, absence of descending chains, or random genericity. “Discrete” here must be read in the locally finite sense used in the paper's constructions, not merely “each point is isolated”: the paper itself excludes \(\{1,1/2,1/3,\ldots\}\) immediately after Theorem 1. The submitted hypotheses are sufficient.

Countability makes all centre bisectors a Lebesgue-null set; every site outside it has strict preferences among distinct centres. For each fixed allocation being compared, countably many rational radii suffice to obtain the distance inequalities on one common full-measure set. There is no need to intersect exceptional sets over the possibly uncountable family of all stable allocations.

The definition of stability agrees with HHP p. 2. An allocation is measurable, has territory volumes at most \(\alpha\), and takes the undefined value \(\Delta\) only on a null set. A site not assigned to a centre desires it if it is closer than its assignment, or the site is unclaimed. A centre covets a site if it is unsated or has even one assigned site farther away. Stability prohibits every such blocking pair, rather than merely almost every pair. Removing a common null set from territories and assigning its sites to \(\Delta\) preserves stability: this removes possible coveting witnesses and exempts those sites, without changing volumes. The proof only needs properties of the original stable allocations on the remaining sites. “Differing on a nonnull set” means exactly \(\mathcal L\{x:s(x)\ne c(x)\}>0\), disregarding undefined exceptional sets. The claim does not replace this with positive probability, non-equivariance, or a difference on finitely many sites.

To complete the last quantifier: fix any stable allocation \(\psi\). Once \(s=c\) a.e., the site sandwich at rational radii forces \(|x-s(x)|=|x-\psi(x)|\) for almost every site. If this distance is infinite, both are unclaimed. If finite, exclusion of bisectors forces the same centre. This proves that every stable allocation agrees a.e. with s. It does not assert a common pointwise exceptional set for every possible representative of every allocation. Finite and empty centre sets, disconnected graphs, unsated centres elsewhere, and unclaimed sites are all covered. Common finite positive appetite is used in the disjoint-volume packing argument; a uniform separation assumption on all centres is not used.

The hard comparison with HHP has a fairly definite answer. Theorem 2's statement alone does **not** imply (a) or the centre-count version of (b). Its hypothesis concerns recurrence of the graphs on **all** centres. Remark (vi), p. 7, supplies the sufficient bound
\[
\#\{\{\xi,\xi'\}\subseteq\Xi\cap B(0,r):|\xi-\xi'|<b\}
=O(r^2\log r),\qquad\text{for every finite }b.
\]
This counts nearby unordered pairs, not centres. Large clusters can make that edge count far larger than N(r). The submitted report correctly identifies this distinction.

For a second check, consider its example \(C_n=\{n+j/(10n):0\le j<n\}\). The centre count is asymptotic to \(r^2/2\). Every pair of successive clusters is completely joined in \(G_2\). Assign flow \(1/[n(n+1)]\) to each of their \(n(n+1)\) forward edges. Each vertex in layer n >= 2 receives \(1/n\) and sends \(1/n\); the first vertex sends one. The energy is exactly \(\sum_{n\ge1}1/[n(n+1)]=1\). Thus a quadratic centre count does not make that full graph recurrent. The proposed improvement is substantive as a sufficient hypothesis, even though its proof is short.

HHP's actual **proof** contains almost all the mechanism. I independently reconstructed the omitted geometric observation using their original definitions, without using the extremal graph. Normalize appetite to one as they do, or retain \(\alpha\) below. Write
\[
U_v=\psi_1^{-1}(v)\cup\psi_2^{-1}(v),\qquad
r_v=\sup\{r:\mathcal L(U_v\cap B(v,r))<\alpha\}.
\]
Their edges have capacities \(\mathcal L(N_u\cap M_v)\). Lemma 8 says \(N_v\cap M_v\) is null. For almost every site on a positive edge \(u\to v\), this gives
\[
r_v\le|x-v|<|x-u|<r_u.
\]
In particular every receiving radius is finite, and all descendants of a fixed first edge have radii bounded by one finite R. By continuity of ball-intersection volume, a finite \(r_v\) implies
\[
\mathcal L(U_v\cap B(v,r_v))=\alpha.
\]
Indeed, the volume starts at zero and cannot jump on a sphere; if it failed to reach alpha at any finite radius then the supremum defining \(r_v\) would be infinite. The sets \(U_v\) have multiplicity at most two, since each of the two allocations assigns a site to at most one centre. Therefore, for descendants in a bounded K,
\[
\alpha\,\#\{v\in K\text{ among the descendants}\}
\le2\mathcal L(K^{+R}).
\]
This is the claim's packing assertion with a harmless factor two. HHP Lemmas 10–12 and Proposition 13 already produce the bounded-edge, finite-energy disagreement flow. The packing bound supplies its uniform degree and spatial growth bounds, and the submitted cut argument finishes (a) and (b).

There is no concealed earlier use of growth to block this deduction. Although HHP Section 3 announces the recurrence assumption at its start, the constructions and proofs of Lemmas 8–12 and Proposition 13 do not use it. Recurrence of the whole-set graph is invoked only in the final contradiction proving Theorem 2 on p. 14. Proposition 13 alone is an abstract flow result and contains no packing theorem. Thus the added packing observation is an unstated step, obtainable directly from HHP's ingredients. I cannot establish why the authors did not state this consequence; attributing motives or an overlooked technical obstruction would be speculation. The proper value assessment is a useful short extension/corollary of their proof, not a new transience method and not a full solution of (i).

For numerical evidence I wrote [code/independent_grid.py](code/independent_grid.py) from scratch. It does not read, execute, or import any code in `claim_files/code`. It implements two separate deferred-acceptance algorithms: sites propose and centres retain their closest sites up to quota; centres propose to sites while vacancies remain and sites retain their closest proposer. Exhaustive blocking-pair and quota checks run after each solver. Eighty additional random shared-cost/quota instances test both excess-supply regimes. The separate [code/audit_saved_grid.py](code/audit_saved_grid.py) reloads the saved coordinates and allocations and checks every blocking pair again, without importing either solver.

The centre configurations are nested prefixes of infinite locally finite sets. At each integer radius n, place \(n^2\) centres in each of the \(2d\) clusters near \(\pm ne_i\), with jitter confined to a fixed small cube; include one central centre. Jitter is fixed by a separate deterministic seed for each layer. Thus \(N(r)=\Theta(r^3)\) in both d=1 and d=2, with increasingly dense remote clusters. The finite centre count is
\[
1+2d\sum_{j=1}^{n}j^2=1+\frac{d\,n(n+1)(2n+1)}3.
\]
For layer prefix n the site window is \([-n-1,n+1]^d\), sampled at cell midpoints. A cell has mass \(h^d\); appetite one is an integer quota \(h^{-d}\). Centre coordinates and both allocations are saved as plain CSV. Strictness of both preference lists is checked numerically, with positive minimum adjacent squared-distance gaps exceeding \(10^{-11}\). An initial rational central coordinate produced ties in d=2, which the check caught; the reported runs use irrational central coordinates and were rerun in full.

In the observation cube \([-1,1]^d\), window enlargement changed no assignments in d=1. In d=2, the first enlargement changed 1, 2, and 5 core cells at the three respective meshes; the second enlargement changed none. Comparing piecewise-constant cell allocations at the largest window across successive meshes gave core disagreement volumes 0.0625 then 0.015625 in d=1, and 1 then 0.71875 in d=2. These are differences between discretizations, while the two algorithms agree at every fixed discretization. Neither observation proves continuum convergence.

The reason this is weak evidence is structural: for finite strict preferences induced by a shared pair cost, a globally minimum available pair is forced in every stable matching. Otherwise that site and a centre with residual quota, or with a worse occupant, would block. Remove the forced site, decrease the centre's quota, and repeat. This proves uniqueness of the finite problem. Consequently an infinite nonuniqueness example could still have agreement in every finite terminal experiment. There is no numerical route here to an independent proof of (a) or (b), and no simulated counterexample.
