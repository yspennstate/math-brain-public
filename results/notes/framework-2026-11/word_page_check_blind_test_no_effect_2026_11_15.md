The word walk's `page` command lists a draft's habitual and repeated words, each with a few near words. The test
asked whether that list helps a writer revise.

Design. The 40 plain drafts from the word-palette test were each revised once by a Codex writer in two arms: R, a
plain revision, and P, a revision with the page check's list and the instruction to use any of it only where it makes
the sentence better. Sixteen fresh Opus 5.5 judge sessions read each pair in both orders; a pick that flips with the
order is a tie. The rule fixed before the run: P helps at 28 or more of 40 with sense not below -0.25; it hurts at R
28 or more, or sense below -0.25 at p < 0.05.

Result: R 13, P 12, ties 15; sense P minus R +0.03 (sign-flip p 0.91). No detectable effect. By form, story 5-2 for
R, documentary 5-1 for P, poem 4-2 for R, paper 3-3: noise at this size. Judges picked Text 1 in 51 of 80 readings, a
position bias the two-order design absorbs. A second judge family (Codex, same prompts) gave R 16, P 10, ties 14,
sense -0.08 (p 0.32), agreeing with Opus on 25 of 40 pairs (kappa 0.44); Codex also wrote the revisions, so it is not
independent of the writer.

Why it is inert: the check flagged 100 word occurrences across the 40 drafts; R left 93 of them in place and P left
85. P took an offered word 4 times, R twice by chance. Both arms changed about 9 percent of the words.

How to apply: use the page check as an editor's flag (it names words such as somewhere, nobody, inside, someone that
can often be cut). Do not show its near words as replacements; they are often off sense (rope offered roach, animal
offered female). With the palette test, the word walk has not yet helped a page in a blind read. What would change
this: a story-only test at a larger n, or a third judge family that did not write the pages.
